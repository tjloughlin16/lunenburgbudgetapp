# What is worth saying, and to whom

Every candidate item from a full runthrough of the 78 published conclusions, the model's
17 headline cards, `WHAT-DECIDERS-NEED.md`, and the 123-row gap register. Grouped by
subject, strongest first inside each group. **Nothing here is scheduled** — which week each
item lands in is the next conversation, and this one is about whether the item is worth a
reader's time at all.

---

**The content is below, as it would be written.** Each item opens with the card itself —
the line a reader sees, the figures behind it, and what it means for them. Who it is for
and where it came from are folded underneath each one.

How items were chosen, and who the readers are, is at the END of this file. That order is
deliberate and it is rule 7a: the method is what you want when you disagree with a
judgement, not before you have read one.

### 1.1 Why a school with fewer children is not a cheaper school

> ### Lunenburg teaches 256 fewer children than in 2008 — and the schools did not get cheaper. Exactly as many children with disabilities are still here, 258 then and 258 now, and losing a pupil takes about $150 of state aid with it rather than the $5,847 a pupil the aid works out to.

> The count did not move while the school around it shrank, so the SHARE went 14.1% to 16.3%. Children in low-income households roughly doubled as a share, 10.0% to 25.2% — the largest change of the three, and the one to read with care, because DESE altered how it identifies low income part-way through the series. English learners went 3 to 70, the smallest of the three in children: +67 against +212.  
> *— then/now*

> **And the price of the thing rose, in the state's own books.** DESE's foundation budget — what the state calculates an adequate education for *these* children costs — went from $7,880.78 a pupil in FY2008 to $13,770.40 in FY2026, +74.7%, on 62 fewer foundation pupils. Over the years that can be compared like for like, actual against actual, Lunenburg's net school spending went $8,950.89 a pupil in FY2008 to $14,887.59 in FY2024, +66.3%, against +62.3% in the state's own foundation rate over the same span. Four mechanisms sit underneath that and each is its own item here: the aid barely moves with enrolment (2.1); the out-of-district tuition the 258 attract is set by other districts and by each child's plan, not by Lunenburg (4.1, 6.6); insurance grows faster than the levy that pays for it and $1,521,536 of the schools' share is not even in the school budget (3.1, 3.6); and costs are lumpy — a grade losing five children does not remove a seventh of a teacher, a bus route or a building.

**If you are a resident:** Both things you have heard are true, and neither is somebody's fault. There are fewer children; the schools are not cheaper; and what connects them is that the expensive part of the work did not leave with the headcount, while the state's own price for a year of school rose 74.7% a pupil.

**If you are a Finance Committee member:** The chart you were shown at Tri-Board measured headcount. This is the column beside it, from the same publisher, and it moves the other way.

**If you are a School Committee member:** "Even flat staffing represents a reduction in effective capacity" is a claim about this table. The table is published for FY1992–FY2026 and the claim has never been put beside it.

**What we expect the reader to take away**

**Both are true.** There are 256 fewer children, and the schools are not cheaper — and the part a reader usually does not have is that neither of those is somebody's fault. The expensive work did not leave with the headcount: the aid barely moves with enrolment, the 258 children with the most expensive needs are all still here, insurance outgrows the levy, and a grade losing five children does not remove a bus route. Over the years that can be compared like for like, Lunenburg's spending for each pupil rose 66.3% while the state's own price for a year of school here rose 62.3% — so the town tracked the cost of the thing rather than outrunning it, which says nothing at all about whether the money bought the right things.

## Subject: state aid, and what a pupil is actually worth

### 2.1 Losing a student does not cost the town its share of the school budget

> ### One more or one fewer student moves Lunenburg's state aid by about **$150** — not the $5,847 a pupil the aid works out to.

> , and the two figures differ by a factor of 38.  
> *— assumed/mechanism*

**If you are a resident:** If you have heard that children leaving is draining our state aid: it is not the aid that leaves. Where a departure costs the town is the tuition that follows a child, which is a different number in a different place.

**If you are a School Committee member:** A revenue argument built on per-pupil aid is out by two orders of magnitude. The aid is last year's aid plus an increase, and in FY2026 the whole increase was the Legislature's flat floor.

**What we expect the reader to take away**

**Both halves of what you have heard are true, and they are about different money.** Children leaving does cost the town, and it is not the state aid that leaves: the aid moves by about $150 a pupil, because it is last year's aid plus a flat increase rather than a payment per child. What does not follow is that enrolment stops mattering — a school choice transfer still takes a tuition payment with it, a different number in a different place. It is also a marginal rate and does not extrapolate to a large loss of pupils.

### 2.3 The bill the state sends the town is set by property values, not by children

### 3.3 Outside money used to pay a dollar in five. It now pays about one in ten

> ### The town's own share of school spending went from 81.5% in FY2009 to 89.9% in FY2025 — 8.4 points, worth $2,520,227 a year at today's spending.

> *— then/now*

**If you are a resident:** This is why the town's bill can rise in a year when the schools spend less. In FY2025 the general fund rose $1,999,627 while spending across all funds fell $23,561.

**If you are a Finance Committee member:** The shift is an outcome of grant programmes starting and ending and of what the town appropriates; nothing here separates them, and the share did not move in a straight line.

**What we expect the reader to take away**

## The test every item had to pass

Three things, and an item failing any one of them is in list 2 or list 3.

1. **A contrast.** One of: *then/now · us/them · said/shown · document/document ·
   assumed/mechanism*. A fact with no contrast is inert — TJ's own test is that a fun fact
   from five years ago wastes a reader's time, and the same thing five years ago set against
   today does not.
2. **An impact somebody would recognise as theirs.** What it costs them, what their child
   gets, what choice is in front of them, or what they could do about it. Contrast is
   necessary and is not sufficient: *English learners went from 3 to 70* is a genuine
   then/now and is still a fact about a spreadsheet until the line after it says what
   follows.
3. **A derived figure behind it.** A conclusion id, a report, a registered gap, or a
   reconciled table in the database. Nothing typed.

**The impact lines are per-persona and all of them are shown to everybody.** A resident
reading the School Committee's line learns what their representatives are weighing on
their behalf, which is the job. So every line is written as though both audiences read it,
because both will. The roster of readers is in the next section, and none of it is invented
here.

**Two limits on the impact line, both easy to breach.** It may not smuggle in a cause
(rule 7): *fewer sections ran* is measured, *so your child had less choice* is not, because
enrolment fell too. And it may not become advice (rule 8): the Monty Tech line is the model
— *a child moving there does not add to the town's bill, it moves part of it* — which
changes how a reader reads their assessment and tells nobody how to vote.

**Counts.** 48 ready · 13 needing work first · 29 rejected. The rejected list is the one to
read if you want to know whether the filter is working.

## Who each item is for

Every item carries **FOR** (the one reader who most needs it and for whom it would change
something), **ALSO REACHES**, and one line arguing the choice. That last line is there
because the field is the one most likely to be filled in reflexively — everything looks

**The roster, all of it already in use here.** Four bodies from `WHAT-DECIDERS-NEED.md`:

And within "resident", four kinds, because they are not one person and only one of them is

- **a household with children in the schools**
- **a senior, or a household without children in the schools**
- **somebody deciding how to vote on an override**
- **somebody who has just been told a number at a meeting and wants to check it**

**The first two are the axis the argument in this town actually runs on**, and both are
spoken for out loud. *"This will shift the burden of the towns revenues needs away from the
fixed income seniors"* — Finance Committee, 27 June 2024. *"The Commission agreed to keep
the minimum low to help those on fixed incomes"* — Sewer Commission, 12 February 2026. The
Council on Aging has carried **Senior Tax Relief** as a warrant-article item to both an
annual and a special Town Meeting. On the other side, the claim is put as a share: *"this is
good for the 30% of people who have students in the school."*

**An item that lands differently on those two is among the strongest available, and it is
where rule 8 is hardest.** The job is to say what each option costs somebody, never which
somebody matters more. Write both impacts and let them sit beside each other. A card that
makes seniors the obstacle, or parents the special interest, has stopped being a budget
project.

**And do not publish either group's size.** We hold DESE's detail on the 1,568 children and
effectively nothing about the other ten thousand residents — no age distribution, no
household composition, no income by age, no tenure. The "30% of people" figure carries the
commonest error shape available: a count of HOUSEHOLDS with children read as a share of
PEOPLE. 1,568 students is about 13% of the town's people and the household share is a
different denominator entirely. Registered as a gap, `people` side. See **N0** below, which
is cheap and would close it.
