   1|                                                                       DEPARTMENT OF PUBLIC WORKS
   2|                     "The mission of the Department of Public Works is to protect, preserve, and improve the Town's infrastructure and related
   3|                     assets in a manner which meets and enhances the current and future social and economic needs of the community, to
   4|                     contribute to a healthy, safe and quality environment for the Town's citizens and visitors, and to provide a cost effective
   5|                    and economically sound means of disposing of solid waste and wastewater"
   6|                     The umbrella of Public works combines the resources of the Highway, Park, Cemetery, Sewer, and Facilities divisions, and
   7|                     Vehicle Maintenance services into one department organizing and scheduling personnel and equipment to best serve the
   8|                     residents of the community through the effective delivery of an extensive scope services.
   9|                     In January, our hopes of a much need renovation of the Public Works facility were realized. The existing building at 520
  10|                     Chase Rd. was totally renovated inclusive of new office space, efficient and safe heating/cooling and ventilation. The
  11|                     installation of more efficient lighting and numerous energy saving upgrades were rewarded with a $14,000.00 rebate from
  12|                     our electrical supplier. Additionally, a separate structure was erected which allows for our entire fleet to be under cover.
  13|                     This has, as expected, decreased response time during winter events. Over time, the protection of our vehicles from severe
  14|                    weather will prove invaluable, Modernized office space allows for a professional working environment for staff and
  15|                    residents requiring service.
  16|                     It is impossible to express the level of appreciation to you the residents who supported the project and especially those
  17|                    individuals who gave so much and worked so diligently toward the success of this project. Thank you.
  18|                    PAVEMENT MANAGEMENT
  19|                     Consistent with the prioritization as determined by our Pavement Management Plan, road improvements were initiated on a
  20|                     portion of Reservoir Rd., the completion of an overlay on New West Townsend Rd to the Townsend line, a section of
  21|                    Lakefront Ave. beyond the scope of Emerald Place, and Chase Rd. (Rt. 13) from the State taking to just south of the
  22|                     Northfield Rd. intersection. The scope of the project included: a leveling course of asphalt followed by a top or finish
  23|                     course, sections of "berm" or curbing, shoulder work, minor drainage improvements and driveway aprons.
  24|                     Expanding the scope of our pavement preservation methods we added "crack sealing" to the overall program. This
  25|                     process involves the installation of rubberized asphalt under high pressure to cracks or separations in the pavement. This
  26|                     application can add approximately 5 years or more to the existing surface. The Pavement Management Plan also identifies
  27|                     specific areas where this method is the best option.
  28|                     Funding continues to be the major detriment to improving the overall condition of our roadways. The primary source of
  29|                     funding is the Massachusetts Department of Transportation Chapter 90 program. The estimated value of our 90 miles of
  30|                    paved streets is, by industry standards, is calculated at 62 million dollars. The General Highway maintenance line within the
  31|                    operational budget remains stagnant. In order to initiate and sustain a road improvement program a substantial infusion of
  32|                    funds is necessary. A conservative estimate would be approximately 1.2 million dollars per year total or an additional
  33|                    $800,000.00 to existing allocations. Applying $400,000 a year to a backlog of work that exceeds 10 million dollars places
  34|                     this department and the Town as a whole in dismal failure. Many roads are compromised to the degree that only a major
  35|                    reconstruction can bring them to an acceptable quality. Patching, or filling pot holes by DPW staff is in most areas is no
  36|                     longer the appropriate application. Without a substantial infusion of funds our roads Town-wide will continue to
  37|                    deteriorate to a point where the safety of our travelling public will be compromised and the overall quality of is life
  38|                    negatively affected.
  39|                     Line Markings

  41|                     curb on all our major roadways. Further, where applicable, crosswalks, stop bars and designated parking areas were
  42|                     repainted. By means of a supplemental allocation the Town has maintained compliance with current MUTCD mandates.

  25|                                                                              SEWER COMMISSION
  26|                    The five member Sewer Commission was originally established at the Special Town Meeting on May 6, 2006. The Sewer
  27|                    Commission bears the responsibility of overseeing the Town's sewer system, planning for the future, and developing and
  28|                    implementing rules, regulations and policies. The Department of Public Works manages and maintains the operational side
  29|                    of the system. Under the Town's Charter, responsibility for the day-to day operation, care and maintenance of the sanitary
  30|                    sewers are under the supervision of the Town Manager. During 2012, Dave MacDonald and Butch Bilotta were re-elected
  31|                   for three year terms at the Town Election, in August our Chairman Bob Ebersole resigned, and Mike Nault was appointed in
  32|                   November to finish out his term until the May 2013 elections.
  33|                    There are currently 673 properties connected to the Lunenburg system and another 387 that have access to Town Sewer
  34|                    but are not yet connected. During 2012, we had 35 new accounts connect to Town Sewer.
  35|                    The Town of Lunenburg does not operate a sewer treatment plant. Approximately 80% of Lunenburg sewer is discharged to
  36|                   the City of Leominster, and the other 20% is discharged to the City of Fitchburg. As the rates charged by the City of
  37|                   Leominster and the City of Fitchburg remained stable during 2012, there were no rate increases to Lunenburg users since
  38|                    1/1/12. However, in order to balance the FY 13 Budget, it was necessary for the Commission to transfer $73,208 from
  39|                   retained earnings, a practice that is not sustainable. It is a goal of the Sewer Commission to present a balanced budget for
  40|                   the next fiscal year.
  41|                   The Commission continues to focus on two major issues that affect the operating costs of the sewer system; grease and
  42|                   infiltration and inflow (I/l). There has been excellent cooperation and progress made with several commercial
  43|                   establishments and the Lunenburg School system related to grease trap design and maintenance. While the Grease Trap
  44|                   Regulation, put into effect in 2011, focuses on commercial and public disposal, individual home owners can also contribute
  45|                   a significant amount of grease into the system which increases everyone's costs. We will continue to educate homeowners
  46|                   on how to protect the infrastructure and keep operating costs low by reducing their disposal of fats, oil and grease into the
  47|                system and avoiding blockages. Whether on sewer or septic, grease should not be put down the drain. The second major
  48|                                                                                            25

  22|                      School. We started the 2012-2013 school year with 506 students in 20 classrooms for an average class size of 23, and 41
  23|                     students in our two preschool classrooms. We constantly strive to find better ways to meet the collective as well as the
  24|                     individual needs of our students. Our Professional Development focused on Reading Comprehension, the Common Core
  25|                     curriculum, and the new teacher evaluation system. We worked with experts in the field of Literacy and established
  26|                     eighteen lab classrooms in the building that are piloting Readers Workshop and Inclusive Practices. All of our classroom
  27|                     teachers continued to use Responsive Classroom strategies as part of our Academic/Social/Emotional Learning
  28|                     Curriculum, which includes morning meetings, guided discovery, rules and logical consequences, and academic choice.
  29|                     The Primary School community continues to strive toward academic excellence. Common assessments are in place in the
  30|                     fall, winter, and spring for all children in grades K-3 in the areas of reading, writing, and math. These common
  31|                     assessments serve to monitor the effectiveness of teacher instruction and drive instruction. We are now able to track
  32|                     progress throughout the year in a faster, more efficient manner in order to improve instruction.
  33|                     The Primary School is a Title One school; we have continued our tiered instructional system to insure that all students
  34|                    have the services they need to be successful. Tiered instructional services are provided in the area of Language Arts and
  35|                     Math. The first tier is the largest, and is fitted for universal instruction in the classroom. Teachers are skilled in
  36|                     differentiating instruction to meet a large number of student needs in the classroom. The second tier is based on student
  37|                     needs that are targeted, individual, and involve more intensive interventions. The third tier is the smallest and involves
  38|                     special services with very intense interventions. The tiered instructional model requires that the teachers not only meet the
  39|                     needs of the students who may be struggling with learning but those students who are doing well and need to strive to
  40|                     achieve higher levels of learning. Learning is becoming more personalized and students are being challenged to be
  41|                    independent learners and problem solvers.
  42|                     Our staff works hard to maintain a school culture that is positive, inclusive, and supportive of each of our members. We
  43|                    have high expectations, insist on respectful behavior, and recognize the importance of building a community where
  44|                     members feel a sense of belonging and responsibility to contribute. Our students have a right to be in an environment that
  45|                    is safe, promotes learning, and enables them to achieve success.

  47|                   night serves as the vehicle for students to display/articulate their strengths as a learner. To prepare for Student Success
