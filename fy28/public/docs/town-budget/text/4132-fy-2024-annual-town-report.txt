In Memoriam 
James J. Larkin, affectionately known as Jim, left the world on March 31, 2024, leav-
ing a legacy of love and laughter. Born on June 24, 1940, in Newton, MA, Jim’s reli-
able good nature and funny wit made him a beacon of joy to all who met him. Jim 
was a devoted husband to his wife Sheila of 45 years, who survives him. He was a 
very positive role model and loving stepfather to Darrell Maki. He was preceded in 
death by his parents, Robert and Mary (Lynch) Larkin and his brother Robert Larkin 
Jr.  
 
Jim was a dedicated systems engineer in the technology sector at GenRad and Poly-
com before his retirement. He was a passionate member of the Maserati Club, the 
Mid State Antique Auto Club, and the Antique Auto Club of America. May God 
grant him a front row seat at the Antique Auto shows in Heaven.  
 
His interest in family genealogy and the history of Lunenburg led him to become a 
curator for the Lunenburg Historical Society, a role he cherished, especially Whalom 
Park memorabilia. He served in the US Navy from 1961-1966. Jim was also an avid 
metal detector, always looking for hidden treasures. Jim, his siblings, and family fre-
quently shared many stories of their bird watching sightings.

Lunenburg was settled in 1718 and incorporated in 1728. It occupies a land area of approximately 26.4 
square miles. Lunenburg is located in north central Massachusetts  approximately  43 miles from  Bos-
ton,  It is bordered on the west by the Town of Ashby and the City of Fitchburg, on the south by the 
Town of Lancaster and the City of Leominster, on the east by the Town of Shirley and on the north by 
the Town of Townsend. 
 
Lunenburg is a semi-rural community located in northern Worcester County.  Lake Whalom and the for-
mer Whalom Amusement Park delineated the Whalom District from the remainder of Lunenburg. This 
district was developed as a housing base for many factory workers working in the mills and factories of 
Leominster and Fitchburg. In many ways the characteristics of the Whalom District closely reflect charac-
teristics of these cities more than of the rest of Lunenburg. The Hickory Hills Lake area was developed as 
a private community comprised largely of vacation cottages. Shirley Reservoir is a public water body also 
developed with mainly vacation cottages that have now been modified to year-round residences.

REPORT OF THE TOWN MANAGER 
Fiscal Year 2024 
 
Note: The Fiscal Year 2024 Report of the Town Manager was not authored by Town Manager Heather Lemieux.  The content 
herein is based on a review and synthesis of periodic Town Manager Reports provided to the Select Board.  
Fiscal Year 2024 (FY24) was a year of meaningful progress for the Town of Lunenburg, marked by the advancement of long -
planned capital and infrastructure projects, significant strides in restoring the public ’s confidence in the property tax assessment 
process, important legislative wins for our senior residents, a coordinated response to multiple storm events, and continued work 
to address staffing needs across Town departments. Despite ongoing challenges, the dedicated staff, appointed and elected off i-
cials, and volunteers of the community continued to deliver a high level of service to Lunenburg residents.  Notably, Town Ma n-
ager Heather Lemieux tendered her resignation, after over seven years of service, to the Select Board on June 4, 2024.  
Below are the highlights of the key topics and accomplishments from Fiscal Year 2024 within the Office of the Town Manager.  
 
Town Manager Goals 
As in prior years, the Town Manager's goals for FY2024 centered on three primary areas: continuing to advance the redevelop-
ment/reuse of 925 Massachusetts Avenue, review of financial policies and address long -term staffing and unknown infrastructure 
needs to help inform the budget process, and employee recruitment, recognition, and retention. Progress was made across all t hree 
areas during the fiscal year. 
 
925 Massachusetts Avenue Redevelopment 
The redevelopment of 925 Massachusetts Avenue remained a top Town Manager goal throughout FY2024, with substantial pro-
gress made toward readying the site for disposal and future reuse. 
 
In the fall of 2023, the EPA-funded contractor completed the removal of the hydraulic lift and underground holding tank at the 
site, with hazardous materials submitted for proper off-site disposal. The Town's environmental consultant, Joe McLaughlin of the 
BETA Group, continued to coordinate with the EPA and MassDEP on follow -up environmental work. The EPA approved addi-
tional Brownfields funding to conduct soil borings, monitoring well installations, and groundwater sampling to address remaining 
data gaps beneath the building. 
 
In February 2024, the Town Manager met with MassDevelopment and their consultant from BSC Group, engaged through the 
Real Estate Technical Assistance program, to develop conceptual plans, perform a market analysis, and draft a Request for Pro-
posals for the disposal of the property. A site visit took place in March 2024, with work on a strict timeline to expend the technical 
assistance funds by June 30, 2024. The Historical Society also expressed interest in preserving the cupola atop the 925 Mass Ave 
building, and the Town Manager referred the request to the APDC for review and incorporation into the property's disposition 
plans. Conceptual plans yielded two options: maintaining the same footprint of the building if it were to be a renovation and  one 
that would be new construction.  
 
Infrastructure, Facilities, and Capital Projects 
 
Flat Hill Culvert Project 
The Flat Hill Road Culvert replacement was a major project in FY2024. Funded in part by a $500,000 Small Bridge Grant, the 
project went out to bid in early 2024 with the lowest responsive and responsible bid coming in at $830,230. The Town Manager 
successfully secured an additional $100,000 from MassDOT to cover higher -than-estimated costs, bringing total available funding 
to approximately $975,000. Construction was planned to begin in the fall of 2024 when lake water levels are lowered, with an 
estimated two-month road closure.  
 
Page Street Culvert Project 
The Page Street Culvert replacement, performed by Powell Stone & Gravel, was completed in September 2023 on time and under 
budget. The road was closed briefly to allow excavation and installation of the new pipe, and the guardrail was installed sho rtly 
thereafter.

LED Streetlight Conversion1 
 
The LED streetlight conversion project, awarded to Pine Ridge Technologies, was completed in December 2023. Approximately 
358 streetlights were converted throughout Town. The project was funded through the Green Communities Grant, and upon 
completion, the Town received a finalized Unitil incentive of $37,900, exceeding the originally projected $33,400. Total gran t-
eligible expenditures were approximately $139,000. The Town also finalized an Outdoor Light Billing Agreement with Unitil 
establishing the billing structure for the new fixtures going forward. 
 
Senior Center ADA Patio 
 
The Senior Center ADA Patio project, long in capital planning, was completed and celebrated with a ribbon cutting ceremony on  
October 11, 2023. The project was the result of collaborative effort over many years and represents a significant improvement  in 
accessibility and outdoor programming space at the Lunenburg Adult Activity Center.  
 
TC Passios Building 
 
Persistent roof leaks at the TC Passios building required ongoing attention throughout FY2024. The Facilities Department 
patched multiple leak areas and sought quotes for a phased roof repair, with a warrant article submitted to the Special Town 
Meeting. In April 2024, a significant leak damaged the Public Access studio. The Town Manager and Facilities Director decided 
to relocate the studio to the dry rear wing of the building, which occurred after the Annual Town Meeting.  
 
Public Safety Building 
In December 2023, a severe rain and wind event tore off a 30-foot section of metal siding at the Public 
Safety Building, exposing the fire apparatus bays. The Facilities Director responded immediately, securing 
the building that same day. Insurance covered the siding repair, which was completed in the spring of 
2024. The Public Safety Carpet replacement project was also bid in late 2023, with bids due in December.  
The bid was awarded to Atkinson Carpet and Flooring in the amount of $43,790. 
 
Welcome to Lunenburg Sign  
 
A “Welcome to Lunenburg” sign that was provided to the town by the North Central Chamber of Commerce and was installed 
on Lancaster Avenue, near the Stillman Farm.  
 
Lunenburg Adult Activity Center Generator 
 
The LAAC Generator project, included in the FY23 Capital Plan, was bid in the fall of 2023. Four bids were received and the 
project was awarded to Morse Brothers of Westminster for $99,940. With a generator lead time of 16 -18 weeks, installation was 
completed in the spring of 2024. 
 
Marshall Park 
 
The Marshall Park revitalization project advanced significantly in FY2024. A Request for Qualifications for an Owner's Projec t 
Manager was issued in early 2024. After the Park Commission evaluated respondents and conducted interviews, the Town final-
ized a contract with the selected OPM for a not-to-exceed fee of $250,000, covering services through designer/engineer selection  
and completion of PARC Grant-funded construction by June 2025. The Parks Commission also engaged a firm for the prelimi-
nary permitting required for the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers permit related to the $1,000,000 Land and Water Conservation 
Fund grant. The Town also finalized the Right of Entry Agreement with the Lunenburg Skate Park organization, with construc-
tion planned to begin in 2024. 
 
Roadway Maintenance and Infrastructure 
 
Roadway maintenance was active throughout FY2024. Sealcoating was completed in the fall of 2023 on twelve roads. PJ Keat-
ing paved Elmwood Road and West Groton Road in the spring of 2024. Premier Fence completed guardrail repairs on North-
field Road, Leominster Shirley Road, and Graham Street. The DPW crew continued routine maintenance including pothole re-
pairs, drainage clearing, roadside vegetation cutting, and sign repairs throughout the year. The Town also received a $21,979  
Rural Roads apportionment from a MassDOT supplemental transportation bond distribution in April 2024.  
 
Route 2A Road Safety Audit 
 
A Road Safety Audit was conducted in September 2023 by VHB and MassDOT, with the Town Manager, Police Chief, Fire 
Chief, and DPW Director participating. MassDOT repainted lane markings on Route 2A in October 2023, and the final RSA 
report was submitted in November 2023. MassDOT subsequently developed a work order to address additional low -cost safety 
improvements at the two intersections.

Financial Management 
 
Tax Assessment and Billing 
 
FY2024 continued to involve significant complexity in tax assessment and billing. With the Town's property revaluation not 
completed until February 2024, the Bureau of Local Assessment approved a valuation delay, and the Town issued third quarter 
preliminary tax bills using a Pro Forma Recap approved by DOR in December 2023. The Treasurer/Collector and Interim Princi-
pal Assessor, Rena Swezey, worked extensively to test billing processes in Munis before generating final bills, and a detaile d tax 
bill insert was prepared to explain the unusual billing cycle to taxpayers.  
After the FY24 fourth quarter actual tax bills were mailed, the Assessors Department discovered a software error in Munis tha t 
caused an incorrect total taxable value to be printed on bills for property owners with exemptions. The payable amount on eve ry 
bill was correct. The Town proactively sent correction letters to all affected taxpayers with copies of the accurate Vision P roperty 
Cards, and a ticket was submitted to Tyler Technologies to resolve the issue.  
FY25 preliminary tax billing also required outside contractor assistance due to persistent software challenges with Tyler Tec h-
nologies. These bills incorporated the estimated additional tax levy from the override approved by voters in May 2024.  
The Town's New Growth for FY24 came in at approximately $630,000, exceeding the original estimate by $230,000 and provid-

CONSERV ATION  
The Town of Lunenburg Conservation Commission is a seven-member commission, each appointed 
to 3-year terms by the Select Board, whose duties are codified under the Conservation Commission 
Act MGL chapter 40 s 8C. The Commission enforces the Massachusetts Wetland Protection Act 
MGL chapter 131 s 40 and Chapter 239 of the local bylaws for the protection of wetlands. The Com-
mission also manages, and controls town owned lands retained for conservation and passive recrea-
tion use. 
 
The Fiscal Year 2024-25 was a very busy and active agenda containing both public hearings and 
meetings for a wide range of projects from commercial development, subdivisions and small single 
family upkeep projects and larger scale industrial/commercial proposals. The Commission also held 
numerous inspections on weekends prior to meetings to familiarize themselves with the upcoming 
proposals that would appear on the agenda. The Conservation Administrator no longer attends site 
walks with the Commissioners which has affected communications. During this period the Conserva-
tion Commission supported (numbers may be inaccurate due to lack of unified tracking system) 25 
NOIs, 42 Administrative Determinations, 15 RDAs, 69 COCs, and numerous other requests.  
 
The Conservation Commission continues to implement forest stewardship plans developed using 
grants as outlined in the last annual reports. This management effort recently completed at the Rayno 
property on Northfield Road resulting in $28,248 deposited into the Timber -Rights fund. The funding 
from the sale of harvested timber provides the seed money to make it possible for the Commission to 
apply for grants to maintain and improve Lunenburg’s Conservation Lands. The Conservation Com-
mission continues to execute on the awarded MASS Recreational Trail Program Grant that was 
awarded in 2023. The purpose of this grant was to fund the engineering design and permitting work 
to create parking access in Table Rock and Rayno Conservation Areas. The grant scope also covered 
improving Accessible Access to the Cowdrey Conservation Area as accessed from Townsend Harbor 
Road. In early 2025, the permits were issued for Table Rock and Rayno Areas. This effort is ongoing 
to complete the engineering design and permitting work in the Cowdrey.  
 
The Pollinator Habitat portion of the Hollis Rd property provides for a diversity of native flowers 
throughout the growing season. The Habitat promotes insect egg laying and nesting sites for pollina-
tors. The Habitat provides food sources for birds and numerous insects including native bees and but-
terflies. This unique “Hidden Gem” works as an ambassador for native pollinators to our community. 
We are very thankful for the dedicated volunteers supporting the Lunenburg Community Pollinator 
Habitat.

DEPARTMENT OF PUBLIC WORKS 
 
The Department of Public Works is made up of the Highway, Cemetery, Sewer, and Vehicle Maintenance Divisions.  
The Department maintains roads, bridges, sewer infrastructure, and provides services essential to the quality of life and 
welfare of the citizens of Lunenburg.  The staff consists of one Director, one Executive Assistant (shared with the Facili-
ties Department), one Highway Superintendent, 3 Heavy Equipment Operators, 2 Driver/Laborers, one Mechanic, one 
Cemetery Superintendent, two Seasonal Cemetery Laborers, one Sewer Business Manager, and one Assistant to the 
Sewer Business Manager.  
 
Departmental Mission: 
The mission of the Department of Public Works is to provide high quality, cost effective, efficient, and safe services to 
protect, preserve and improve the Town’s roadway infrastructure and related assets in a manner which meets and en-
hances the current and future social and economic needs of the community; to contribute to a healthy, safe and quality 
environment for the Town’s citizens and visitors; and to provide environmentally sound means of solid waste and 
wastewater disposal.    
 
Departmental Goals and Objectives 
Be prepared to respond to any weather, roadway infrastructure, or accident-related emergency that may arise on a 24 
hour per day, three hundred and sixty-five day per year basis. 
Maintain and enhance a highly qualified and dedicated staff of public works professionals. 
Provide preventative maintenance in a proactive manner to reduce reactive maintenance as follows: 
Maintain and update the sewer system and associated pumping stations. 
Maintain and repair roadways per the 2022 pavement management plan. 
Maintain and repair sidewalks per the 2020 PROW ADA Transition plan. 
Increase the number of roadsides mowed by developing a roadside maintenance plan. 
Increase the number of catch basin and stormwater structures cleaned and the number of miles of roadways 
swept per the June 2020 Operations and Maintenance Plan. 
Establish a comprehensive evaluation of bridges and culverts, and prioritize by condition, use, and plans for re-
habilitation. 
Increase training offered by our insurance provider to obtain credits to lessen premiums.   
Find new sources (Federal, State, and other grants) of funding to supplement the operational budget. 
Continue to maintain a high level of courteous communication and productive interaction between members of boards 
and commissions as well as Town residents. 
Strive to modernize the operations of the Department by obtaining and using the most up to date equipment and meth-
ods, including asset management.

Working to stay in compliance with our EPA NPDES MS4 Permit continues to still be an important function of the

Tree Removal   
A Tree Risk Management Inventory of almost 1,200 trees on the roads in Lunenburg was completed in 2015.  The inven-
tory prioritizes the removal and/or pruning of the trees.  However, storm damage and natural attrition creates a backlog 
of tree work that exceeds the Department’s allotted budget for tree removal.  The Department strives to fulfill each resi-
dent’s request for tree-work but must prioritize work based upon the determined degree of danger to person and property 
and location.  The DPW continued to act in a reactionary manner as needs arose.  All tree-work other than brush remov-
al, chipping, and the removal of small diameter trees will continue to be outsourced due to the lack of a bucket truck, 
crane, and a licensed tree professional.  Each spring and after storms, the DPW staff remove and dispose of woody de-
bris within the Town’s right-of-way.  An aggressive program by Unitil in recent years has lessened the burden of the 
Town by removing those trees that may cause an interruption of electrical service to the community.  The DPW appreci-
ates and thanks Unitil for their responsiveness in removing hazardous trees when asked to do so. 
 
Roadside Vegetation Maintenance 
The DPW staff responded to many requests to cut and clear overgrown vegetation from roadsides.  This work was most-
ly done using our new utility tractor with a boom rotary mower attachment (22-foot horizontal reach and 19.5-foot verti-
cal reach).  Some of this work continues to me completed using lawn mowers, saws, and other hand tools.   The DPW is 
developing a town-wide program of selective curbside mowing and vegetation clearing using the new tractor and boom 
mower that will significantly enhance our roadside vegetation maintenance program.   
 
Landfill 
The closed and capped landfill off Youngs Road is inspected by a consultant semiannually and is still in compliance with 
the requirements of the permit issued by the Massachusetts Division of Environmental Protection (DEP).  Gas monitor-
ing and groundwater contamination sampling and testing is conducted by a consultant.  The landfill site including the 
perimeter fencing is inspected on a regular schedule by DPW personnel.  The DPW hires a local contractor to mow the 
cap per DEP Requirements. 
 
Adjacent to the closed landfill is a storage area for catch-basin materials and street sweepings both determined to be haz-
ardous waste by the EPA.  The DEP has issued a Beneficial Use Determination (BUD) permit which allows the storage 
of these materials.  A consultant performs the yearly testing of these materials.   
 
On seven Saturdays in the fall and the spring, DPW personnel oversee and manage Yard Waste Days.  Residents may

Harvey & Sons, Inc.  Recyclable materials were also collected curbside weekly by Harvey.  Pay as You Throw bags may 
be bought at Hannaford Supermarket, Smokestack Roasters, Maki Building Center, Honey Farms, Jaxx Country Variety, 
Lunenburg Gulf, and Baker’s Whalom Variety. The DPW remains the point of contact between residents and our trash

Residents may dispose of household hazardous waste for a fee at the Devens Regional Household Hazardous Waste Col-
lection Center on published dates and times.  All Lunenburg residents have access to more fee-based recycling services 
at the CHARM center found at the Townsend Recycling Center at 33 Greenville Road, Townsend, MA.   
 
Lake Shirley Dam 
In the early 1990’s, with the structure in need of repair, The Lake Shirley Association turned over ownership of the dam 
to the Town.  After the completion of the total reconstruction of the dam, the responsibility for the maintenance, moni-
toring, and record keeping were completed by a small number of volunteers from the Lake Association.  Compliance 
with State dam regulations, the establishment of an operations manual as well as an emergency action plan was placed 
under the umbrella of the Department of Public Works in 2004-2005.   The dam is inspected by a professional engineer 
licensed in Massachusetts.  The most recent inspection resulted in a positive report, and the dam is still in compliance 
with regulations. 
 
Winter Operations 
The DPW was prepared for and responded to all snow and ice weather events with the goal of providing a high level of 
service in a timely manner while at the same time trying to minimize costs.  Lunenburg joins with 17 other towns in a 
cooperative bid to obtain the best possible pricing for rock salt.  However, the price of rock salt continues to steadily in-
crease every year.  The DPW maintains a small fleet of snow removal equipment (trucks, plows, spreaders, loader, back-
hoe, and sidewalk plow).  The fleet is supplemented with vendors as needed.  Due to breakdowns of the Town’s aged 
fleet of large dump trucks, more vendors were needed; thus, adding to costs.  Four new snow fighter trucks (large trucks 
with a salt spreader, front plow, and wing plow) have been ordered and are expected to be available in Fiscal Years 2025 
and 2026. 
 
The clearing of snow and ice from roadways is a critical operation of the DPW as it is a matter of public safety.   
The Town and its residents are fortunate to have a dedicated group of DPW personnel and private vendors who tirelessly 
and successfully challenge the worst Nature has to offer regardless of the severity or duration of a storm.  “DPW work-
ers are the original Storm Troopers.”

FINANCE COMMITTEE REPORT  
 
The Lunenburg Finance Committee is pleased to report that Lunenburg’s financial position is solid. In planning for Fis-
cal Year 2024, the Town has continued the sound, conservative management practices from prior years and has produced 
a budget that is responsible and maintains appropriate funding for the needs of all departments and Town residents.  
The process of developing and presenting a budget to Annual Town Meeting begins in the early fall with the Town Man-
ager meeting with department heads to discuss expenses, wants, and needs. The Town Manager then presents the pro-
posed budget to the Finance Committee in mid-February. Over the following 90 days, the Finance Committee meets 
with the various department heads to discuss their individual line item budget requests. The Finance Committee then 
votes on the recommendation of the finalized omnibus budget provided by the Town Manager and reports to Annual 
Town Meeting. As the Legislative body acting on the Town’s behalf, the Finance Committee recommends the approval 
of the budget as presented at Annual Town Meeting. 
Historically, the Capital Plan has been funded though free cash, taxes revenues, and unspent capital appropriation from 
prior years. For FY24, the available free cash is substantially lower due to the need to fill the overlay account with 
enough funds to offset a worst-case scenario with resident tax abatements. Without the use of ARPA funds, the proposed 
Capital Plan would fund $1,039,010, a 52% decrease from FY23. With the inclusion of the ARPA funds, the proposed 
Capital Plan funds $2,025,010, a 6.4% decrease from FY23. This conservative approach allows the Town to fund critical 
items in the Capital Plan while reserving enough money to cover potential abatements. 
Understandably, an important issue on the minds of many residents revolves around the current assessments and subse-
quent tax levy. While the personal positions, understanding, and potential outcomes of the taxpayers may widely vary, 
there are a fair number of certainties.  
1. The increases felt by some taxpayers do not indicate a windfall of cash for the Town. Lunenburg is restricted by 
Proposition 2½ (Mass. Gen. L. c. 59, § 21C) and, as such, is unable to raise the overall levy of the Town by any 
more that 2½% of the previous year’s levy limit plus any New Growth and Excludible Debt. The table above out-
lines the changes in taxes, showing the modest increase from year to year. 
2. Many Town residents have filed abatements, disputing the assessments made of their homes. Should all abatements 
be granted, the Town would be short of revenue by approximately $1,500,000. To best cover this potential outcome, 
the Town Manager has recommended the transfer of $1,500,000 from Free Cash to the Overlay Account.   
3. The abatement process and Overlay Account are the proper methods of both addressing the issue and reversing the 
financial burden places on some of the Town residents.

Noteworthy topics: 
• The end of FY23 will see a 14.56% reduction of our Debt Service and Excludible Debt, from $3,174,257.53 (FY23)

Board, and all the department heads that gave their time and effort to produce the budget presented at Annual Town

What is the Senior Property Tax Work-Off? 
 
The Senior Property Tax Work-Off Program offers income eligible senior citizens age 60+ an opportunity to have 
an abatement applied to their property tax bill. Participants work in various town departments doing jobs such as 
general office work, custodial, library shelving, meals on wheels, school assistance, etc., to earn the tax abatement. 
The 2024 tax abatement maximum is $1,500 and applied to the participant’s 3rd quarter real estate tax bill.  
 
The current hourly rate is $15.00 per hour. 
 
Eligibility Requirements 
• Applicants must be 60 years of age or older 
• Must occupy and be the owner of the property as of July 1st 
• Receive a property tax bill in their name or their spouse’s name (60 years+) 
• Meet the income guidelines of (proof of income is required): 
     one person household - $50,000  
     two person household - $75,000 
• Earnings are exempt from state taxes 
• Earnings are subject to OBRA if participants are not receiving a pension from either Worcester Regional Re-
tirement or Massachusetts State Teachers Retirement 
• Final amount earned will be applied to the 3rd quarter real estate tax  
• All new applicants are required to have a CORI background check 
• All Property Tax Workers must re- apply and be accepted annually for participation in the program

VETERANS’  SERVICES 
 
The Commonwealth of Massachusetts has the most comprehensive system of assistance offered to Veterans, 
their families and surviving spouses in all of the United States. Every city or town in Massachusetts, in accord-
ance with chapter 115 of the Massachusetts General Laws, must have available to its citizens a Veterans’ Ser-
vices Officer (VSO), either full or part-time, as determined by the community population, in order to serve the 
needs of its veterans. If they want to File VA Claims they must be State Certified thru the VA and are called 
Veteran Agents. Lunenburg has a part-time Veteran Agent at the Ritter Building at 960 Massachusetts Ave. 
The Office hours are Thursdays from 9:00 AM thru 12:00 PM.  
Veterans, families, or widows who qualify can be provided monthly financial assistance (Chapter 115 Bene-
fits), which are reimbursements for specific living expenses such as heating fuel, medical care, and pharmacy 
co-payments. With the Veteran Agent’s approval, the Town of Lunenburg provides the financial assistance, 
but then the town is reimbursed by the State’s Department of Veterans’ Services (DVS). It is the function of 
the Veterans’ Services Office to identify qualifying members of the community and assist with enrollment into 
the DVS program. Additionally, certain Veterans and/or widows may also be eligible for federal benefits from 
the Veterans Administration (VA). Only Massachusetts offers both of these services to every community.  
During Fiscal Year 2024 (July 1, 2023- June 30, 2024), Lunenburg is serving an average of 7 recipients 
(Veterans, Families, or Widows) per month on Chapter 115. Lunenburg paid out $44,454.57 in benefits, or an 
average of $3,704.55 per month. Lunenburg was reimbursed $37,233.82 by DVS, which is typically 75% of 
what was given as assistance, the maximum allowable share. There are a couple of services that the Town is 
reimbursed 100%.  
 
Some examples of Veterans’ Service Office assistance services include:  
• Enrollment in chapter 115, State Veterans benefits  
• Assistance obtaining military service records or replacement medals/decorations  
• Tuition waivers at Massachusetts state colleges  
• Housing assistance, including Veterans Shelters and Veterans Homes  
• Payments of “Welcome Home” bonuses for deployed Veterans  
• Assistance obtaining certain Massachusetts automobile and motorcycle license plates  
• Employment assistance

PLANNING BOARD 
 
The Planning Board consists of 5 members elected for terms of 5 years each. It makes careful studies of the resources, 
possibilities, and needs of the Town and plans for, and reviews, a variety of proposed short and long range development. 
 
Included in this is making and maintaining a comprehensive, land use related Master Plan, setting forth in graphic and 
textual form policies to govern the future growth and development of the entire Town, and that effort continued in FY24. 
Related to that, the Board also oversees the statutory power to regulate the subdivision of land and the creation of public 
ways within the Town by the adoption and administration of rules and regulations governing such development. More 
broadly, the Board makes recommendations to Town Meeting on all matters affecting land use and growth, including the 
Zoning By-Laws of the Town. Under its statutory and local permitting authority, it reviewed a variety of applications for 
development and initiatives for regulatory amendments over 23 meetings in FY24, as outlined below. All applications to 
the board are supported by a fee structure and use of peer review services paid for by the applicants, to offset administra-
tive costs to the Town. 
 
SUBDIVISIONS  
In FY24, under its Subdivision Rules and Regulations, the board held one public hearing to amend the application dead-
lines. Submitted applications included Subdivision Approval Not Required (ANR) plans, which are allowed by the Sub-
division Control Act and are an important part of the board’s regulatory authority over divisions of land. Eleven (11) 
ANR Plans not requiring approval under the Lunenburg Subdivision Rules and Regulations, but still requiring Board 
endorsement, were approved in FY24. One (1) Definitive Plan application was also reviewed and approved. Under relat-
ed Zoning Bylaws Regulations, 1 Common Driveways application was approved.  
 
STORMWATER MANAGEMENT  
Lunenburg’s Stormwater Bylaw requires Board review and approval for any development/redevelopment project that 
disturbs 1 acre or more of land. This Bylaw safeguards public health, safety, welfare and the environment by minimizing 
adverse impacts of contaminated stormwater both off-site and downstream. These requirements are more likely to affect 
large commercial projects and multi-family dwellings, rather than average homeowner wanting to put in a shed, garden, 
pool, etc. In FY24, the Board approved 5 stormwater permits.  
 
SITE PLAN REVIEW  
An application for construction of a 367,000 square foot warehouse and distribution building at 260 Falulah Road was 
approved in FY23, and the site was being prepared for construction in FY24. The majority of the building will be located 
on Airport Road/Crawford Street in Fitchburg, but a portion will extend into Lunenburg. In FY24, construction of a 
372,000 square foot warehouse and distribution building approved in FY23 off Leominster-Shirley Road near the Shirley 
town line was completed. As a result of this approval, improvements that were made to Leominster-Shirley Road with 
the addition of an eastbound left-turn lane into the project and the widening of the road to the north (towards Reservoir 
Road) on the project side of the street continued to improve safety, access and circulation. The Board also received and 
approved 2 Site Plan Review applications in FY24, and several modifications to previously approved projects were con-
sidered and acted upon.  
 
SCENIC ROADS

I am grateful for the seven years I have been able to lead and serve the students, staff and families of the Lunenburg  
Public Schools.  I am very fortunate to have worked in partnership with the Lunenburg School Committee which has

Town Manager, Select Board and Finance Committee as we have worked to meet the educational needs of the children 
of Lunenburg.  
  
The district has remained focused on execution of the Strategy for Continuous Improvement, which is aligned with our 
Mission and Vision:  The Mission of the Lunenburg Public Schools is to provide all students with the skills, confidence, 
and passion for life-long learning so that they may find their own paths to successful careers, active citizenship, and re-
warding lives.  
  
The Lunenburg Public Schools is committed to becoming a leader in education by focusing on its values of attending to 
the personal well-being of its students, providing high-quality curricula and student-centered instruction for deeper learn-
ing at all levels, and promoting the development of attributes and skills necessary for continuous learning for all.  
  
The Lunenburg School Committee approved the Strategy for Continuous Improvement in November 2019 and since 
then we have worked with intent to achieve our vision by putting the necessary infrastructure in place to support the well
-being and academic achievement of all students.    
  
Over the last six years, we adhered to our Strategy action plans, even through the pandemic.  At one time the Strategy 
had four overarching objectives and eleven action plans.  We worked in a focused way each year until the Strategy was 
reduced to one overarching objective and three action plans last year.  During the 2024-2025 school year, we have con-
tinued working on those plans.  One of the action plans focused on updating our core values to reflect the culture and 
commitment of our district.  In the fall of 2024, guided by Dr. Kalise Wornum, the Leadership Team began this initial 
work.    
 
Guided by the Strategy for Continuous Improvement since November 2019 and aided by federal and state grant funding, 
over the last six years the district was able to:  
• Add a curriculum leadership and instructional coaching  
• Increase district behavioral health staffing  
• Adopt a standards-aligned, rigorous on-grade-level curriculum in ELA/Literacy K-8, math K-8  
• Adopt a districtwide Tier 1 SEL curriculum  
• Implement district benchmark assessments in literacy and math  
• Use assessment data in order to determine need for intervention  
• Create an intervention model for academics and behavioral health  
• Create a referral process for intervention  
• Increase common planning time  
• Maintain class sizes under twenty-five  
• Create and execute a communication plan using consistent platforms districtwide  
  
As a result, we have seen improvement in student growth and achievement. We must stay the course in order to see more 
progress and improvement.  
  
The district has continued working to build a more inclusive culture in our schools.  In the fall of 2023, the district estab-
lished a Culture & Climate Advisory Council comprised of parents/guardians, staff and high school student representa-
tives.  This Council will help the district to foster increased family engagement in the education of their student.  In June 
2024, the Council presented the School Committee with a Family-School Compact, an overarching document that articu-
lates the roles of the student, parent/guardian, and educator in the education of each student.  During the 2024-2025

Compact into a school specific document which outlines what the Compact looks, feels and sounds like in each

adoption, as well as conduct the biennial review of the district’s Bullying Prevention and Intervention Plan.

TEACHING & LEARNING MODELS AND PD UPDATE  
Town Report 2024  
 
Teaching and learning is at the center of all of our work. This past year, we worked diligently to provide high-quality 
curricular materials and instruction to students, provide strategic interventions and challenges to our learners, and to pro-
vide professional development opportunities for our educators to support their efforts.  
Providing educators with high quality, evidence based, and inclusive curricular materials is a priority for the state of 
Massachusetts’ Department of Elementary and Secondary Education (DESE) and for our district.  Access to these types 
of curricular materials can  “prompt improvement in student outcomes” as evidenced by research.  To this end in 2023-
2024, educators:  
 implemented the second year of our new English language arts and math curriculums, HMH Into Reading and Into 
Math, at grades K-5.   HMH online professional development was provided on both the platform and content. Pro-
fessional Learning Community (PLC) meetings occurred to review assessment data, make adjustments to instruc-
tional practice based on the data, improve instruction through coaching and modeling, and module planning based on 
priority standards.  
 began implementation of Amplify, our new middle school language arts curriculum. Educators taught all of the mod-
ules at least once and  made adjustments and refinements to practice.  The specific focus in the first year was looking 
at each of the program components and determining which were best implemented through the online platform, and 
which were better to do on paper or through the use of Amplify’s supplemental materials that supported the curricu-
lum. Additional work was done on pacing to ensure that the critical units were taught and mastered.  
 completed a process for selecting a new high-quality math program for our middle school as well as developed an 
implementation process. Middle school math teachers created the criteria by which they would select a new math 
program. Once that document was finalized, a curriculum review of all high-quality middle school math programs 
was completed in PLC meetings. Three programs were selected for a pilot of one month each, using all components 
of the program’s units. Grade levels documented their feedback and a group meeting was held to discuss the merits 
of each program. It was unanimously decided to choose the EdGems program based on its quality, and combination 
of differentiated online and paper content that was easily accessible to teachers and students.  
 
MTSS  
A multi-tiered system of support (MTSS) was implemented at Lunenburg Primary and Turkey Hill Elementary School. 
The key components of MTSS include universal screening and progress monitoring tools: a process to analyze data, plan 
for interventions, and monitor student progress; a tiered system of support where every student receives core instruction 
and some students receive strategic or intensive instruction; and a collaborative team-based approach. In short , MTSS is 
a way of doing things within the schools that impacts every student. At each school, teams of educators came together to 
plan for and check student growth.  They worked with our literacy and math specialists to analyze multiple data points so 
that strategic and intensive interventions for support and/or enrichment were provided.  Student progress was monitored 
and adjustments were made as necessary.  
Lunenburg Public Schools utilizes multiple assessments to evaluate student achievement and growth. Students in grades 
K-8 completed a fall, winter and spring iReady assessment in both reading and mathematics and the personalized learn-
ing platforms through iReady provided differentiated instruction, systematic practice, and scaffolded feedback in both 
reading and mathematics. The  Dynamic Indicators of Basic Literacy Skills (DIBELS)  is used in grades K-3 to deter-
mine how students are performing on the critical skills necessary for reading: phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, 
vocabulary, and comprehension. In mathematics grades K-5,  the Forefront Universal Screener for Number Sense was 
used to determine students' early numeracy competency.   
Structured common planning time for teachers in grade level continued for grades K-8.  Our district literacy coaches met 
with teachers during common planning time to support curriculum development and help foster student growth.  High 
school department heads structured department meeting time to facilitate the necessary curriculum work needed to move 
each department forward and improve learning for students.

on educators’ planning and instruction, and plan to have Dr. Wornum return next year to focus more specifically on Cul-
turally Responsive Practices: Teacher Moves.

LUNENBURG PRIMARY SCHOOL 
 
Lunenburg Primary School is a student -focused early childhood learning school dedicated to serving children from 
preschool through grade 2. With students ranging in age from three to eight years old, we are committed to fostering a 
nurturing and inclusive environment where each child’s academic, social, and emotional growth is supported.  
Our school community consists of students, families, teachers, instructional staff, and support personnel who 
collaborate to ensure that every child’s individual needs are met. By focusing on the whole development of each 
student, we strive to cultivate not only strong academic skills but also essential social and emotional competencies that 
will serve them throughout their educational journey and beyond. At Lunenburg Primary School, we continue to create 
a welcoming, enriching atmosphere where all children feel valued and encouraged to reach their full potential. We 
look forward to another year of growth, learning, and community together.   
As of October 1, 2024, Lunenburg Primary School reported a total student enrollment of 382 students to the 
Department of Elementary and Secondary Education. This includes 49 preschool students who attend various half-day 
sessions, across two classrooms. The school also serves Kindergarten through second grade, with the following 
enrollment breakdown:  
• Kindergarten: 110 students distributed across five classrooms, with class sizes ranging from 21 to 23 students per 
class.  
• Grade 1: 109 students, also across five classrooms, with class sizes ranging from 18 to 22 students.  
• Grade 2: 114 students, distributed across five classrooms, with class sizes ranging from 22 to 25 students.  
This structure ensures that each grade level is supported by dedicated classroom spaces, allowing for an optimal 
learning environment for our students. We are proud to provide a strong foundation for academic growth and 
development at Lunenburg Primary School.  
 
At Lunenburg Primary School, our professional development efforts have been focused on enhancing inclusive 
practices and refining our instructional strategies. Central to this is our continued use of the HMH Into Reading and 
Into Math curricula, which guide our approach to literacy and mathematics instruction. Common Planning Time 
remains a priority in our building schedule, enabling teachers to collaborate, analyze student performance data, and 
align instructional practices to meet student needs effectively. We have also leveraged consultants and district 
curriculum coordinators to support our teaching staff in developing evidence-based instruction and fostering a 
consistent learning experience across grade levels.  In addition to academic growth, we place a strong emphasis on 
cultivating a positive, inclusive, and supportive school culture. Our staff consistently works to create an environment 
where all students feel valued and empowered.

Lunenburg Primary School is a Title One school, and we are committed to providing all students with the support they 
need to succeed. To ensure that every child has access to the services and resources they require, we have continued to 
refine our tiered instructional system. As part of this system, we conduct common assessments in the fall, winter, and

LUNENBURG MIDDLE SCHOOL 
 
Lunenburg Middle School provides a rigorous academic curriculum as well as strong programs that support students’ 
social and emotional growth. These programs ensure that each child becomes a self-motivated, creative, lifelong learner 
in a safe, stimulating environment that encourages diversity and open honest communication.   The dedicated and ac-
complished staff of professionals within the school embraces the philosophy of educating the whole child academically, 
socially, and emotionally. This philosophy is a means by which all students become positive community members that 
possess the skills to be successful in high school and beyond.  
Lunenburg Middle School is a co-located middle - high school serving grades six through eight. Our effective, student 
centered middle school embraces interdisciplinary teams of teachers that enable teachers to work with smaller groups of 
students. Smaller groups of students allow teachers to learn and engage students in their preferred learning style, making 
them more confident and successful.  
Our effective middle school model is student centered and provides opportunities to explore a variety of different inter-
ests and talents through special areas and enrichment classes and extracurricular activities. One of our goals is for stu-
dents to discover something they are passionate about and want to continue exploring as they move through high school 
and beyond. We will continue to seek out opportunities for students that foster critical thinking and problem solving 
skills that prepare them for high school and beyond or where their passion may lead them to.  
Our grade eight team of teachers continue to implement a schedule that mirrors the high school schedule. Our philoso-
phy is to ease the difficult transition from middle school to the first year of high school by developing student’s inde-
pendent skills they will require to be successful in high school. Grade eight teachers function as one inclusive team 
where students take classes with a variety of different teachers as they would in a high school setting. This differs from 
our two interdisciplinary team of teachers that have been a successful model in grades six and seven.      
LMS continues to utilize the i-Ready diagnostic to provide an additional tool to assess students’ skills in addition to 
classroom instruction.  Parents will be provided fall, winter and spring benchmark assessment reports created by i- ready 
so they too can monitor student progress.           
Lunenburg Middle School has benefited from a top notch guidance support team. A guidance counselor and a school 
social worker coupled with our shared school psychologist has been able to provide a high level of social emotional sup-
port to our students and their families. Our guidance team has been able to meet the needs of our students while prepar-
ing them to be available for academic learning.

LUNENBURG HIGH SCHOOL 
 
Lunenburg High School and its faculty continue to focus on rigorous curriculum, high standards and opportunities for 
students, both academic and extracurricular, so that every student feels supported and included in our community. LHS 
faculty is student centered in their teaching practices, and strive to learn and to change in order to best serve all of our 
students.  
2024 School Year  
Lunenburg High School returned this August with a renewed philosophy of support for our school community. We have 
assembled an outstanding, talented faculty with a shared vison of supporting students academically, socially and emo-
tionally as they tackle a rigorous and challenging curriculum.  With our renewed sense of support, students are able to 
acquire the skills necessary to earn a high school diploma as well as be successful in post-secondary education as well as 
in the work force.  One pillar of support that has proven to be successful for students is the LHS Intervention Teacher. 
This teaching position provides direct instruction to students who were identified by their teachers as to needing addi-
tional support to be successful in the classroom. Students report to the Interventionist during their study skills block and 
receive assistance to the specific class or classes they are struggling in. The data show the students participating in the 
intervention classroom show substantial improvement in the completion of classwork and in their overall grades.    
===PAGE 112===
112 
 
We continue to structure our Algebra classes to ensure all students are successful. We continue to keep class sizes low in 
Algebra II that allow teachers to provide more personalized, individualized instruction while working with smaller 
groups of students in a traditionally academically demanding course. This has been an effective strategy as you can see 
though our results in the MCAS table below.    
The restoration of faculty and courses at Lunenburg High School have allowed students a more varied options for course 
selection, provided individualized and programmatic support and structure for students to be successful.

The results indicate the LHS percentage scores for meeting and exceeding expectations are high, higher than the state 
average and in mathematics, 14 percentage points higher than the state average when compared to schools who serve 
students in similar grade levels. Reducing class sizes and providing students with additional individual classroom sup-
port was a successful strategy for improving student learning. The percentage of grade 10 students scoring in the meeting 
and exceeding expectations category in mathematics is the highest percentage in the district.   
We were also very pleased to learn that 30 students at Lunenburg High School received the state’s John and Abigail Ad-
ams Scholarship this year. The scholarship is available to students whose MCAS performance puts them at the top 25 
percent of their district. To be eligible, students must either score Advanced on one exam and Proficient on the other, or 
Advanced on both the English and Math exams. That is approximately 30 % of grade 10 students receiving the award.

-star team.

CURRICULUM  
Teachers and administrators continue to refine, revise and create curriculum that is aligned to the state frameworks and is 
rigorous and challenging. This year’s focus is to work in vertical content area teams to ensure a smooth transition for 
students as they advance grade by grade. This process also ensures that students will have and build upon the educational 
skills that they will need to be successful in future grades. Scope and sequence maps have been created in order to assist 
in this grade level curriculum mapping.   
Lunenburg High School continues to provide a high quality education that ensures student success and promotes a pas-
sion for lifelong learning.  The key components to our success continue to be our talented faculty, our parents and our 
community members who support our teaching and learning. Most of all, our students who inspire us through their com-
mitment to learning. Together we continue to make the difference in the lives of students.

B. Proposed §250-4.13F  
  
1. Purpose  
 
The purpose of this subsection is to reasonably regulate the development and operation of Battery Energy 
Storage Systems (BESS) by providing standards for the placement, design, construction, operation, monitor-
ing, modification, and removal of such systems that address public safety, minimize impacts on scenic, natu-
ral, and historic resources and to provide adequate financial assurance for the decommissioning of such sys-
tems in a manner which fulfills the following objectives:  
 
A. To designate properties suitable for the location, construction and operation of BESS;  
B. To ensure compatible land uses in the vicinity of the areas affected by BESS;  
C. To mitigate the impacts of BESS on environmental resources, other protected resources, and private 
property; and  
D. To create synergy between BESS development and the Commonwealth of Massachusetts Act to Ad-
vance Clean Energy that established the Clean Peak Standard Energy Storage System.  
 
2. Applicability  
Provisions of this subsection §250-4.13F shall apply as follows:   
  
A. To all Battery Energy Storage Systems as defined herein, which are permitted, installed, or modified in 
the Town of Lunenburg excluding general maintenance and repair.  
B. To modifications, retrofits or replacements of an existing BESS that increase the total battery energy 
storage.  
C. Except as an accessory to an approved solar generating facility, BESS facilities shall not be constructed 
or installed prior to the effective date of this by-law.  
 
3. Definitions:  
 
The definitions below are specific to the requirements of this subsection §250-4.13F and are intended to 
supplement the standard definitions of the Zoning Bylaws, Article II (§250-2.1).  
 
A. Battery Energy Storage Management System: A system that protects energy storage systems from operat-
ing outside their safe operating parameters and disconnects electrical power to the energy storage system or 
places it in a safe condition if potentially hazardous temperatures or other conditions are detected.   
 
===PAGE 150===
150 
 
B. Battery Energy Storage System: One or more devices, assembled together, capable of storing energy in or-
der to supply electrical energy at a future time to the local power loads, to the utility grid, or for grid sup-
port. For the purposes of this definition, a BESS shall not be considered a Public Utility and Facility.  
C. Battery Energy Storage System, Accessory: A Battery Energy Storage System installed and operated to 
support an on -site Solar Generating Facility. This would include a Solar Parking Canopy as defined in 
§250-4.5A and §250-4.6A.  
D. Battery Energy Storage System, Standalone: A system that is capable of absorbing energy from the electric 
grid, storing it for a period of time and thereafter distributing electricity.  
E. Battery Energy Storage System, Small scale: A system which serves  
        [1] a residential, commercial, industrial, or institutional development or  
        [2] a parking facility which is not a solar generating facility but has one or more Electric Vehicle Charg - 
        ing stations and the BESS is primarily associated with the Electric Vehicle Charging station functions.  
F. Battery Energy Storage System, Medium scale: A pad site or sites no more than 10,000 square feet on a 
single parcel of land.  
G. Battery Energy Storage System, Large scale: Pad site(s) larger than 10,000 square feet on a single parcel of 
land.  
H. Pad compound: The enclosure (including the fence) in which one or more pads are installed upon which 
standalone Battery Energy Storage Systems are installed.  
I. Pad site: Locations within a pad compound on which Battery Energy Storage Systems equipment are posi-
tioned.  
J. Site Safety Plan: A plan which demonstrates the safe development, operation, and access to/from the facili-
ty taking into consideration such factors as, but not limited to, means of access, roadway conditions, prox-
imity to residential and other populated structures, hazardous materials, and monitoring protocols, etc.  
K. Site Security Plan: A plan which details how the BESS will be secured from unauthorized entry and details 
the method of monitoring of entry.

250-4.9 WATER SUPPLY PROTECTION DISTRICT  
A. PURPOSE OF DISTRICT  
The purpose of this Water Supply Protection District is to:  
1. promote the health, safety, and general welfare of the community by ensuring an adequate quality and 
quantity of drinking water for the Town of Lunenburg;  
2. preserve and protect existing and potential sources of drinking water;  
3. conserve natural resources in the Town of Lunenburg;  
4. and  prevent temporary and permanent contamination of the environment.  
 
B. SCOPE OF AUTHORITY 
1. The Water Supply Protection District is an overlay district superimposed on the zoning districts. This over-
lay district shall apply to all new construction, reconstruction, or expansion of existing buildings and new 
or expanded uses. Applicable activities and uses in a portion of one of the underlying zoning districts that 
fall within the Water Supply Protection District must additionally comply with the requirements of this by-
law. Uses prohibited in the underlying zoning districts shall not be permitted in the Water Supply Protec-
tion District.  
2. Nothing in 250-4.9 imposes regulation on parcels or portions of parcels located within Zone I.

250-4.9 WATER SUPPLY PROTECTION DISTRICT   
 
A. PURPOSE OF DISTRICT  
The purpose of this Water Supply Protection District is to:  
1. Promote the health, safety, and general welfare of the community by ensuring an adequate quality and 
quantity of drinking water for the Town of Lunenburg;  
2. preserve and protect existing and potential sources of drinking water;  
3. Conserve natural resources in the Town of Lunenburg; and  
4. Prevent temporary and permanent contamination of the environment.  
 
  
B. SCOPE OF AUTHORITY  
  
1. The Water Supply Protection District is an overlay district superimposed on the zoning districts. This over-
lay district shall apply to all new construction, reconstruction, or expansion of existing buildings and new 
or expanded uses. Applicable activities and uses in a portion of one of the underlying zoning districts that 
fall within the Water Supply Protection District must additionally comply with the requirements of this by-
law. Uses prohibited in the underlying zoning districts shall not be permitted in the Water Supply Protec-
tion District.  
 
C. DEFINITIONS  
  
Automobile Graveyard: An establishment that is maintained, used, or operated for storing, keeping, buying, or 
selling wrecked, scrapped, ruined, or motor vehicle parts as defined in MGL c.140B, s.1.  
  
Aquifer: A geologic formation composed of rock, sand or gravel that contains significant amounts of potential-
ly recoverable water.  
  
CMR: Code of Massachusetts Regulations.  
  
Commercial Fertilizer: Any substance containing one or more recognized plant nutrients which is used for its 
plant nutrient content and which is designed for use, or claimed to have value in promoting plant growth, ex-
cept un-manipulated animal and vegetable manures, marl, lime, limestone, wood ashes, and gypsum, and other 
products exempted by state regulations.  
 
Contaminated Groundwater: Groundwater containing oil and/or hazardous material at concentrations equal to 
or greater than a release notification threshold established by 310 CMR 40.0300 and 40.1600.  
  
Contaminated Surface Water: Surface water containing oil and/or hazardous material associated with a release 
for which notification is required under 310 CMR 40.0300 and 40.1600.  
 
Discharge: The accidental or intentional disposal, deposit, injection, dumping, spilling, leaking, pouring, or 
placing of toxic or hazardous material or hazardous waste upon or into any land or water such that it may enter 
the surface or ground waters.

Dry Well: A subsurface pit with open-jointed lining or holes through which storm - water drainage from roofs, 
basement floors, foundations, or other areas seep into the surrounding soil.  
  
Water Supply Protection District : The land area consisting of aquifers, Zone II recharge areas, and Zone III 
areas, all as approved by MassDEP and identified on a map and adopted pursuant to this bylaw.  
  
Hazardous Material: Any substance in any form which because of its quantity, concentration, or its chemical, 
corrosive, flammable, reactive, toxic, infectious or radioactive characteristics, either separately or in combina-
tion with one or more substances, constitutes a present or potential threat to human health, safety, welfare, or 
to the environment, when improperly stored, treated, transported, disposed of, used, or otherwise managed. 
Hazardous material includes, without limitation, synthetic organic chemicals, petroleum products, heavy met-
als, radioactive or infectious materials, and all substances defined as toxic or hazardous under MGL c. 21E. 
This term shall not include hazardous waste or oil.  
  
Historical High Groundwater Table Elevation: A groundwater elevation determined from monitoring wells and 
historical water table fluctuation data compiled by the United States Geological Survey.  
  
Hazardous Waste : A substance or combination of substances, which because of quantity, concentration, or 
physical, chemical or infectious characteristics may cause, or significantly contribute to an increase in mortali-
ty or an increase in serious irreversible, or incapacitating reversible illness or pose a substantial present or po-
tential hazard to human health, safety, or welfare or to the environment when improperly treated, stored, trans-
ported, used or disposed of, or otherwise managed. This term shall include all substances identified as hazard-
ous pursuant to the Hazardous Waste Regulations, 310 CMR 30.000.  
 
Impervious Surface: Material or structure on, above, or below the ground that does not allow precipitation or 
surface water runoff to penetrate into the soil.  
  
Interim Wellhead Protection Area (IWPA) : The MassDEP designated protection radius around a public water 
well that lacks a Zone II.  
  
Junkyard: An establishment that is maintained, operated, or used for storing, keeping, buying, or selling junk, 
or for the maintenance or operation of an automobile graveyard, as defined in MGL c.140B, s.1.  
  
Landfill: A facility established in accordance with a valid site assignment for the purposes of disposing solid 
waste into or on the land, pursuant to the Solid Waste  
Regulations, 310 CMR 19.006.  
  
MassDEP: Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection. MGL: Massachusetts General Law.  
  
Petroleum Product: Includes, but not limited to, fuel oil; gasoline; diesel; kerosene; aviation jet fuel; aviation 
gasoline; lubricating oils; oily sludge; oil refuse; oil mixed with other wastes; crude oils; or other liquid hydro-
carbons regardless of specific gravity. Petroleum product shall not include liquefied petroleum gas including, 
but not limited to, liquefied natural gas, propane, or butane.  
  
Non-Sanitary Wastewater: Wastewater discharges from industrial and commercial facilities containing wastes 
from any activity other than collection of sanitary sewage including, but not limited to, activities specified in 
310 CMR 15.004(6).  
  
Open Dump: A facility operated or maintained in violation of the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act 42 
U.S.C. 4004(a)(b), or state regulations and criteria for solid waste disposal.  
  
Recharge Areas: Land areas, such as a Zone II or an Interim Wellhead Protection Area, where precipitation 
and surface water infiltrates into the ground to replenish groundwater and aquifers used for public drinking 
water supplies.

Septage: The liquid, solid, and semi -solid contents of privies, chemical toilets, cesspools, holding tanks, or 
other sewage waste receptacles. This term shall not include any material that is a hazardous waste, as defined 
by 310 CMR 30.000.  
  
Sludge: The solid, semi -solid, and liquid residue that results from a process of wastewater treatment or drink-
ing water treatment including wastewater residuals. This term shall not include grit, screening, or grease and

Treatment Works : Any and all devices, processes and properties, real or personal, used in the collection, 
pumping, transmission, storage, treatment, disposal, recycling, reclamation, or reuse of waterborne pollutants, 
but not including any works receiving a hazardous waste from off the site of the works for the purpose of treat-
ment, storage, or disposal.  
  
Utility Works : Regulated activities providing for public services, including roads, water, sewer, electricity, 
gas, telephone, transportation, and their associated maintenance activities. This term shall include the installa-
tion of detention and retention basins for the purpose of controlling storm water.  
  
Very Small Quantity Generator : Any public or private entity, other than residential, which produces less than 
27 gallons (100 kilograms) a month of hazardous waste or waste oil, but not including any acutely hazardous 
waste as defined in 310 CMR 30.136.  
  
Waste Oil Retention Facility : A waste oil collection facility for automobile service stations, retail outlets, and 
marinas which is sheltered and has adequate protection to contain a spill, seepage, or discharge of petroleum 
waste products in accordance with MGL c.21. s.52A.  
  
Zone I: A protective radius around a public water supply well or wellfield as approved by MassDEP and de-
fined under the Massachusetts Drinking Water Regulations 310 CMR 22.00. “provided, however, that Zone 1 
ends at the water ’s edge and does not extend into the waters of Hickory Hills Lake, Lake Shirley or Whalom 
Lake.”  
  
Zone II: The delineated recharge area to a public drinking water well as approved by MassDEP and defined 
under the Massachusetts Drinking Water Regulations 310 CMR  22.00. For the purposes of this § 250 -4.9, 
Zone II shall not extend within that area included in Zone I. Accordingly, no parcels or portions of parcels lo-
cated within Zone I are considered to be included within Zone II.  
 
Zone III: An area beyond a Zone II recharge area, as approved by MassDEP and defined under the Massachu-
setts Drinking Water Regulations 310 CMR 22.00.  
 
D. ESTABLISHMENT AND DELINEATION OF WATER SUPPLY PROTECTION DISTRICT  
 
For the purposes of this bylaw, there are hereby established within the Town of Lunenburg certain water sup-
ply protection areas a Zone I, Zone II, and Zone III which consist of a protective radius around a public water 
supply well or wellfield (Zone I), aquifers or recharge areas (Zone II), and certain protected areas beyond those 
Zone II areas (Zone III).   These areas are delineated on a map entitled Lunenburg Water District Aquifer Pro-
tection Zones dated July 2021 which is hereby made part of the Water Supply Protection District Bylaw and is 
on file in the office of the Town Clerk. Provided, however,  Zone 1 ends at the water ’s edge and does not ex-
tend into the waters of Hickory Hills Lake, Lake Shirley or Whalom Lake.  
 
E. DISTRICT BOUNDARY DISPUTES  
 
1. If the location of the Water Supply Protection District in relation to a particular parcel is in doubt, resolu-
tion of the boundary dispute shall be through a Special Permit application to the Special Permit Granting 
Authority. Any application for a special permit for this purpose shall be accompanied by adequate docu-
mentation.

2. Burden of proof shall be upon the landowner to demonstrate that the location of the Water Supply Protec-

Town may engage a professional engineer, hydrologist, geologist, or soil scientist to determine more accu-

F. PERMITTED USES  
 
1. Irrespective of the permitted uses in the underlying district, only the following uses are permitted within 
Zone I or Zone II of the Water Supply Protection District except as may also be allowed by Special Permit 
in § 250-4.9 H. This same restriction does not apply to Zone III.  
2. The following uses are permitted within Zone I:  
A. Conservation of soil, water, plants, and wildlife;  
B. Outdoor recreation, nature study, swimming, boating, fishing, and hunting where otherwise le-
gally permitted;  
C. Raised wooden walkways, foot, bicycle and/or horse paths, and bridges;  
D. Normal operation and maintenance of existing water bodies and dams, splash boards, and other 
water control, supply, and conservation devices;  
E. Uses accessory and subordinate to the above uses, if permitted in the underlying district and 
subject to the provision of the underlying district, provided that the use does not result in cover-
age of more than 15% of the lot with impervious surfaces.  
F. Maintenance and repair of existing drinking water supply related works such as, but not limited 
to, wells, pipelines, aqueducts, and tunnels.  
G. Removing non-native, nuisance, or invasive species, interplanting native species, and control-
ling species in a manner approved by Mass DEP that preserves drinking water quality and mini-
mizes damage to surrounding, non- target species.  
H. Maintaining vegetation as approved by MassDEP including pruning, trimming, cutting, mow-
ing, and removing brush to prevent, control, and manage hazards, disease, insect, or fire dam-
age, and to preserve drinking water quality and maintain the condition of the premises.   
 
3. The following uses are permitted within Zone II and Zone III of the Water Supply Protection District, pro-
vided that all necessary permits, orders, or approvals required by local, state, or federal law are also ob-
tained:   
A. conservation of soil, water, plants, and wildlife;  
B. outdoor recreation, nature study, boating, fishing, and hunting where otherwise legally permit-
ted;  
C. foot, bicycle and/or horse paths, and bridges;  
D. normal operation and maintenance of existing water bodies and dams, splash boards, and other 
water control, supply, and conservation devices;  
E. maintenance, repair, and enlargement of any existing structure, subject to § 250 -4.9 G, § 250 -
4.9 H and § 250-4.9 J of this Zoning Bylaw;  
F. residential development, subject to § 250-4.9 G, § 250-4.9 H and § 250-4.9 J of this Zoning By-
law;  
G. farming, gardening, nursery, conservation, forestry, harvesting, and grazing, subject to § 250 -
4.9 G, § 250-4.9 H and § 250-4.9 J of this Zoning Bylaw;  
H. construction, maintenance, repair, and enlargement of existing drinking water supply related 
facilities such as, but not limited to, wells, pipelines, aqueducts, and tunnels but shall not in-
clude construction of new treatment works and related;  
I. Water Remediation Treatment works approved by MassDEP designed for the treatment of con-
taminated ground or surface water operating in compliance with 314 CMR 5.05(3).

G. PROHIBITED USES  
 
1. The following land uses and activities are prohibited in Zone II unless designed in accordance with the 
specified performance standards :  
A. landfills and open dumps;  
B. automobile graveyards and junkyards,;  
C. landfills receiving only wastewater residuals and/or septage, including those approved by 
MassDEP pursuant to MGL c. 21 s.26 through s.53, MGL c.111 s.17, and MGL c.83 s.6 and 
s.7;  
D. facilities that generate, treat, store, or dispose of hazardous waste that are subject to MGL c.21C 
and 310 CMR 30.000, except for:  
 1. very small quantity generators as defined under 310 CMR 30.000;  
 2. household hazardous waste centers and events under 310 CMR 30.390;  
 3. waste oil retention facilities required by MGL c. 21, s.52A; and  
 4. water remediation treatment works approved by MassDEP for the treatment of contaminated 
waters subject to § 250- 4.9 H.  
E. petroleum, fuel oil, and heating oil bulk stations and terminals including, but not limited to 
those listed under North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) Codes 424710 and 
454311, except for liquefied petroleum gas.  
F. storage of liquid hazardous materials and/or liquid petroleum products unless such storage is 
above ground level and on an impervious surface and either:  
      1. in container(s) or above ground tank(s) within a building; or  
       2.outdoors in covered container(s) or above ground tank(s) in an area that has a containment 
  system designed and operated to hold either; 10% of the total possible storage capacity of all 
  containers or 110% of the largest container ’s storage capacity, whichever is greater. However, 
  these storage requirements shall not apply to the replacement of existing tanks or systems for 
  the keeping, dispensing, or storing of gasoline provided the replacement is performed in a man
  ner consistent with state and local requirements;  
G. storage of sludge and septage, unless such storage is in compliance with 310 CMR 32.30 and 
310 CMR 32.31;  
H. storage of deicing chemicals, with the exception of small quantities stored in containers and 
ready for application, unless such storage, is within a structure designed to prevent the genera-
tion and escape of contaminated runoff or leachate;  
I. storage of animal manure, with the exception of small quantities stored in containers and ready 
for application, unless contained within a structure designed to prevent the generation and es-
cape of contaminated runoff or leachate;  
J. storage of commercial fertilizers, with the exception of small quantities stored in containers and 
ready for application, unless such storage is within a structure designed to prevent the genera-
tion and escape of contaminated runoff or leachate;  
K. stockpiling and disposal of snow and ice containing deicing chemicals brought in from outside 
the Water Supply Protection District;  
L. earth removal, consisting of the removal of soil, loam, sand, gravel, or any other earth material 
to within 4 feet of historical high groundwater as determined from monitoring wells and histori-
cal water table fluctuation data compiled by the United States Geological Survey, except for 
excavations for building foundations, roads, utility works or wetland restoration work conduct-
ed in accordance with a valid Order of Condition issued pursuant to MGL c. 131, s.40; and  
M. treatment or disposal works subject to 314 CMR 5.00, for non - sanitary wastewater, including 
those activities listed under 310 CMR 15.004(6), except for:  
1. treatment works approved by MassDEP designed for the treatment of contaminated ground 
or surface water and operating in compliance with 314 CMR 5.05(3) or 5.05(13) subject to 
§ 250-4.9 H; and   
2. publicly owned treatment works subject to § 250-4.9 H.

H. USES AND ACTIVITIES REQUIRING A SPECIAL PERMIT  
 
1. In Zone I the following uses and activities are permitted only upon the issuance of a Special Permit by the 
Special Permit Granting Authority (SPGA) under such conditions as they may require:  
A. Construction of new treatment works and related structures.  
B. Water remediation treatment works except as expressly permitted in Section 250-4.9F(1)(i)  
C. Publicly owned treatment works.  
D. Construction of new water treatment works.  
 
2. In Zone II, the following uses and activities are permitted only upon the issuance of a Special Permit by the 
Special Permit Granting Authority (SPGA) under such conditions as they may require:  
A. enlargement or alteration of existing uses that do not conform to the Water Supply Protection 
District;  
B. except as prohibited under § 250-4.9 G of this bylaw, activities that involve the handling of tox-
ic or hazardous materials in quantities greater than those associated with normal household use, 
and which are permitted in the underlying zoning district;  
C. rendering impervious any lot or parcel more than 15% or 2,500 square feet, whichever is great-
er; unless artificial recharge, that will not degrade water quality, is provided using methods 
demonstrated to be capable of removing contaminants from storm water and which are con-
sistent with methods described in MassDEP's Stormwater Handbook, Vol. I, II and III, as 
amended.  
D. Construction of new treatment works and related structures.  
E. Water remediation treatment works except as expressly permitted in Section 250-4.9F(1)(i)  
F. Publicly owned treatment works.  
G. Construction of new water treatment works. 
 
I. PROCEDURES FOR ISSUANCE OF SPECIAL PERMIT PURSUANT TO § 250-4.9   
 
1.  The Special Permit Granting Authority (SPGA) under this bylaw shall be the Planning Board. A special 
permit shall be granted if the SPGA determines, with advisement from the Lunenburg Water District, 
Building Commissioner, Conservation Commission, Department of Public Works, and Board of Health, 
that the intent of this bylaw, as well as its specific criteria, are met. The SPGA shall not grant a special per-
mit under this section unless the petitioner's application materials include, in the SPGA's opinion, suffi-
ciently detailed, definite, and credible information to support positive findings in relation to the standards 
given in this section. The SPGA shall document the basis for any departures from the recommendations of 
the other municipal boards, departments, or commissions in its decision.  
2. Upon receipt of the special permit application, the SPGA shall transmit one copy to the Lunenburg Water 
District, Building Commissioner, Conservation Commission, Department of Public Works, Board of 
Health, and Lunenburg Fire Department. Failure to respond in writing within 35 days of receipt shall be 
considered as no desire to comment. The necessary number of copies of the application shall be furnished 
by the applicant.  
3. The SPGA may grant the required special permit upon finding that the proposed use meets the following 
standards, those standards and requirements specified in § 250 -4.9 G of this bylaw, and any regulations or 
guidelines adopted by the SPGA. The proposed use must:   
A. in no way, during construction or thereafter, materially adversely affect the quality or quantity 
of the water supplies protected by the Water Supply Protection District; and  
B. be designed to avoid substantial disturbance of the soils, topography, drainage, vegetation, and 
other water-related natural characteristics of the site to be developed, except as depicted on an 
approved site plan.   
4.  The SPGA may adopt controls to govern design features of projects. Such controls shall be consistent with 
the Town's subdivision regulations, Site Plan Approval, and Stormwater Bylaws.
