 Executive Summary 
 I began my entry in Lunenburg by listening to students, staff, and families so we could start this 
 work with clarity and honesty. Three companion surveys asked shared questions about culture 
 and communication and group-specific questions about daily experience and needs. The goal 
 wasn’t a perfect instrument; it was a reliable baseline to guide action. 
 Who responded 
 ●  Students:  491 of 832 LMHS students (≈60%) across grades  6–12, with especially 
 strong participation in grades 6–8. 
 ●  Staff:  106 responses (≈38%) across roles and years  of service. 
 ●  Families:  186 responses from every school level; many  with children in multiple schools, 
 offering a districtwide lens. 
 What we heard from students 
 ●  Culture:  Most view their school culture positively;  district culture is also positive for 
 many, but more mixed. 
 ●  Support & challenge:  ≈70% feel supported socially,  emotionally, and academically; just 
 over 60% feel appropriately challenged. 
 ●  Belonging & respect:  This is our clearest gap. More  than half do not feel accepted for 
 who they are; only about one in five feel respected by peers, and many report 
 inconsistent respect from adults. 
 ●  Voice & daily experience:  Students want to be heard  and to see follow-through on 
 concerns (bullying, fairness). They also highlighted practical needs such as lunch quality, 
 activities (notably volleyball), facilities, and schedules. 
 ●  Communication:  Email is the anchor; in-person visibility  matters; short video/social can 
 help reach students where they are. 
 What we heard from staff 
 ●  Strengths:  Our people. Dedication, collegiality, and  care for the whole child are 
 consistent points of pride. 
===PAGE 2===
 ●  Culture:  Largely positive within buildings, with resilience and teamwork; districtwide 
 perceptions reflect recovery from past instability. Many note an improved tone this year. 
 ●  Challenges & Priorities:  Budget constraints drive  larger classes and fewer supports; 
 staffing shortages strain classrooms; uneven communication and involvement in 
 decisions have hurt trust. Top priorities: Student mental health and wellness; MTSS and 
 targeted EL supports; early literacy; meaningful professional learning; clear, timely, and 
 transparent communication; safe, well-maintained facilities. 
 What we heard from families 
 ●  Satisfaction:  Median 4/5; average 3.7—generally positive  with room to strengthen 
 consistency. 
 ●  Supports:  Social-emotional support is perceived slightly  stronger than academics, but 
 both show unevenness across classrooms and levels. 
 ●  Communication:  “Fairly well” overall at school and  district levels; families want 
 predictable, streamlined updates email first, supported by newsletters, the website, and 
 selective social media. 
 ●  Strengths:  Caring educators, strong community, safety,  and opportunities beyond the 
 classroom. 
 ●  Challenges & priorities:  Budget is the dominant concern,  closely tied to 
 staffing/retention, program stability, and class size. Families also name belonging, 
 bullying, and equitable access to opportunities. Top priorities: retain great staff, protect 
 academic programs, and support student mental health. 
 Cross-cutting conclusions 
 ●  Connection exists; belonging is fragile.  Many students  have friends, yet too many do 
 not feel accepted or respected. 
 ●  Culture is positive locally; trust is uneven systemwide.  Staff pride and teamwork are 
 strong; confidence in the system is rebuilding. 
 ●  Budget is a culture issue.  Fiscal instability shapes  options, expectations, and trust. 
 ●  Visibility and clarity matter.  People want leadership  they can see, communication they 
 can count on, and follow-through they can trust.

 What this means for Year 1 
 1.  Make belonging central:  Tighten bullying prevention  and response, model 
 adult-to-adult respect, and elevate student voice. 
 2.  Stabilize the system and tell the truth about trade-offs:  Bring radical clarity to what is 
 funded, what is paused, and why. 
 3.  Strengthen core instruction and supports:  Provide  consistent MTSS, strong support 
 for English Learners, and early literacy instruction, all backed by meaningful professional 
 learning. 
 4.  Simplify and align communication:  Email as the anchor;  predictable cadence; “you 
 said / we did” updates; presence in schools as the message behind the message. 
 Lunenburg’s heart, our people and our sense of community, is real and strong. If we lead visibly, 
 communicate plainly, budget transparently, and center belonging in every classroom, One 
 ʻOhana moves from theme to daily practice: welcoming all, lifting each other, learning with 
 purpose, and leading with heart.

 Introduction and Methodology 
 As I begin my time in Lunenburg, I felt it was important to start not by talking, but by listening. I 
 wanted to hear directly from the people who make up our school, our students, our staff, and our 
 families, about what is going well, what is challenging, and what you hope for in the future. This 
 entry report is my first attempt to share back what I heard, so that we can begin this work 
 together with clarity and honesty. 
 To do that, I invited each group to take part in a survey. Some of the questions were consistent 
 across groups. For example, everyone was asked about school and district culture. This gives 
 us the chance to compare perspectives and see where experiences align and where they 
 diverge. In other cases, the questions were different, shaped by the role each group plays in our 
 schools. Students were asked about belonging, respect, and acceptance, because those are 
 the day-to-day experiences that define school life. Staff reflected on their strengths and 
 challenges, their sense of culture, and the supports they need to serve students well. Families 
 were invited to share their satisfaction, communication preferences, and priorities for the future. 
 What I hoped to gain from this process was not a perfect survey or a set of quick answers, but a 
 baseline of understanding. From students, I wanted to know how it feels to walk through our 
 halls, to learn in our classrooms, and to be part of this community. From staff, I wanted to 
 understand both the pride they feel in their work and the pressures they carry. From families, I 
 wanted to learn how our schools look and feel from their vantage point—where they see 
 strengths and where they hope for change. 
 The responses tell a story of a district with a strong heart, one that is defined by caring people 
 and a deep sense of community. They also highlight areas where trust has been stretched, 
 where belonging is fragile, and where budget and staffing challenges weigh heavily. Listening to 
 these voices is only the beginning, but it is a crucial first step. This report is meant to give us a 
 shared starting point as we work to strengthen what is best about Lunenburg and address the 
 areas that need our collective focus. 
 The report that follows is organized in three parts. It begins with the voices of our students, then 
 turns to the perspectives of our staff, and finally to the reflections of our families. Each section 
 provides a window into their experiences, and together they create a fuller picture of where we 
 are as a district. The report concludes by weaving these threads together into a narrative about 
 where Lunenburg stands today, and where we have the greatest opportunities to grow. 
 Student Perspectives 
 Respondents 
 All students in grades 6–12 were invited to participate in the entry survey. Out of 832 students at 
 Lunenburg Middle High School (LMHS), 491 responded, representing nearly 60% of our 
 secondary student body. This strong participation reflects a willingness from students to share 
 their perspectives on their school experience. 
===PAGE 5===
 Responses came from every grade level, with the highest numbers from grades 7 and 8, 
 followed closely by grade 6. At the high school level, grades 9–11 provided steady participation, 
 while grade 12 responded at a smaller but still meaningful rate. 
 This distribution suggests that our younger students were especially eager to share their voices, 
 while our older students, particularly seniors, offered perspectives rooted in the culmination of 
 their Lunenburg education. These responses help us hear a full range of student experiences 
 across Lunenburg Public Schools. 
 Culture and Climate 
 School 
 Students’ reflections on school culture show a solid foundation of positivity. Nearly 
 three-quarters of respondents described their school as  Generally Positive  or  Very Positive  . This 
 indicates that many students feel supported and connected in their day-to-day school 
 environment. 
 About one-quarter of students described their experience as  Mixed  . These responses remind us 
 that while many aspects of school life are going well, some students are having more uneven 
 experiences. A very small number rated the culture as  Generally Negative  , underscoring that 
 not every student feels equally included. 
 Overall, the responses show that most students see their schools as welcoming and 
 constructive places to learn, while also pointing to areas where greater consistency would 
 benefit all learners. 

===PAGE 6===
 District 
 When asked about the district as a whole, a strong majority of students shared positive views, 
 with two-thirds describing district culture as  Generally  Positive  or  Very Positive  . This reflects 
 confidence in the district’s broader mission and direction. 
 At the same time, about one-third of students selected  Mixed  . Their responses suggest that the 
 district culture is not always experienced in the same way across schools, and that some 
 students encounter both encouraging and less encouraging elements. A small percentage 
 selected  Generally Negative  , highlighting the importance  of continuing to build stronger 
 connections at the district level. 
 These results indicate that while the district is widely regarded in a positive light, there is room 
 to strengthen alignment so more students share in that positive perspective. 
 Comparison of School and District Culture 
 When students described culture at both the school and district levels, an encouraging pattern 
 emerged. A strong majority rated their school culture positively, with nearly three out of four 
 students selecting  Generally Positive  or  Very Positive  .  Fewer students chose  Mixed  or  Negative  , 
 we can infer that most feel their day-to-day experiences in classrooms and hallways are 
 supportive and constructive. 
 The comparison highlights both promise and work ahead: schools are widely seen as positive 
 and welcoming, and the district can build on that foundation by ensuring each and every student 
 experiences the same sense of consistency and belonging at every level. 

===PAGE 7===
 Student perceptions of Support and Challenge 
 Support 
 When asked,  “I am supported socially, emotionally,  and academically,”  students’ responses 
 point to a promising level of support. More than 70% agreed or strongly agreed, reflecting the 
 commitment of staff to meet the diverse needs of learners. A quarter of students selected 
 Strongly Agree  , signaling that for many, the sense  of support is both visible and meaningful. 
 Nearly one in four students responded neutrally, and a smaller group expressed disagreement. 
 These results indicate that while most students experience strong support, not all are equally 
 certain about it. This highlights the importance of checking in with students who may not 
 consistently feel supported and ensuring that systems of care and guidance are accessible to 
 every learner. 
 Challenge 
 When asked,  “I am challenged academically in Lunenburg  schools,”  most students affirmed that 
 they do feel challenged. More than six in ten agreed or strongly agreed, suggesting that many 
 students experience their coursework as appropriately demanding. Nearly one in five selected 
 Strongly Agree  , showing that for some, academic challenge  is a clear and consistent part of 
 their education. 
 At the same time, about one-quarter of students responded neutrally, and just over one in ten 
 disagreed. These responses remind us that academic rigor is not felt evenly across the student 
 body. For some, expectations may feel too light, while for others, the challenge may not always 
 be evident. This feedback underscores the importance of ensuring that every student is 
 engaged at the right level; supported, stretched, and inspired in ways that help them grow. 
 Looking at these two questions together, a balanced picture emerges. Many students feel both 
 supported and challenged, which is the foundation of a strong educational experience. At the 
 same time, the neutral and disagreeing responses highlight the importance of continuing to 
 strengthen consistency;so that every student experiences both the encouragement and the 
 stretch that help them grow.

 Acceptance and Belonging 
 Acceptance 
 The results from our student survey on the statement  “I am accepted for who I am in 
 Lunenburg”  are sobering and important for us to reflect  on. 
 A significant number of students, more than half, indicated that they do not feel accepted, with 
 32% strongly disagreeing and another 20% disagreeing. Only about 30% of students agreed or 
 strongly agreed, while nearly 18% remained neutral. 
 This data tells us that while some students do feel a sense of belonging, far too many do not. 
 For a district that values community and inclusivity this should serve as a call to action. Our 
 students are telling us, in no uncertain terms, that they need to feel more seen, supported, and 
 valued for who they are. 
 As we move forward, this feedback will guide our work. Building an environment where every 
 student feels accepted is not just aspirational, it is essential. 

===PAGE 9===
 Peer Connections 
 When students were asked to describe how they connect with peers, the majority, just over half, 
 shared that they have a small group of close friends. Another third reported having a large friend 
 group, while about 12% said they mostly talk to just a few people. Very few students indicated 
 that they move across many groups, and a very small number shared that they only have 
 acquaintances or feel isolated. 
 This allows us to learn that most of our students do experience a sense of belonging, though 
 often within smaller, tighter circles rather than broad networks. At the same time, the data 
 reminds us to pay attention to those who feel more limited in their connections, especially the 
 small number of students who expressed feeling alone. Our goal as a district must be to ensure 
 that every student finds not only their circle of belonging but also feels comfortable connecting 
 more widely across the school community. 

===PAGE 10===
 When we look at these two sets of data side by side, an important story emerges. On one hand, 
 most of our students report having friends—whether in small, close-knit groups (51%) or larger 
 circles (35%). Only a very small number indicate that they feel isolated. 
 At the same time, when asked if they feel accepted for who they are, a much different picture 
 appears. More than half of students selected  disagree  or  strongly disagree,  while only 30% 
 agreed or strongly agreed. 
 This tells us that social connection and acceptance are not the same thing. While many 
 students have friendships, too many do not feel fully seen, valued, or accepted within the 
 broader school community. Strengthening belonging and inclusivity must be a priority if we want 
 our schools to reflect our community values. 

===PAGE 11===
 Peer Respect 
 Respect among peers emerged as one of the most concerning findings. Just one in five 
 students agreed that they feel respected, while more than 40% said they do not. Over a third 
 remained neutral, which alludes to inconsistency or uncertainty in how students experience 
 respect at school. These results make clear the importance of fostering a culture where respect 
 is the norm and every student feels valued. 
 When we place this alongside what students told us about acceptance, an even clearer picture 
 comes into focus. More than half of students reported that they do not feel accepted for who 
 they are, while only about 30% feel positively. Acceptance, like respect, is not yet a consistent 
 part of every student’s daily experience. 
 And yet, the story begins with friendships. Most of our students do report having friends, 
 whether in small, close-knit groups or larger circles. Very few say they are isolated. But having 
 friends, we now see, does not necessarily translate into feeling accepted or respected. 
 When combined, these three measures show us where we must focus. Connection exists in our 
 schools, but it is not always deep enough to ensure that every student feels truly accepted and 
 respected. This is where our work lies: moving beyond social groups to build a school culture of 
 belonging grounded in acceptance and respect. 
 Teacher Respect 
 When students were asked if teachers respect all students, their responses remind us of the 
 importance of this core value. While many students feel supported, a significant number shared 
 that they do not consistently experience respect from every adult. About 70% selected  disagree 
 or  strongly disagree,  while only 17% responded positively.  These results do not define us, but 
 they do highlight an opportunity. Respect is the foundation of our work, and the example we set 
 shapes the culture students experience every day. Together, we can reflect on these findings, 
 learn from them, and recommit to building classrooms where every student feels respected, 
 valued, and seen. By working together, we have the opportunity to turn this feedback into

 meaningful change, ensuring that every student experiences the respect and care that define 
 who we are as a school community. 
 These four measures tell a powerful story about belonging in our schools. Most students do 
 report having friends, whether in small close-knit groups or larger circles. Yet when asked if they 
 feel accepted for who they are, more than half said no. When asked about respect from their 
 peers, only one in five responded positively, and respect from teachers was rated even lower, 
 with nearly 70% of students saying teachers do not respect all students. 
 The progression of these findings leads us to a clear conclusion: connection is present, but 
 belonging is fragile. Friendships exist, but they do not guarantee that students feel truly 
 accepted or respected either by peers or by adults. If we want our schools to reflect our values, 
 we must strengthen the culture of respect and ensure that acceptance is not conditional but

 Student Voice 
 Students were asked if their voices and opinions matter in our schools, many students shared 
 that they do not feel heard. More than half disagreed with this statement, while only about one in 
 four expressed agreement. A quarter of students remained neutral, which may reflect mixed or 
 inconsistent experiences. This feedback is important because when students believe their 
 voices matter, they are more engaged, more connected, and more invested in their learning. 
 These results show us that we need to do more to ensure every student feels that their 
 perspective is valued and their voice has a place in shaping their school experience. 

===PAGE 14===
 Students were asked what they would change about Lunenburg, many students responded with 
 a simple  “nothing.”  They described the schools as  “perfect,”  “great,”  or said they  “wouldn’t 
 change a thing.”  At the same time, their open-ended  responses revealed several strong themes 
 that deserve our attention. 
 The most common concern was the budget. Students asked us to  “give it more money,”  “fix the 
 low budget,”  and  “be more financially stable.”  These  comments show that even our learners are 
 aware of the financial pressures facing our schools and how those challenges shape their 
 experience. 
 Lunch was another frequent topic. Students asked for  “better school lunches,”  “more food 
 options,”  and even  “dessert with every lunch.”  Others  suggested  “vending machines”  or  “longer 
 lunch times.”  Their feedback points to the central  role that food and shared time together play in 
 shaping school climate. 
 Sports and activities also came through strongly. Over and over, students requested a volleyball 
 team. They also asked for more afterschool programs and improved athletic facilities, reflecting 
 their desire for more opportunities to connect beyond the classroom. 
 Many students spoke directly about school culture. They asked us to  “stop bullying,”  “make 
 students more respectful,”  and even to  “get rid of  homophobic, racist, and transphobic kids.” 
 These candid comments underscore how deeply students want a learning environment built on 
 respect and kindness. 
 Students also raised questions about policies that shape daily life. They mentioned  “the phone 
 policy,”  called for  “no homework,”  and asked about  “changing the dress code.”  While not every 
 suggestion is practical, these comments reveal how closely students pay attention to the rules 
 that define their school day. 
 Some students focused on the structure of the day, asking for  “later start times,”  “fewer packed 
 classes,”  and  “longer passing times.”  Others looked  to the curriculum, requesting  “more AP 
 class offerings,”  “STEM programs,”  and  “more creative  electives.”  Facilities also came up often, 
 with requests for  “bigger lockers,”  “air conditioning,”  and  “cleaner bathrooms.” 
 This blend of voices,from students who see their schools as already excellent to those pushing 
 for specific improvements,offers us both encouragement and direction. Their words remind us 
 that while many are satisfied, there are also clear opportunities to improve how we support, 
 respect, and engage every learner. 
===PAGE 15===
 Overall, student voices reflect both pride and a desire for growth in Lunenburg schools. While 
 many expressed satisfaction with their school experience, others highlighted practical needs like 
 food, funding, sports, and facilities, as well as deeper concerns around respect, kindness, and 
 fairness. 
 When asked if there was anything else they wanted to share, most students simply responded 
 “no” or “nothing,” which in itself indicates that many felt their voices were already heard in earlier 
 parts of the survey. Among the comments that were shared, several were positive: “It is a great 
 school,” “I love it,” “It’s awesome,” “Teachers are very supportive,” and “Overall it has been a 
 good experience.” Students often praised teachers, with notes like “Mrs. Warren the art teacher 
 and all of my other teachers are great,” and “Mr. Sheasgreen is the best.” 
 At the same time, some students raised concerns. A number mentioned bullying, saying “people 
 get bullied a lot,” “boys keep teasing me at school,” and “I have been bullied and nothing was 
 done.” Others noted broader issues such as past leadership, budget cuts, or building conditions 
 (“poor leadership of the schools in the past,” “the high school building is falling apart”). A few 
 highlighted struggles with belonging, sharing experiences of friendship challenges, feeling 
 alone, or not fitting in. There were also calls for better communication and responsiveness, with 
 one student remarking that when complaints are made, “action needs to be addressed soon, not 
 next year.” 
 These responses reflect both appreciation and challenge. Many students are proud of their 
 schools, enjoy their teachers, and feel supported. At the same time, others are asking for us to 
 listen more carefully to issues like bullying, respect, and belonging. Their voices remind us that 
 while much is going well, we have more work to do to ensure that every student not only learns 
 but also feels safe, valued, and included in the life of our schools. 
 Communication and Relationship with the Superintendent 
 When asked about preferred modes of communication with the superintendent, students 
 expressed a clear preference for Email Updates, with 123 mentions by far the most common 
 and consistent choice. The next most requested option was In-person visits or drop-ins at 
 schools (49 mentions), showing that visibility and personal connection matter to many. 
 Instagram Reels also stood out with 27 mentions, pointing to some students value short, 
 engaging content on platforms they already use. Smaller but still notable groups identified Office 
 Hours (17 mentions) and Video Messages (15 mentions) as additional ways they would like to 
 hear from the superintendent. 
 The results highlight a balance of needs: students rely on email for consistent information, they 
 appreciate opportunities to see and interact with leadership in person, and they are open to 
 more creative outreach through videos and social media. This mix reflects both their desire for 
 clear communication and their interest in feeling personally connected to district leadership. 
===PAGE 16===
 When students were asked about the importance of having a superintendent who is visible and 
 accessible in their schools, student responses indicate that they largely see superintendent 
 visibility as valuable. More than half rated it a 3 or 4, signaling that while not everyone sees it as 
 critical, most want some level of presence and accessibility. Over 50 students rated it at the 
 highest level, showing that for many, visible leadership really matters. Only a small portion felt it 
 was unimportant, underscoring that accessibility is a meaningful part of how students 
 experience leadership in their schools. 
 I asked students if there was anything else I should know about communication, most said 
 “nothing”  or  “I don’t know.”  At first glance, that  might seem unhelpful, but in many ways it tells 
 me that for most, communication already feels steady and familiar. 
 Among those who did share, several affirmed that they feel informed, with comments like  “I’m 
 getting the information that I need”  and  “communication  here is positive.”  Others reminded me 
 that presence matters. Simple notes like  “just being  there, so we feel like you want to get to 
 know us”  and  “better to be in person”  underscore the  value of showing up where students are. 
 Students also tied communication to respect and listening. They asked that we  “make sure 
 everyone is heard,”  “understand students and teachers,”  and  “communicate regularly so 
 everyone knows what is going on.” 
 There were also reminders that communication is not just about information; it’s about action. A 
 few students spoke candidly about bullying, fairness, and follow-through:  “Other students are 
 mean to people just because they are different,”  and  “when students make complaints, action 
 needs to be addressed soon, not next year.” 

===PAGE 17===
 What I take from this is simple but powerful: communication matters most when it is clear, 
 consistent, human, and visible. Students want to feel included, respected, and assured that 
 when they speak up, they are heard and their concerns lead to action. 
 Looking Ahead 
 Our students were generous and honest in sharing their experiences of school in Lunenburg. 
 Their voices point to both the strengths they value and the challenges we need to confront if we 
 are to make our schools truly inclusive and supportive. 
 The student data tells a story that is both encouraging and challenging. Our young people value 
 their schools, they see kindness in their teachers and peers, and many feel connected and 
 supported. Concurrently, they are calling us to do more; ensure that every student feels 
 accepted, respected, and heard. Their voices reinforce the urgency of creating a culture where 
 respect is consistent, communication is clear and human, and every student has the opportunity 
 to belong.

 community. In fact, every category of service is well represented. This mix brings strength to our 
 district: new educators and staff bring fresh perspectives and energy, while long-serving 
 members contribute stability, institutional knowledge, and continuity for our students and 
 families.

 Culture and Climate 
 School Culture 
 When asked about the culture at their work location, most staff described it as generally positive 
 or very positive. Many noted that, despite challenges, there is a strong sense of collegiality, 
 teamwork, and commitment to students that sustains their school communities. Staff expressed 
 pride in their work and appreciation for colleagues who continue to show up for one another and 
 for students, even through difficult times. 
 At the same time, a significant number of responses reflected a mixed picture. These staff 
 members described both positives and frustrations, often pointing to the cumulative impact of 
 recent years. Budget cuts, position eliminations, and administrative turnover have left some 
 feeling that their schools are “hurting.” In particular, high class sizes, reductions in support staff, 
 and the perception that some courses or departments are undervalued were identified as areas 
 of concern. As one staff member shared,  “We are a  community that has always pushed through 
 difficult times even when we should have stood our ground and said no. Our building is 
 hurting… and our students are suffering because of it.” 
 A smaller portion of responses described the culture as generally negative or very negative, 
 highlighting frustrations with feeling unheard, under-resourced, or stretched too thin. These 
 voices remind us that, for some, morale remains low and confidence in the system is fragile. 
 Encouragingly, several staff members noted that things already feel as though they are 
 beginning to improve. One respondent wrote simply,  “Feels like it’s getting better since Dr. F has 
 started.”  This feedback underscores both the challenges  and the opportunities ahead: while we 
 must acknowledge the pain of the past few years, there is a shared desire to rebuild trust, 
 restore stability, and strengthen a culture where every staff member feels valued and every 
 student is supported. 
===PAGE 20===
 District Culture 
 When asked about the overall culture in Lunenburg Public Schools, most staff described it as 
 generally positive, with a significant number also reporting it as mixed. These responses 
 suggest a district that is rebuilding: while optimism is strong, many staff continue to carry the 
 weight of recent challenges. 
 Several respondents reflected on the damage to morale and trust caused by past leadership 
 and by budget-related stress. One staff member shared:  “It was very negative with our previous 
 superintendent. She did real damage to morale and trust. However, I am cautiously optimistic 
 that you may be able to make some real progress. I have already really appreciated the change 
 in tone.”  This sentiment was echoed by others who  noted that, while the last few years have 
 been difficult, the tone and direction of leadership this year feel different and more hopeful. 
 A number of comments highlighted budget anxiety and staffing shortages as key drivers of 
 stress. Staff described operating with “skeleton staff” levels, feeling stretched thin with 
 “curriculum demands and fewer and fewer support people every year,” and a culture of needing 
 to justify one’s position amid uncertainty. These conditions leave many feeling that, despite their 
 best efforts, it is difficult to provide the level of support and consistency students deserve. 
 Encouragingly, some staff emphasized that the culture is already improving this year, pointing to 
 shifts in communication, tone, and transparency. One wrote simply,  “Improving a lot so far this 
 year.”  Another shared,  “Again, we are new to this  school but so far the climate has been very 
 welcoming and helpful.”  These comments, coupled with  the overall balance of responses, 
 suggest that while challenges remain, there is a shared sense that the district is moving in the 
 right direction.

 Taken together, these results reflect a district culture that is in transition. The scars of financial

 Comparative Analysis: Work Location vs. District Culture 
 When viewed side by side, the data on staff perceptions of culture at their individual work 
 locations and of the district as a whole reveal both important consistencies and some 
 meaningful differences. At both levels, staff most often described the culture as “generally 
 positive” or “mixed.” This indicates that while many employees find collegiality, teamwork, and 
 support within their buildings, there is also a significant undercurrent of frustration, fatigue, and 
 concern. A smaller but important share of responses fell into the “generally negative” 
 or “very negative” categories, underscoring that for some, morale and trust remain fragile. 
 At the district level, staff were more likely to reflect on system-wide issues such as budget cuts, 
 leadership transitions, and long-term instability. In contrast, the work location data leaned more 
 heavily on immediate workplace climate such as relationships with colleagues, class sizes, 
 staffing levels, and the daily experience of supporting students. 
 Across both sets of data, staff emphasized resilience and commitment. Many noted their pride in 
 colleagues who continue to show up for one another and for students, even during difficult 
 times. Encouragingly, a number of respondents in both surveys said that the culture already 

===PAGE 22===
 feels like it is beginning to improve this year. They pointed to a change in tone, greater clarity in 
 communication, and appreciation for leadership that feels more transparent. Staff also 
 consistently highlighted the values they share: a deep commitment to students, a belief in the 
 importance of all courses and programs, and a desire for their voices to be heard. These 
 common values offer a strong foundation for building a healthier and more collaborative culture. 
 At the same time, staff made it clear that there is important work ahead. Many district-level 
 responses referenced the damage to morale and trust caused by past leadership and financial 
 stress. Rebuilding confidence will require transparency, follow-through, and accountability in all 
 aspects of district operations. Staff also spoke candidly about the stress caused by budget 
 uncertainty, high class sizes, and reduced staffing. They want to know that resources will be 
 managed responsibly and equitably, and that their work will be valued across all schools and 
 departments. Several noted the challenge of feeling unheard or dismissed during times of 
 change. Creating consistent opportunities for staff input, and then showing how that input 
 informs decisions, will strengthen trust and belonging. Finally, while many described their

 Conclusion 
 Lunenburg is a district with a strong heart. Over the past several weeks, I’ve listened to 
 students, staff, and families describe what they love about our schools and where they want us 
 to grow. A clear picture has emerged. Day to day, our classrooms are powered by caring adults 
 and a close-knit community that knows its children by name. Most students and staff describe 
 their school culture as positive, and families, on the whole, are satisfied with their children’s 
 experiences. That foundation matters. It tells me that our best work is already happening closest 
 to students in relationships, in teams, and in the small moments of care that define a school’s 
 soul. 
 At the same time, the story is more complicated when we look across the whole system. Many 
 students report having friends, yet too many do not feel fully accepted or respected by peers 
 and some do not feel respected by adults. Families appreciate steady communication but want 
 it to be clearer, more streamlined, and more consistent. Staff describe colleagues who show up 
 for one another every day, even as they navigate the weight of budget constraints, staffing 
 shortages, and the residue of past instability. In short, connection exists, but belonging is fragile; 
 culture is positive in many places, but not yet universally felt; and trust, while improving, still 
 needs to be earned in the details. 
 One pressure point sits under nearly every concern: budget instability. Families see its impact in 
 class sizes, electives, and extracurricular opportunities. Staff feel it in stretched supports, 
 uneven coverage, and the strain of doing more with less. Students notice fewer options and 
 facilities that need attention. Budget is not just a spreadsheet—it's a culture issue. It shapes 
 confidence, communicates what we value, and influences whether people feel the system will 
 deliver on its promises. 
 Even with these challenges, I hear hope. Students want to be seen and heard. Staff describe a 
 shift in tone and are ready to partner. Families value our people and our programs and want to 
 help us protect what matters most. That hope is our opportunity. It asks us to do a few things 
 consistently and well: lead visibly, communicate plainly, stabilize the system, and make 
 belonging non-negotiable for every student in every classroom. 
 In the year ahead, we will focus on what our community told us matters most. We will make 
 belonging measurable and real, tightening our responses to bullying, modeling respect in every 
 adult interaction, and creating space for student voice to meaningfully shape decisions. We will 
 bring radical clarity to the budget: what is funded, what is paused, and why so that staff and 
 families can see the trade-offs we make and trust the process that leads to them. We will 
 strengthen the core of instruction and support, ensuring that “supported and appropriately 
 challenged” is more than a promise; it’s a daily experience in every class. And we will simplify 
===PAGE 43===
 communication so it is predictable, concise, and aligned, with email as our anchor and our 
 presence in schools as the message behind the message. 
 This work will be transparent. We will publish clear goals, timelines, and simple indicators of 
 progress—on climate and belonging, on staffing and stability, on learning and literacy, and on 
 how well we communicate and follow through. We will share what we’re hearing and what we’re 
 doing about it in regular “You said / We did” updates. We will show up at classrooms, 
 assemblies, games, performances, and family forums because visibility is not symbolic; it is how 
 we listen, learn, and lead together. 
 I believe Lunenburg is ready for this moment. Our strengths, our people and our community are 
 exactly what we need to steady the system and lift our students. If we keep our commitments, 
 One ʻOhana will move from a hopeful theme to a daily reality: welcoming all, lifting each other, 
 learning with purpose, and leading with heart. That is how we will rebuild trust, protect what 
 matters most, and ensure that every student in Lunenburg is known, supported, challenged, and 
 proud to belong.
