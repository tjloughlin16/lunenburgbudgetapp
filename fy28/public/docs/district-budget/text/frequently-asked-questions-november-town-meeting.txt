Frequently Asked Questions:  
November Special Town Meeting & District Priorities 
 
Academic Priorities & Class Sizes 
Q: You mentioned our class sizes are "far above optimal." Why aren't we 
using this new funding to hire more general education teachers and reduce 
class sizes? 
A: We share your frustration regarding class sizes. As parents and educators, 
our instinct is always that fewer students per room equals better outcomes. 
However, the math of our current budget reality prevents this. Even if we 
receive the maximum projected state revenue, dividing those funds to hire 
general education teachers would not allow us to reduce class size across all 
elementary grades. 
 
Q: If we can't make classes small, how does hiring Reading Specialists fix 
the problem? 
A: Educational research, including comprehensive meta-analyses by 
researchers like John Hattie, shows that simply reducing a class from 30 
students to 25 does not significantly change student outcomes unless the 
teacher drastically alters their instructional method. However, intensive, 
targeted intervention by a specialized reading teacher has one of the highest 
effect sizes in education—specifically, it is shown to be 8 to 15 times more 
effective at improving literacy than minor class size reductions. By hiring 
specialists, we guarantee that students who are struggling receive dedicated, 
small-group expert instruction that a classroom teacher cannot provide to a 
room of 25+ students.

Q: Why does the proposed funding prioritize the Primary School, THES, and 
the High School? What about the Middle School? 
A: We look at the district holistically to determine where a specific injection of 
funds will prevent the most long-term academic damage. Right now, data 
dictates that early literacy intervention (Primary and THES) is our most urgent 
academic vulnerability. Catching reading gaps early prevents compound 
struggles in middle and high school. At the High School level, restoring 
leadership and scheduling flexibility ensures students can access the credits 
they need to graduate. The Middle School remains a priority, and their 
foundational needs are currently stabilized within our existing operating budget. 
Athletics & Extracurriculars 
Q: The district cut several athletic programs recently. Why isn't this new 
money being used to bring those sports back? 
A: We deeply feel the loss of these programs and know how vital they are to our 
students' physical and mental well-being, as well as our community spirit. 
However, as a public school district, our foundational, statutory mandate is 
academic instruction. When we face severe budget constraints, we must secure 
the academic core—like reading interventions and graduation requirements—
before we can fund extracurriculars. Restoring sports remains a long-term goal, 
but we cannot legally or ethically fund them while critical academic 
interventions remain understaffed. 
Q: You mentioned having over $280,000 in savings for Fiscal Year 2028. 
Why can’t we use that money now to save our sports? 
A: We cannot use those specific funds for sports because it is against state law. 
The funds in question are Massachusetts Special Education Circuit Breaker 
funds. The state strictly mandates that these dollars can only be applied toward 
special education out-of-district tuitions and related mandated services. 
Moving them into the general operating budget to fund athletics, or even 
general education teachers, is legally prohibited.

Q: When will athletic programs be fully restored? 
A: We cannot provide a definitive timeline until we see a structural shift in our 
year-over-year revenue. We will continue to explore grants, community 
partnerships, and alternative funding models, but relying on the district’s 
general operating budget for a full restoration will require a larger, sustained 
increase in district funding beyond this fall's anticipated state revenue. 
Next Steps 
Q: How can parents access the research mentioned regarding reading

A: We believe in complete transparency. Our curriculum office has compiled a 
summary of the data and case studies that guided this decision. You can review 
the literature by contacting the Director of Teaching and Learning, Dr. Sarfde’s 
office directly. 
Q: How can I make my voice heard regarding the November Special Town
