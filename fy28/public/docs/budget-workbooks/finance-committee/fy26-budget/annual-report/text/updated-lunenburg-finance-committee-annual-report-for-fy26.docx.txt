
Lunenburg Finance Committee Annual Report for FY26 
On May 3, 2025, members of the public will gather in the Lunenburg Middle/High School and participate in the centuries-long tradition of New England style town government. As part of this tradition, the Finance Committee has reviewed all monied articles, recommended updates, held public hearings, and has voted on our official recommendations. This warrant contains five budgetary articles that are worth highlighting:
Article 10: Omnibus Budget article that provides most funds for the operations of town (both municipal and schools).
Article 12: One-time, free cash funding to the School Department, breaking from Lunenburg’s traditional practices but potentially necessary to fund essential school services.
Article 11: A Citizens Petition requesting a Proposition 2 ½ override of $2,099,337.
Article 28: A Debt Exclusion article requesting $13.8M in bonds for construction and  revitalization following Master Plan of Marshall Park.
Article 29: A Debt Exclusion article requesting between $21,409,100 and $22,301,145 in bonds to renovate Town Hall into a Meeting House, renovate Ritter Memorial, and add a Town Hall addition onto Ritter Memorial.
The first three articles are related to operational budgets and service levels for the town. This report will summarize the impact on planned operations, and detailed presentations will be given at Town Meeting. The last two articles are debt exclusion articles resulting from years of planning and design work by two commissions.
Town Revenue Projections
Lunenburg’s revenue comes from multiple sources, primarily Property Tax. This year’s budget builds on the FY25 Levy Limit of $31,602,108, including increases from the successful FY25 Proposition 2 ½ override. By law, the levy limit increases automatically by 2.5%, resulting in an increase of $790,052.70. Additionally, the town estimates New Growth of $400,000, leading to a total increase of 3.76%. Previously approved Excluded Debt of $2,213,677 is then applied, resulting in a Maximum Allowable Levy of $35,005,838.
FY2026 Net State Aid is estimated at $10,006,739, based on the Governor's budget, with potential increases pending State legislators negotiations. Chapter 70 Governor’s budget is set at $9,109,185, reflecting ongoing challenges with state aid for education. The Chapter 70 program is the primary source of state aid for funding local public elementary and secondary schools. From FY13-FY23 state aid increased at an annual rate of 5.3%, but in the last three years, it has slowed to 1.5%, increasing the financial burden on the town to raise and appropriate funds. From a Lunenburg resident’s perspective, State Aid is funded through income and sales taxes.

Additional revenue sources include local receipts (Excise Tax, Meals Tax, etc.) and enterprise funds such as Sewer. Notably, Chapter 90 funding for municipal capital improvements has increased from $418,227 to $690,000 due to a five-year state-level bond bill.
Operations
Many Town Meeting articles relate to operational budgets. Lunenburg, like most municipalities in the Commonwealth, faces increased health insurance costs (14.84% increase), stagnant state aid, necessary salary increases to remain competitive, and inflation. As a result, the vast majority of department staffing plans have been maintained, placed on hold, or reduced. The Fire Department and School Department experienced the most significant impacts of these decisions this year, though all departments were affected.
School Department Operations
The School Committee and Superintendent have aligned their budget with the Town Manager’s plan, representing $25,787,474 in the omnibus budget. While grants augment this budget, Town Meeting voters determine omnibus budget funding. This budget necessitates eliminating approximately 28 Full-Time Equivalents (39 employees) at the time of writing this report.
A Citizens Petition article for $2,099,337 aims to restore level funding to FY25 operations. As of this report, the Finance Committee has not received details of this article. The Select Board voted unanimously to recommend disapproval of this article and further voted to not include the Proposition 2 ½ override on the Annual Town Election Warrant. The Finance Committee also voted unanimously to recommend disapproval of this article.  As a Proposition 2 ½ override the article would require passage at Town Meeting and at an Election, therefor no path exists for this article.
If approved, the Tax Rate would increase by approximately $0.89 per $1,000 of property value, translating to an average annual increase of $449 for a single-family household valued at $504,937.  This is a 6.19% increase in property tax.
If the override is rejected, Article 12 proposes a $500,000 Free Cash transfer to the School Department as a temporary measure to delay service cuts. The School Committee has not yet determined how these funds would be allocated, with further details expected at Annual Town Meeting.
Fire Department Operations
The Fire Department currently operates three shifts covering 24/7 town operations, leading to a 48-hour, six-day-per-week schedule. Sundays are covered by call and per-diem staff, with 62% of coverage provided by career firefighters. Of the 197 Massachusetts Fire Departments providing 24/7 coverage, Lunenburg is one of only six operating with three shifts. Most departments use a four-shift model, making Lunenburg less competitive in hiring and retention. The shift expansion plan remains on hold for another year.
Borrowing
The town employs two types of borrowing:
Regular Debt, typically for smaller projects within the Tax Levy Limit.
Excludable Debt, requiring Town Meeting and ballot approval, which does not permanently increase taxes—once the debt is repaid, the tax rate decreases.
According to the Massachusetts Division of Local Services, “Municipal debt is an important tool for city and town governments to finance the purchase of expensive capital assets and to construct and maintain municipally owned infrastructure.  However, debt cannot become overburdensome to either the community’s property tax paying population or to the budget. Capital purchasing and/or improvements should ideally be planned with its priorities analyzed and reviewed by a capital planning committee seeking to coordinate community planning with debt affordability and aiming to present its recommendations to municipal leaders and to the public.”
The town policy states that:
Principal and Interest on Regular Debt remain below 4%.
Excludable Debt remains below 11%.
Combined debt remains below 14%.
This year, Regular Debt decreases by $393,881.14, with these funds being utilized for:
FY26 Capital Program ($244,576)
Operating Budget Offsets ($149,305)
Two debt-funded projects are on the Warrant and Ballot:
Marshall Park improvements ($13.8M via Excludable Debt)
Municipal Building renovations ($21.4M - $22.3M via Excludable Debt)
Marshall Park Improvements ($13.8M via Excludable Debt): Require full funding approval before pursuing grants exceeding $1.5M. The Parks Commission has been encouraged to reapply for prior high-scoring grants such as PARC and Land and Water that scored highly. The Parks Commission was successful in securing Grants in excess of $1.5M prior to the project being put on hold in 2024.   As presented for the Marshall Park project, the Tax Levy would increase by approximately $0.46 per $1,000 on top of the FY26 increase. The average single-family household has a valuation of $504,937 and would result in an increase of $231 per year in taxes. This is a 3.2% increase in property tax.
Municipal Building renovations ($21.4M - $22.3M via Excludable Debt): As presented for the Municipal Building project the Tax Levy would increase by approximately $0.57 per $1,000 on top of the FY26 increase. The average single-family household has a valuation of $504,937 and would result in an increase of $288 per year in taxes. This is a 3.97% increase in property tax.
The estimated tax impact for the Marshall Park and Municipal Building projects will be detailed at Town Meeting.  Both projects combined would remain under the Combined debt policy limit of 14%.  The combined projects would be around the 11% excludable debt policy limit. 
 Summary
This year’s Town Meeting presents critical budgetary decisions for voters. The Finance Committee acknowledges the financial concerns of residents and appreciates the work done by town boards and commissions to prepare budgets and warrant articles. Ultimately, the decisions rest with those who participate in Town Meeting.
The Finance Committee extends gratitude to Town Manager Jennifer Warren-Dyment for her rapid acclimation to Lunenburg’s finances. We also thank Ezequiel Ayala for his financial analysis, as well as Superintendent Dr. Kate Burnham for her service, wishing her well in retirement. We appreciate all department heads, municipal staff, and school employees for their dedication to the town.
