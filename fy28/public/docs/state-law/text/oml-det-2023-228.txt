FACTS

members constitute a quorum.  The Committee also includes three non-voting members.

During a meeting held on August 31, 2022, the Committee unanimously voted to appoint 
the Collins Center to assist in the search for a new Superintendent.  The Committee then  
appointed member Kim Rajdev to work with the Collins Center to “lead the search process” and 
to recommend to the Committee individuals to serve on a Superintendent screening

During an October 13, 2022, meeting, the Committee discussed the search process for a 
new Superintendent with the consultant from the Collins Center.  The search process would 
include the development of a candidate profile and online community survey, as well as the 
creation of a Superintendent screening subcommittee.  The Committee considered the makeup of 
the screening subcommittee and that it should include staff, parents and community members 
and consist of 12 to 14 members.  Individuals who wished to serve on the Committee were asked 
to submit applications by October 30, 2022. 
 
On November 8, 2022, Kim Rajdev separately texted Adam Hogue and Susan Taylor 
“hoping to connect” with each and get their thoughts on candidates for the Superintendent 
screening subcommittee.  Ms. Rajdev explained that her conversation with Ms. Taylor related to 
whether one of the screening subcommittee applicants had a potential financial conflict of 
interest.  After speaking with the applicant, Ms. Rajdev confirmed with Ms. Taylor that there 
was no conflict.  Ms. Rajdev explained that her conversation with Mr. Hogue pertained to the 
total number of screening subcommittee applications received from parents of Lincoln students 
and whether there was an adequate mix of representation.  Ms. Rajdev did not discuss the details 
of either conversation with any other Committee member.  
 
During a November 17, 2022, meeting, the consultant with the Collins Center presented 
the Committee with a Superintendent Profile Report that would be used to guide the 
superintendent search and included desired characteristics, skills, and experience for a new 
Superintendent and issues that the new Superintendent should address in the first year.  Ms. 
Rajdev then reported that 30 individuals submitted applications to serve on the Superintendent 
screening subcommittee, which she narrowed down to 15 people.  The Committee unanimously 
voted to appoint the 15 proposed candidates to serve on the Superintendent screening 
subcommittee.   
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the  
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of  
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement  
that meetings of public bodies be open and accessible to the public.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except 
when meeting in executive session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”).   
 
The Open Meeting Law defines a “meeting,” in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a 
public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The 
law defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including 
electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its

jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of a meeting 
agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other procedural meeting [sic] or the 
distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided than no 
opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” 
is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id.   
 
The complaint alleges that Ms. Rajdev communicated with Committee members outside 
of a properly posted meeting.  In early November 2022, Ms. Rajdev separately reached out to 
two Committee members, Adam Hogue and Susan Taylor, to get their “thoughts” or “input” on 
the Superintendent screening subcommittee.  Ms. Rajdev explained that her conversation with 
Ms. Taylor related to a potential conflict of interest with one of the screening subcommittee 
applicants, while her conversation with Mr. Hogue pertained to the number of applications 
received from parents of students.  Even though Ms. Rajdev spoke with two Committee 
members, and thus appeared to reach a quorum of members, we do not find that the Committee 
engaged in serial communication.  A public body may not engage in a serial communication 
whereby a quorum communicates in a non-contemporaneous manner outside of a meeting on a 
particular subject matter within the public body’s jurisdiction.  See OML 2015-3; OML 2012-84; 
OML 2011-27; McCrea v. Flaherty, 71 Mass. App. Ct. 637 (2008) (holding that private serial 
communications violate the spirit of the Open Meeting Law and may not be used to circumvent 
the intent of the Law).
2  Although the communications between Ms. Rajdev and Mr. Hogue or 
Ms. Taylor related to the Superintendent screening subcommittee, a matter within the jurisdiction 
of the Committee, we find that each conservation that Ms. Rajdev had with Mr. Hogue and Ms. 
Taylor were on two separate issues and the content of these individual conversations were not 
shared or otherwise discussed with a quorum of the Committee.  See OML 2022-137; OML 
2014-84.  As such, because the individual communications were between two members of the 
Committee, a subquorum, each did not constitute deliberation outside of a posted meeting and 
therefore did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-132; OML 2017-199; OML 
2017-69.  
 
We note that any communications Ms. Rajdev may have had with applicants for the 
Superintendent screening subcommittee would not violate the Open Meeting Law.  The Open 
Meeting Law prohibits deliberation of Committee business among a quorum of members outside 
of a properly posted meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The law does not restrict an individual’s 
right to make comments to the general public.  See OML 2017-111.  Therefore, any 
communications between Ms. Rajdev and applicants, who are not members of the Committee, 
and which did not otherwise reach a quorum of the Committee, do not constitute deliberation.  
See OML 2017-199; OML 2017-69; OML 2015-77; OML 2011-52.

notice must include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair

required to list topics on a notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of 
the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1) (b).  We generally consider a 
topic sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and 
understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.  In 
addition, the Open Meeting Law requires public bodies to create and approve meeting minutes, 
whether for open or executive session, in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely 
manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is 
later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 29.11; see OML 
2018-48.  Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next 
meeting.  See OML 2018-67; OML 2017-133.  
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.
