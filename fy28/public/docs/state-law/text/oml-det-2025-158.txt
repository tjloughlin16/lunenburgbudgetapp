This office received a complaint from Derek Dobosz on June 20, 2025, alleging that the 
Chicopee City Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.
1  
The complaint was originally filed with the Council on April 4, and you responded, on behalf of 
the Council, by letter dated May 7, following an extension of time granted by this office.  The 
complaint alleges that the Council posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a meeting held 
on March 31.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Council’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice and open and executive session minutes of the 
Council meeting held on March 31.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a public body shall post 
notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays 
and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice must be printed in a legible, easily

understandable format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a 
list
ing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed.  Id.  The list of topics 
shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific 
when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated 
nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35; OML 2013-39.
2  When reviewing a 
meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a 
meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2022-154; OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, 
we review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently 
specific to make the public aware of the discussion topics.  See OML 2021-22; OML 2020-137; 
OML 2019-64. 
 
The C
ouncil posted notice for a meeting to be held on March 31 at 6:00 P.M.  The notice 
listed six topics, one of which was “Confirmation of the appointment of Karla Bourassa as Chief 
Human Resource Officer.”  During the meeting, the Council heard from residents and city 
employees who voiced support for Ms. Bourassa and urged the Council to confirm her 
appointment as Chief Human Resource Officer.  The Council then convened in executive session 
to discuss a complaint filed against Ms. Bourassa and announced that it would reconvene in open 
session.  After reconvening in open session, the Council discussed the appointment with some 
Councilors explaining why they would confirm the appointment.  Ultimately, the Council 
unanimously voted to confirm the appointment of Karla Bourassa as Chief Human Resource 
Officer.   
 
The complaint alleges that the topic, “Confirmation of appointment of Karla Bourassa as 
Chief Human Resource Officer,” was insufficiently specific where the notice did not include 
details about the proposed salary, contract length, or starting and ending date for the Chief 
Human Resource Officer.
3  A public body need not list each and every detail about a topic, nor 
anticipate on the notice the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion.  See OML 
2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134.  However, notice topics must be described with 
sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting 
so that the public can make an informed decision about whether to attend the meeting in order to 
observe an anticipated discussion.  See OML 2023-86; OML 2019-95; OML 2018-22; OML 
2015-74.  We find here that where the notice informed the public that the Council would be 
discussing the appointment of a Chief Human Resource Officer, and that is in fact what it 
discussed, the notice was sufficient to comply with the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2020-43; 
OML 2015-35; OML 2014-58.  The Open Meeting Law does not require the level of detail 
requested in the complaint.  See OML 2021-181.
