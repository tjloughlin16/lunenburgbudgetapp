 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Castro: 
 
This office received a complaint from Marnie Hoolahan1 on September 10, 2024, alleging 
that the Southborough Select Board (the “Board”), and specifically Board member Al Hamilton, 
violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.2  The complaint was originally filed with 
the Board on or about June 19, and you responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated July 
11.  The complaint alleges that during the Board’s June 18 meeting, Board member Hamilton 
discussed matters that were not included on the meeting notice and improperly sought to 
influence the appointments of individuals to the Community Preservation Committee (“CPC”) 
and Affordable Housing Trust Fund Committee (“AHTFC”). 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law 
complaint, the Board’s response, and the request for further review of the complaint.  We also 
reviewed the notice and minutes of the Board’s June 18 meeting as well as a video recording of 
the June 18 meeting.3

The Open Meeting Law requires that notice be posted of every meeting of a public body.   
G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Meeting notices must include, among other things, a list of the topics that 
the chair, and any other individual authorized to create the notice, reasonably anticipates 48 
hours in advance of the meeting will be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 
OML 2022-206; OML 2020-152.4  The topics for discussion must be listed with “sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 
CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable 
member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public 
body’s discussion.  See OML 2018-7; OML 2015-35.  The public body need not list each and 
every detail about a notice topic, nor anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a 
discussion.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134.  When reviewing a meeting 
notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting 
notice standing alone.  See OML 2015-21; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was 
actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the 
public aware of the nature of the anticipated topics for discussion.  
 
 The complaint alleges that during the Board’s June 14 meeting, the Board, and 
specifically Board member Hamilton, strayed from the posted discussion topics and improperly 
conveyed opinions and concerns regarding the operations of the CPC and its relationship with 
the AHTFC.  After reviewing the video recording of the Board’s June 14 meeting, 5 we find that 
the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged.  The meeting notice included the 
topic “FY25 Committee reappointments and/or organization” and listed 4 committees and 
commissions: Stewardship Committee, Open Space Preservation Commission; Southborough 
Housing Opportunity Partnership Commission, and the CPC.  Separately, the notice included a 
topic pertaining to the Open Space Preservation Commission.  During the meeting, the Board as 
well as representatives of other public bodies and members of the public engaged in an extended 
discussion regarding the several committees and commissions, including whether some should 
merge or be reduced in size as well as what their priorities should be.  Although the focus of the 
discussion was the reappointment of certain individuals to each of the committees and 
commissions, that discussion included comments as to whether Board members believed the 
specific individuals being considered for reappointment as well as the committees and 
commissions had effectively carried out their responsibilities and served their purposes during 
the preceding years.  Where a public body’s discussion naturally flowed from its consideration of 
the posted agenda topic, the notice was sufficient to inform the public of the discussion that 
occurred. See OML 2023-145; OML 2018-14; OML 2014-44; OML 2014-38.  That is precisely 
what occurred here.  Therefore, we do not find that the Board or Mr. Hamilton specifically 
violated the Open Meeting Law by discussing matters that were not properly noticed. 
 
   Although not raised in the complaint, we note that the notice for the Board’s June 14 
meeting should have included the names of the specific individuals who were being considered 
for appointment or re-appointment to each of the committees and commissions listed on the 
notice.  We have consistently explained that to comply with the Open Meeting Law’s

 Finally, we decline to review allegations that Mr. Hamilton exceeded his authority as a 
Board member and attempted to influence the appointment process and the perspectives of 
individuals appointed to certain committees.  The Division of Open Government’s review 
concerns compliance with the Open Meeting Law.  These allegations, even if true, would not 
constitute violations of the Open Meeting Law, and we decline to review them.  See OML 
Declination 1-25-16 (Nahant Housing Authority Board of Commissioners).  We offer no opinion 
as to whether Mr. Hamilton’s comments may have violated any other law or local rule outside of 
the Open Meeting Law. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law as alleged in the complaint.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this 
determination to be resolved. This determination does not address any other complaints that may 
be pending with the Board or with our office.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-
2540 if you have any questions regarding this letter.
