Dear Attorney Lydon: 
 
This office received a complaint from Ronald Beaty on May 12, 2022, alleging that the 
Yarmouth Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on April 20, 2022, and you responded 
to the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated May 12, 2022.
1, 2  The complaint alleges 
that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to include sufficient detail on the 
notices for the Board meetings held on March 29 and April 5.    
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter.  We resolve this 
matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), after reviewing the 
original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review, the notice and minutes of the Board meeting held on March 29, and 
the notices and video recordings of the Board meetings held on April 5, May 3, and May 24.3  
We find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.

On March 24 at 1:59 P.M., the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on March 29 
at 6:00 P.M.  The notice listed ten topics, including “Board and Committee Actions.”  This topic 
heading included further specific item descriptions that advised the public of the topics to be 
discussed including “Committee Appointments.”  However, the notice did not include the names 
of the individuals to be considered for appointment.  During the March 29 meeting, the Board 
voted to appoint James Saben to the Planning Board and to reappoint David Botting, Gail 
Charette and George Warner to the Disability Commission.   
 
 On March 31 at 2:18 P.M., the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on April 5 at 
5:30 P.M.  The notice listed nine topics, including an Executive Session “to consider the 
purchase of real property, and to discuss strategy for collective bargaining.”  During the April 5 
meeting, the Board convened in executive session to discuss strategy with respect to collective 
bargaining and the purchase of real property but did not identify the specific union or property to 
be discussed.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at least 
48 hours prior to such meeting” and that every notice include “the date, time and place of such 
meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the 
meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are required to list topics on a notice with 
“sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a 
reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of 
the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2022-206; OML 2015-35.
4  When reviewing a meeting 
notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting 
notice standing alone.  See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was 
actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the 
public aware of the discussion topics. 
 
Executive session topics must be described, both in the meeting notice and in an 
announcement during open session, in as much detail as possible without compromising the 
purpose for which the executive session was called.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also 
District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] 
precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is 
the only notification given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and 
the only way the public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  When 
convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 3, a public body must identify the collective 
bargaining unit with whom it will be negotiating, if doing so will not compromise the lawful 
purpose for secrecy.  See OML 2022-182; OML 2020-130; OML 2018-139; OML 2016-43; 
OML 2015-87.  When convening an executive session pursuant to Purpose 6, a public body must 
identify the property to be discussed if doing so would not compromise the purpose for which the 
executive session was called.  See OML 2016-5; OML 2014-136; OML 2013-9. 
 
Here, the complaint specifically alleges that the Committee failed to list the names of 
candidates being considered for appointment during the March 29 meeting and failed to identify

With respect to the executive session topic listed on the April 5 meeting notice, the Board 
maintains that identifying the specific union and property it planned to discuss would have 
compromised the Board’s bargaining and negotiating positions.  Nevertheless, in response to the 
complaint, the Board included on the notice for its May 3 meeting and announced in open 
session during its May 3 meeting, the specific collective bargaining unit (firefighters) and 
property (Higgins Crowell Road) that it had discussed in executive session during the April 5 
meeting.  Moreover, during the May 24 meeting, the Board voted in open session to approve the 
contract with the firefighter’s union that it had discussed in executive session. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting a notice with insufficient specificity.  We order the Board’s immediate and future 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and caution that future similar violations may be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
