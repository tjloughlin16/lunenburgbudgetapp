This office received a complaint from David Travis on August 8, 2024, alleging that the 
New Marlborough Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on June 16, 2024, and Chair William 
West responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated June 24, 2024.
1  The complaint alleges 
that the Board posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a meeting held on April 30, and 
created insufficient minutes of the April 30 meeting.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law by 
creating insufficient minutes.  We decline to review the allegation that the Board posted a notice 
with insufficient specificity for the reasons stated below.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review.  We also reviewed the notices and open session 
minutes of the Board meetings held on April 30, May 31, June 3, and June 24.   
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On April 25 at 2:17 P.M., the Board posted notice of a 
meeting to be held on April 30 at 5:00 P.M.  The notice listed 11 topics, one of which was 
“Review and award Highway Bid to resurface various roads-2024.”

The April 30 meeting was held as planned.  During the meeting, the Board discussed bids

DISCUSSION 
 
The complaint alleges that the Board posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a 
meeting held on April 30.  We find this allegation to be untimely and decline to review it.  
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public body 
within 30 days of the alleged violation.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b).  If the alleged violation could not 
reasonably have been known at the time it occurred, then the complaint must be filed within 30 
days of the date it should reasonably have been discovered.  940 CMR 29.05(3).  When an 
alleged violation occurs during an open meeting, the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable 
at the time it occurs.  See OML 2014-85; OML 2012-52.
3   
  
Here, the meeting during which the alleged violation occurred was held in open session 
on April 30.  Complaints alleging violations that take place during an open session can 
reasonably be discovered at the time they occur, and therefore must be filed within 30 days of the 
meeting.  See OML 2012-52; OML Declination 9-30-13 (Boston Zoning Board of Appeal).  That 
an individual chooses not to attend a particular meeting does not change the fact that any 
member of the public could have attended the meeting and learned of the alleged violation on 
that date.  See OML Declination 5-21-12 (Montague Board of Selectmen).  Therefore, because

law for a public body to discuss or vote on a topic without first taking comments from the public.  See OML 2022-

circumstances permit.  See OML 2019-120; OML 2015-12; OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59.

the April 30 meeting occurred entirely in open session, any violation concerning discussions that 
occurred during that meeting could have been discovered at that time, even though the 
complainant might not have personally attended the meeting.  See OML Declination 3-3-14 
(Sterling Board of Selectmen).  Because the complaint was filed with the Board on June 16, 
which was 48 days after the April 30 meeting, we find this allegation to be untimely and we 
decline to review it.  See OML Declination 11-27-23 (Wellfleet Select Board); OML Declination 
11-21-16 (Middleton Planning Board); OML Declination 12-15-14 (Ware Conservation Board).   
 
While we do not review the merits of this allegation, the Board acknowledged that the 
notice should have included the specific roads to be resurfaced.  The Open Meeting Law requires 
that “a public body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, 
excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays” and that notices include “the date, time and 
place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  A public body is required to list topics on a 
notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a 
reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of 
the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.  We remind the Board of its obligations under 
the Open Meeting Law to include sufficient specificity on the notice that provides members of 
the public with information to understand what business will be transacted by the public body 
and the opportunity to attend and observe deliberations in which they are interested.  See OML 
2016-167.  Specifically, where a public body knows the specific roads or addresses that will be 
discussed, such information must be listed on the notice.  See OML 2023-209 (finding the notice 
topic “Road openings” insufficiently specific where the Board discussed three specific addresses 
where the road would be cut open to allow for gas maintenance); OML 2022-76 (finding the 
notice topic “Dublin Road Pole Re-Location Hearing” insufficiently specific where the road to 
be discussed was several miles long with numerous utility poles).   
 
The complaint also alleges that the Board created insufficient minutes of the April 30 
meeting.
4  The Board approved the minutes of the April 30 meeting during its meeting held on 
May 31.  Because the complaint regarding the accuracy of these minutes was filed within 30 
days of the minutes being approved, i.e. filed on June 16, we find this allegation to be timely and 
therefore review it.  See OML 2015-26.   
    
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.  By substantial compliance, we mean that the 
minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not

attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See

Specifically, the complaint alleges that the “minutes lacked clarity . . . to understand what 
‘re-surfacing’ actually meant.”  This office reviews meeting minutes to ensure that they 
accurately reflect the discussions that take place during meetings.  See OML 2020-62.  Because 
the minutes must accurately capture the discussions that actually took place, it is inappropriate to 
include information in meeting minutes that was not discussed at a meeting.  Moreover, the Open 
Meeting Law does not require a public body to discuss any particular matter nor to discuss a 
matter in any particular depth.  See OML 2023-37; OML 2022-231.  Additionally, although 
minutes must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a transcript is not required, and 
the minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion presented.  See OML 2017-104; 
OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55.   The complaint does not allege, nor have we been presented with 
any evidence, that the Board discussed the meaning of or extent of the planned “resurfacing” 
during the April 30 meeting.  Rather, the minutes of the April 30 meeting state that the Board 
discussed bids it received to resurface three roads in town and then awarded a contract to the 
company who submitted the lowest bid.  In the absence of evidence that the Board discussed 
matters that were not summarized in the meeting minutes, we find that the Board did not violate 
the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-50.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
