 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Ms. McCarthy: 
 
This office received a complaint from Alexander Neary on September 8, 2021, alleging 
that the Brookline Preservation Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.1  The complaint was originally filed with the Commission on 
August 2, and you responded, on behalf of the Commission, by letter dated October 7.2  The 
complaint alleges that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely 
respond to a request for minutes of meetings held between January and July 2021, and by failing 
to timely approve those minutes.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law in 
the ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Commission’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting 
further review.  In addition, we reviewed relevant notices and open session minutes from 
Commission meetings held between January 12 and December 20.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On July 20 at 8:40 A.M., the complainant sent an email to 
Alison Steinfeld, Director of Planning and Community Development, requesting copies of 
minutes of Commission meetings held between January and July 2021.  The complainant further 
requested that minutes be posted to the Town of Brookline website.  The Chair of the 
Commission was copied on the email sent to Ms. Steinfeld.  At 8:43 A.M. that same day, Ms. 
Steinfeld forwarded the request to Tina McCarthy, the Preservation Planner, who advised Ms. 
Steinfeld that the majority of Commission minutes only existed in handwritten form. 
 
Two days later, on July 22, the complainant submitted a public records request, via the 
Town of Brookline’s public records portal, requesting certain emails as well as copies of minutes 
of Commission meetings held between January and July 2021.  On August 4, the Town’s Record 
Access Officer provided an initial response to the request explaining that additional time was 
needed to respond.  On August 23, the Town provided records responsive to the request, 
including several sets of meeting minutes.  The Commission did not separately respond to the 
request it had received on July 20. 
 
The Commission held 15 meetings between January 12 and July 20, the date of the 
complainant’s request for minutes.  During a June 24 meeting, the Commission approved 
minutes of the meeting held on January 12.  Then, during four meetings held between September 
29 and November 22, the Commission approved 12 sets of meeting minutes, including minutes 
of meetings held on February 9, March 1, March 9, March 25, July 1, July 13, July 14, July 21, 
August 25, and September 14, September 29, and October 12.  The Commission has posted all 
minutes of meetings held between January and July 2021 to the Town’s website; some of those 
minutes appear to still be in draft form.    
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires public bodies to create and approve meeting minutes, 
whether for open or executive session, in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely 
manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is 
later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 29.11; see OML 
2018-48.  Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next 
meeting.  See OML 2018-67; OML 2017-133.3  Minutes of an open session, if they exist and 
whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 
days.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22 (c). 
 
The complaint alleges that the Commission failed to timely respond to a request for 
meeting minutes.  Here, the complainant sent an email to the Director of Planning and 
Community Development on July 20 requesting copies of Commission meeting minutes under 
the Open Meeting Law.  The Chair of the Commission was copied on this email and the email 
was also forwarded to the Preservation Planner, who works with the Commission.  Separately,

on July 22, the complainant submitted a public records request to the Town’s Records Access 
Officer and requested the same set of meeting minutes.  A request for meeting minutes that is 
directed to a public body will fall under the Open Meeting Law and requires a response in 10 
calendar days.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (g); OML 2019-163; OML 2019-20; OML 2017-50.  
On the other hand, a request for minutes that is directed to a clerk or other municipal employee 
as Records Access Officer will likely fall under the Public Records Law and requires a response 
in 10 business days.  See G.L. c. 66, § 10; OML 2019-126; OML 2019-68.   
 
Because the Chair of the Commission and the Preservation Planner received the July 20 
email, we find that the Commission was on notice of the request for meeting minutes for 
purposes of the Open Meeting Law and therefore had an obligation to respond to that request 
within 10 calendar days.  Compare OML 2019-125 (a request for meeting minutes that is only 
sent to a town’s Records Access Officer without any indication that it was intended for the public 
body nor a request that it be forwarded to the public body will not fall under the Open Meeting 
Law); OML 2019-68 (same).  Because the Commission failed to provide such a response, we 
find that it violated the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-34.  We acknowledge that there was 
some confusion regarding the Commission’s obligations to respond where the complainant filed 
a separate request two days later for the same records to the Records Access Officer, who 
responded to the complainant’s request within 10 days.   
 
The complaint further alleges that the Commission failed to timely approve minutes of 
meetings held between January and July 2021.  We agree and find that the Commission failed to 
timely approve 15 sets of minutes of meetings held during the seven month period of January to 
July 2021 where those minutes were not approved within the later of the next three meetings or 
30 days following each meeting.  See OML 2021-155.  As such, we find that the Commission 
violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to approve these minutes in a timely manner.  We note 
that the Open Meeting Law does not require that public bodies post their minutes to a website or 
that minutes be submitted to the Town Clerk, but rather that meeting minutes be made available 
within 10 days of a request.4  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c); OML 2020-19; OML 2018-107; OML 
2013-103.  The Commission has posted minutes of all meetings held between January and July 
2021 to the town’s website, and therefore, we order no further remedial action. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to timely respond to a request for meeting minutes and by failing to timely 
approve meeting minutes.  We order immediate and future compliance with the law’s 
requirements and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent 
to violate the law.
