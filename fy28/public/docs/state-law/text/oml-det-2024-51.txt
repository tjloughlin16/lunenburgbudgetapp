RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint  
 
Dear Mr. Cappetta:  
 
This office received a complaint from Geoffrey Beach on January 24, 2024, alleging that 
the Acton Planning Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint originally was filed with the Board on October 26.1  You responded on behalf of 
the Board by letter dated November 13.  The complaint alleges that the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law when it failed to create and approve minutes of a meeting it held on July 19, 2022 
in a timely manner.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law as alleged 
but had already taken corrective action by approving the meeting minutes in question before this 
complaint was filed.  We resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 940 
CMR 29.07(1), after reviewing the original complaint, the Board's response to the complaint, the 
Complainant's request for further review, and a further response from the Board. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies “create and maintain accurate minutes 
of all meetings.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  These minutes must be reviewed and approved by the 
public body in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” means “within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can 
show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 29.11; see OML 2018-48.2  Whenever possible,

The Board has not disputed that it failed to approve minutes of its July 19, 2022 meeting 
until the instant complaint was filed fifteen months later.  It attributes the oversight to “staffing 
changes.”  Upon receipt and review of the complaint, the Board approved the subject minutes 
and posted them to the Town’s website on November 8.  Revised minutes of the July 19, 2022 
meeting were posted on December 21.  Based on the undisputed facts, we find that the minutes 
of the July 19, 2022 meeting were not timely approved, in violation of the Open Meeting Law.  
The staffing changes cited by the Board do not excuse the untimeliness of the approval of 
minutes, and the Board does not argue that it does.  See OML 2023-179 (noting that “staffing 
shortages of the type that all governmental bodies with limited resources experience, including 
periodic staff vacancies, do not constitute good cause for failing to approve minutes in a timely 
manner”); OML 2023-117 (same).  We acknowledge that the Board took corrective action by 
approving the meeting minutes in question prior to the complainant’s request for review.  
Therefore, we order no further remedial action.   
 
To the extent that the complainant alleges an intentional violation in his request for 
review, we note that the complaint filed with the public body included no such allegation.  We 
will address only allegations made in an Open Meeting Law complaint filed with the public body 
to give public bodies an opportunity to address those allegations.  See OML 2023-80; OML 
2019-85.  In any event, we decline to find that the violation was intentional.  An intentional 
violation is an “act or omission by a public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of 
[the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An intentional violation may be found where the 
public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the law’s requirements or has previously been 
advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  See also OML 2023-244.  We 
agree that the failure to approve the minutes was, as described by the Board, an “omission.”  It 
was not an intentional act.  Moreover, the Board was not previously warned that its actions ran 
afoul of the Open Meeting Law.
