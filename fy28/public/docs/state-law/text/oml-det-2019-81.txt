Dear Attorney Coughlin:
This office received a complaint from Collin Dias on February 22, 2019, alleging that the 
Fall River Redevelopment Authority Preliminary Screening Committee (the “Committee”) 
violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.1 The complaint was originally filed with 
the chair of the Committee on January 22, and you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the 
Committee’s parent public body, the Fall River Redevelopment Authority, by letter dated March 
7, 2019.2 In his complaint, Mr. Dias alleges four violations of the Open Meeting Law pertaining 
to the Committee’s January 9 meeting: 1) the chair did not state the purpose for the executive 
session, nor whether the public body would reconvene in open session; 2) the vote by the public 
body to go into executive session was inaudible; 3) the Committee’s minutes of the executive 
session are inaccurate; and 4) the Committee’s minutes of the open session are inaccurate.3 Mr. 
Dias subsequently withdrew part 4 of the complaint.

Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when 
someone other than the chair stated the purposes for the executive session, although we find that 
this violation was de minimis. We do not find that the Committee violated the law in the other 
ways alleged in the complaint. In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original 
complaint, the Committee’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review. Additionally, we reviewed the meeting notice and the open and 
executive session minutes of the January 9 meeting and spoke with the complainant and with the 
Committee’s counsel4 by telephone on June 20.
FACTS
We find the facts as follows. The Committee posted notice for a meeting to be held on 
January 9, 2019. The meeting notice included two executive session topics: “Proposed executive 
session to review and approve Executive Session Minutes of December 12, 2018 meeting 
pursuant to M.G.L. c. 30A, 21(a)(7)” and “Proposed executive session to review and respond to 
Open Meeting Law Complaints filed by Collin Dias pursuant to M.G.L. c. 30A, 21(a)(1).”
On January 9, the Committee convened in open session. After approving the open session 
minutes of the December 12, 2018, meeting, the board voted by roll call to enter into executive 
session under Purposes 1 and 7. While in executive session, the board reviewed and approved 
the executive session minutes from December 12, 2018, reviewed Open Meeting Law complaints 
by Collin Dias, and authorized Committee member Kara O’Connell to respond to the Open 
Meeting Law complaints. After the Committee concluded its business in executive session, the 
Board voted by roll call to adjourn.
DISCUSSION
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Commission 
of Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session 
is convened. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21. A public body may enter executive session for 
any of ten enumerated purposes, provided that if first convened in open session, that a majority 
of the members of the body have voted to go into executive session, and that the vote of each 
member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21 (a)-(b); OML 
2019-32; OML 2014-94.
I. The Committee entered executive session for proper purposes, although the Chair
technically should have made the statement of the purposes for the executive sessions.
Before entering executive session, the chair must state the purpose for the executive 
session, stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which 
the executive session was called. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3). Among the ten purposes are

Purpose 1, “[t]o discuss the reputation, character, physical condition or mental health, rather than 
professional competence, of an individual, or to discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or 
complaints or charges brought against, a public officer, employee, staff member or individual”; 
and Purpose 7, “[t]o comply with, or act under the authority of, any general or special law or 
federal grant-in-aid requirements.” G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a)(1), (7).
Here, the meeting notice specified the executive session purposes as reviewing and 
responding to the Open Meeting Law complaints filed by Collin Dias under Purpose 1 and as 
reviewing and approving executive session minutes from December 12, 2018, under Purpose 7. 
We have stated that a public body may enter into executive session under Purpose 1 to discuss an 
Open Meeting Law complaint because it represents a complaint against the public body members 
as individual public officials. See OML 2015-105; OML 2013-82; OML 2012-119; OML 2011- 
6. Additionally, we have stated that public bodies may enter into executive session under 
Purpose 7 to review executive session minutes and that the governing law for that purpose is the 
Open Meeting Law. See OML 2013-199. We also find that in addition to the information 
contained on the meeting notice, the Committee’s legal counsel described in open session the 
topics that would be discussed in executive session with sufficient specificity. Therefore, we 
find that the executive sessions were proper.
Nonetheless, the complaint alleges that the chair did not state the purpose of the 
executive session. The Open Meeting Law specifically provides that the chair must state the 
purposes for executive session. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3) (“before the executive session, the 
chair shall state the purpose for the executive session, stating all subjects that may be revealed 
without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called[.]”). Accordingly, 
we are constrained to find a violation of the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2019-34; OML 
2016-75. We note, however, that it would have been pennissible for the chair to explicitly 
delegate the responsibility for making the required statement to another individual such as legal 
counsel. See OML 2019-34; OML 2016-124 (it is not a violation where the chair specifically 
asked legal counsel to make the statement on his behalf). Where, as here, the statement was 
properly made in open session, but by counsel rather than the chair, the violation 
is de minimis. See OML 2019-34; OML 2013-128; OML 2013-187. We encourage the 
complainant to consider whether filing a complaint about such a violation is an appropriate use 
of the resources of the Committee and our office.
II. The Committee’s vote to enter executive session was audible
The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a public body be open and accessible to 
the public. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a); OML 2017-40; OML 2012-49. Access must include the 
opportunity to be physically present as well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the 
members of the public body. See OML 2017-40; 2017-16; OML 2013-189; OML 2012-66;
OML 2012-48. The complainant alleges that members of the Committee were without 
microphones and mumbling. However, the Committee responds that there was no request at the 
time to speak louder or to repeat what was said, even though the complainant was present at the 
meeting, and that all deliberations were conducted at a normal spoken level. Absent sufficient 
evidence to the contrary, we credit a public body’s account of the facts. See OML 2017-93;
OML 2016-141; See also LaPointe v. License Board of Worcester, 389 Mass. 454 (1983)
(“There is every presumption in favor of the honesty and sufficiency of the motives actuating

public officers in actions ostensibly taken for the general welfare.”). We therefore find no 
violation.
III. The Committee took accurate minutes of the executive session.
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies “shall create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); 940 CMR 29.11(1). Although minutes 
must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a transcript is not required, and the 
minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion presented. See OML 2012-29; OML 
2011-55. When reviewing minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for 
substantial compliance with the accuracy requirement. For substantial compliance, the minutes 
should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not attend the 
meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred. See id. The 
complainant here includes no particular allegations as to how the executive session minutes were 
inaccurate or what topics or information he contends were missing. We find that the meeting 
minutes for the executive session substantially follow the posted notice and are sufficiently 
detailed that a member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and 
understand what occurred in the short executive session. We therefore find no violation.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
when someone other than the chair stated the purposes for the executive session, but not in the 
other ways alleged. We order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s 
requirements and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent 
to violate the law.
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Committee or 
with our office. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you have any 
questions.
