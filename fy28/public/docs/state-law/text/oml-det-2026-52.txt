 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Ms. Vogel: 
 
This office received a complaint from Maureen Lepak on April 1, 2026, alleging that the 
Brookfield Cultural Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Council on February 10, 2026, and you 
responded, on behalf of the Council, by letter on February 18, 2026.  The complaint alleges that 
i) the Council posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a meeting held on February 2, 
2026; ii) the February 2, 2026, meeting was inaccessible; and iii) the Council does not post 
meeting minutes.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Council’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice and minutes of the Council meeting held on 
February 2, 2026.   
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session

is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a 
deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 18.  The law defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any 
medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public 
business within its jurisdiction.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” is 
a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id.  
 
The Council is a ten-member public body; thus, six members constitute a quorum.  The 
Council posted notice for a meeting to be held on February 2, 2026.  Although the February 2, 
2026, meeting was held as planned, only three members of the Council attended the meeting.  
Because only three members of the Council, a subquorum, attended and participated in the 
meeting, the Council did not hold a “meeting” for the purposes of the Open Meeting Law.  See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 18; OML 2016-99; OML 2013-106.1  Where the Council did not hold a meeting 
subject to the Open Meeting Law, it could not violate the Open Meeting Law.  We offer no 
opinion on whether the actions taken by the Council could be a violation of some other law or 
regulation outside the scope of the Division’s review.  See OML Declination 7-23-18 (Norwell 
Board of Selectmen).    
 
Although we do not find a violation, we take seriously the allegations raised in the 
complaint and remind the Council that the Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an 
emergency, “a public body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such 
meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice 
must be printed in a legible, easily understandable format and must contain the date, time and 
place of the meeting, as well as a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed.  Id.  The list of topics shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public 
of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a 
topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and 
understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35; OML 
2013-39.  While a public body may publish a general notice to reserve a meeting date, it must 
update the notice 48 hours (excluding weekends and holidays) or more prior to the meeting to 
include a sufficiently specific list of topics that the Chair reasonably expects to discuss.  See G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(b), OML 2022-173. 
 
In addition, we remind the Council that the Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of 
a public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive 
session is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  Access to a meeting generally must 
include the opportunity to be physically present, as well as to see and hear what is being 
discussed by the members of the public body.  See OML 2015-38; OML 2014-3; OML 2013-
189; OML 2012-66.  In order for a meeting to be truly “open to the public,” the meeting venue 
must be accessible to the public for the duration of the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a); OML 
2023-99; OML 2017-83.  Ideally, doors necessary to access meeting locations should not be 
locked.  See, e.g., OML 2023-99 (finding public body in violation of the Open Meeting Law 
when doors to City Hall were locked intermittently during meeting); OML 2022- 88 (finding 
public body in violation of the Open Meeting Law when the accessible entrance into the building

was locked, requiring use of a security buzzer which was not answered); OML 2014-118 
(finding that a locked door and a “closed” sign served as a deterrent to attending a meeting, in 
violation of the Open Meeting Law).  However, we have found that a meeting in a locked 
building is sufficiently accessible where members of the public can promptly access the building 
by notifying personnel or knocking on a window.  See OML 2011-52; see also OML 2017-172 
(finding that a meeting location with a locked door was sufficiently accessible where a note was 
posted on the door with instructions for how to gain entrance to the meeting). 
 
Finally, the Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Meeting minutes 
must include the date, time and place of the meeting, the members present or absent, a summary 
of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the 
decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all votes.  Id. 
When reviewing minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial 
compliance with the accuracy requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.  By substantial 
compliance, we mean that the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a 
member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear 
understanding of what occurred.  See OML 2012-106.  The Open Meeting Law requires that 
meeting minutes include more than a statement that a public body held a discussion about a 
specified topic; the Law requires that the minutes summarize the substance of the public body’s 
discussion.  See OML 2020-53; OML 2019-167; OML 2018-8.   
 
Moreover, minutes of open session meetings must be created and approved in a timely 
manner, G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c), which the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations 
define as being “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, 
unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 29.11(2).  However, 
the Open Law Meeting does not require that public bodies post their minutes to a website or 
elsewhere, but only that minutes be created, maintained, and approved in a timely manner, and 
made available within 10 days of a request.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(a), (c); OML 2020-78; OML 
2017-98.   
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Council.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
