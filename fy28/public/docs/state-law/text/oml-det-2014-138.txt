This office received a complaint from Mr. Eric Bibler, dated May 13, 2014, alleging that 
the Cape & Vineyard Electric Cooperative ("CVEC") violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. 
c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with CVEC on March 15, 2014, and CVEC 
responded by letter dated March 28, 2014. Mr. Bibler's complaint enumerates a number of 
alleged violations of the Open Meeting Law concerning 37 separate meetings held between April 
2008 and January 2014.1 Chief among these is the allegation that CVEC, "[f|ailed to name the 
topics of discussion, or to provide adequate specificity regarding the topics of discussion, for 
executive sessions." We address this issue, and others raised by the complaint, herein. 
Following our review, we find that CVEC violated the Open Meeting Law in certain 
respects. However, we find that CVEC stated the purposes for the executive sessions at issue 
with sufficient specificity. In reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaint; 
CVEC's response; and selected executive session meeting minutes from the period of August 
2010 to present.2

FACTS 
We find the facts as follows. CVEC was formed out of a strategic planning process 
commissioned and undertaken by the Cape Light Compact ("CLC").3 See G.L. c. 164, §136. 
CVEC was organized in 2007 with three members: CLC, Barnstable County, and the Town of 
Barnstable. Currently, CVEC has 19 members consisting of 16 towns, Barnstable and Dukes 
County, and the CLC. CVEC's long-term goal is to develop electric generation projects or 
contracts to stabilize electric rates for all CLC customers. The CLC does not have the authority 
to develop electric generation projects. The CVEC Board of Directors is a public body subject to 
the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2013-73; OML 2012-81. 
On February 5, 2014, the complainant e-mailed a request to CVEC seeking: (1) the 
publication of any executive session minutes approved for release by the CVEC board during its 
meeting held on January 30, 2014; and (2) a review by the CVEC board of all unpublished 
executive session minutes and the release of such minutes if the executive session purpose no 
longer required confidentiality. In a reply dated February 14, 2014, John C. Checklick, President 
of CVEC, stated that no executive session minutes had been approved for release during the 
January 30, 2014 meeting, but that CVEC would review and publish executive session minutes 
that were deemed to no longer require confidentiality. 
On March 6, 2014, CVEC posted to its website numerous previously unreleased 
executive session minutes, with dates ranging from April 2008 to December 2012. In addition, 
previously-redacted minutes were replaced with full versions of minutes for dates ranging from 
November 2008 to December 2010. The complaint alleges that these minutes show "a pattern of 
failure" and "a systematic effort to deprive the public of access to information to which the 
public is entitled." 4 
DISCUSSION 
A. The Complaint's Main Allegation 
I. CVEC Stated the Purposes for the Executive Sessions at Issue with Sufficient 
Specificity. 
While public bodies are generally required to hold meetings that are open and accessible 
to the public, such bodies are, in certain circumstances, permitted to enter executive sessions in 
order to deliberate outside of public view. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a). Before entering an executive 
session, "the chair shall state the purpose for the executive session, stating all subjects that may 
be revealed without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called." G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21(b)(3) (emphasis added).

One executive session purpose applies to a limited number of public bodies that act as 
municipal energy aggregators. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(10) (Purpose 10). Purpose 10 allows 
such public bodies to discuss trade secrets or confidential, competitively-sensitive or other 
proprietary information when the public body determines that such disclosure will adversely 
affect its ability to conduct business in relation to other entities making, selling or distributing 
electric power and energy. See G.L. c. 3OA, § 21(a)(10); OML 2012-81. The discretion 
afforded to public bodies is not unlimited, as public bodies must be able to demonstrate that the 
information being discussed is a trade secret or competitively sensitive information. See OML 
2014-24. However, the law provides this type of public body wide latitude to determine what 
information may be discussed outside of the public's view. See OML 2012-81. 
At issue is how much detail CVEC is required to disclose when invoking Purpose 10 to 
enter into executive session. Mr. Bibler describes this as his "primary complaint" against CVEC. 
A typical example of CVEC's open meeting minutes reads as follows: 
[Chair] stated the need for an Executive Session to discuss [general name of topic] having 
declared that an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on CVEC's ability to conduct 
business in relation to other entities making, selling or distributing electric power and energy; 
and that the consideration of the purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property will have 
a detrimental effect on CVEC's negotiating position as a public body. He also stated the 
Executive Committee would not reconvene in open session this day at the conclusion of the 
Executive Session. [Board member] moved the Committee vote to enter into Executive 
Session pursuant to MGL Ch. 3OA § 21 (a) (10) to conduct a [general name of topic] 
discussion containing confidential and competitively sensitive information, seconded by 
[Board member] and voted by roll call unanimously in favor.5 
We find that, in the instances at issue in this complaint, a description of this type was 
sufficient to meet the standards of the Open Meeting Law. For example, in the January 31, 2013 
minutes, CVEC cited "Phase II-PV procurement strategy" as the topic to be discussed in a 
Purpose 10 executive session.6 The complaint argues that such descriptions are "broad and 
virtually meaningless." After our review, we find that these meetings were used to discuss trade 
secrets, confidential information, and other competitively-sensitive information related to 
CVEC's activities as an energy supplier. Furthermore, we find that this description truthfully 
encapsulates the discussion that took place during the executive session. 
Another example is illustrative: The complaint points to the April 18, 2013 meeting 
where CVEC cited as the purpose for its executive session, "longer range confidential and 
competitively sensitive energy projects and long-term contract opportunities, financing structures 
and organizational objectives."7 As the complaint correctly states, many, if not all, of CVEC's

B. Other Allegations 
II. CVEC Failed to Periodically Review Executive Session Minutes and Failed to 
Respond to the Complainant's Request for Executive Session Minutes within the 
Timeframe Required by the Open Meeting Law. 
When the purpose for which a valid executive session was held has been served, the 
minutes, preparatory materials, and documents and exhibits of the session shall be disclosed 
unless the attorney-client privilege or some other exemption applies. G.L. c. 3OA, § 22(f). 
Public bodies have an obligation to review the minutes of executive sessions at reasonable 
intervals to determine if continued non-disclosure of minutes is warranted and to announce that 
determination at the next meeting following its review. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1). The burden of 
justifying continued nondisclosure of executive session minutes lies with the public body. Foudy 
v. Amherst-Pelham Regional School Committee, 402 Mass. 179, 184 (1988). Furthermore, a 
public body must respond to a request to inspect or copy executive minutes within 10 days of the 
request and promptly release the minutes if they are subject to disclosure. G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 22(g)(2). If the body has not performed a review to determine whether the minutes may be 
disclosed, it must review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no later than its next meeting or 
within 30 days, whichever is sooner. Id. 
Here, the complainant made his request for CVEC's meeting minutes on February 5, 
2014. CVEC held a meeting on February 27, 2014, at which the request for minutes was 
discussed. The minutes were released on March 5, 2014, six days after the meeting. While we 
commend CVEC for reviewing its executive session minutes at the next meeting, as is required 
by law, we find that CVEC violated the Open Meeting Law by waiting six days before releasing 
the minutes that it determined could be released during the February 27, 2014 meeting. See G.L. 
c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). To be in full compliance, CVEC should have released the minutes 
immediately after the meeting or, if that was not feasible given the time of day, on the next 
business day.

Furthermore, the fact that CVEC waited until it received Mr. Bibler's Open Meeting Law 
complaint before initiating a review of executive session minutes that were, in some cases, nearly 
four years old, suggests that CVEC was not periodically reviewing executive session minutes. 
See OML 2014-105. The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies review executive 
session minutes at regular intervals in order to release minutes for which the executive session 
purpose has expired in a timely fashion. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f). We have spoken to CVEC's 
counsel about the periodic review requirement, and CVEC has committed to adhering to this 
requirement in the future. 
III. On Certain Occasions, CVEC Failed to Comply with Appropriate Procedure when 
Entering into Executive Session. 
A public body must first convene in open session before entering into executive session. 
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b); OML 2013-10. Before entering into executive session, the chair of a 
public body must publicly announce whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion 
of the executive session. G.L. c. 30A,§ 21(b)(4); OML 2014-69. The requirements that a body 
convene in open session, state the purpose for the executive session, and conduct a roll call vote 
to enter executive session are intended to protect the right of the public to know when a body is 
discussing matters behind closed doors and the reasons its members believe a private discussion 
is warranted. See OML 2012-10. 
The minutes for CVEC's March 30, 2012 meeting fail to record that CVEC met in open 
session before convening in executive session. Additionally, on April 21, 2011, CVEC entered 
into executive session without indication as to whether it would resume in open session 
following the closed discussion. These failures constitute violations of the Open Meeting Law. 
We note that these deficiencies took place years ago, before CVEC had the benefit of our 
subsequent determinations. See OML 2013-77; OML 2013-73; OML 2012-82; OML 2012-81. 
The recent minutes posted to the CVEC website suggest the body has been more attentive to the 
requirements of the Open Meeting Law since receiving those decisions. We therefore do not 
find this violation to be intentional. 
IV. CVEC Specified all Reasons for Entering Executive Session on April 5. 2013. 
On April 5, 2013, CVEC stated that it was entering executive session to discuss two 
topics, "Approval of executive session minutes" and "Round I and II PV Procurement Strategy 
Discussion." The complaint alleges, instead, that four topics were discussed: (i) Approval of 
Executive Session Minutes; (ii) Round I and II PV Procurement Strategy Discussion; (iii) Cape 
Cod Commission DRI Determination; and (iv) Round II PV Procurement. The complaint also 
alleges that there were assorted other discussion topics under no general heading. We find that 
these allegedly unlisted discussion topics were actually subtopics of the "Round I and Round II 
PV Procurement Strategy Discussion" cited in the open session minutes. Accordingly, we find 
the notice was sufficient and there was no violation. 
V. CVEC Did Not Violate the Open Meeting Law by Deliberating Outside of a Noticed 
Meeting on August 19, 2010. 
The minutes for a CVEC meeting held on August 19, 2010 state that the members of the 
Executive Committee "met this morning on Cape Wind." There was no other set of minutes

posted for August 19, 2010, suggesting that the CVEC Executive Committee had failed to 
convene a properly noticed meeting to discuss Cape Wind. The complaint alleges that this 
violated the Open Meeting Law. 
After conversation with CVEC's legal counsel, we find that an administrative error 
occurred in the labeling of these meeting minutes and that a second set of minutes did actually 
exist. The minutes posted on the CVEC website under the title "2010-08-19 Executive 
Committee Executive Session Minutes.pdf' are actually the minutes from a meeting of the full 
CVEC Board, as opposed to the Executive Committee. The full CVEC Board was discussing a 
separate meeting of the Executive Committee which had taken place earlier in the day. CVEC's 
legal counsel provided us with a copy of the true Executive Committee meeting minutes from the 
morning of August 19, 2010, and both sets of minutes from this date are now posted on the 
CVEC website. These minutes show that the topic of Cape Wind was discussed and, ultimately, 
no action was taken on the subject. Accordingly, this appears to have been merely an 
administrative error, and it appears that both the Executive Committee and the full CVEC Board 
complied with the minutes requirements of the Open Meeting Law, we find no violation with 
respect to this issue. 
For the reasons stated above, we find that CVEC violated the Open Meeting Law in 
certain respects. We order immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and 
caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of an intentional violation of 
the law.
