RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint — Rowe School Committee 
Dear Chair Miller: 
This office received nine complaints from Lisa Danek-Burke, between February 24, 2012 
and May 21, 2012, alleging that the Rowe School Committee (the "Committee") violated the 
Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaints allege that the Committee 
deliberated on "public business outside of an open public meeting through written 
communication by electronic mail among a quorum of the Rowe School Committee." The 
complaints were filed with the Committee on or about December 22, 2011; January 18, 2012 
(two complaints); February 8, 2012; February 13, 2012; February 15, 2012; February 16, 2012; 
February 17, 2012; and May 16, 2012. The Committee responded to the complaints by letters 
dated February 4, 2012; March 23, 2012; and August 2, 2012. 
Following our review, we find that former Committee Chair William Loomis 1  
individually violated the Open Meeting Law by sending emails to a quorum of the Committee 
regarding public business within its jurisdiction. We do not believe that the violations were 
intentional, however, and we offer this determination as guidance for future conduct. In reaching 
this determination, we reviewed the nine complaints filed with the Committee; the Committee's 
February 4, 2012; March 23, 2012; and August 2, 2012 responses; and the complaints filed with 
our office between February 24, 2012 and May 21, 2012. We also reviewed emails, provided by 
the complainant, which were sent by Chair Loomis and dated December 2, 2011; December 22, 
2011; January 12, 2012; January 17, 2012; January 18, 2012; January 25, 2012; and April 23, 
2012.

FACTS  
At the time these complaints were filed, the Committee consisted of three members: 
Chair William Loomis, Lisa Miller,2  and Lisa Danek-Burke, the complainant. The nine 
complaints each followed the sending of an email by Chair Loomis to various individuals. In 
eight of the nine emails, Chair Loomis copied the two other Committee members. The emails 
were sent on December 2, 2011; December 22, 2011 (two emails); January 12, 2012; January 17, 
2012; January 18, 2012; January 25, 2012 (two emails); and April 23, 2012. Although the emails 
cover a variety of topics, each contained a communication to a quorum of the Committee about 
Committee business. Two representative emails are described below. 
On December 2, 2011, Chair Loomis sent an email to a number of individuals, including 
Committee members Lisa Miller and Lisa Danek-Burke. Chair Loomis wrote, in part, that: 
As of now I don't see any reason of [sic] Rowe entering the district for our 7-12 students, 
which I stated at our last Rowe School Committee Meeting. I am speaking for myself not 
the committee when I say that, 'the Mohawk School Committee shouldn't be wasting too 
much of an effort on this issue at this time.' 
No members of the Committee responded to this email. 
On January 25, 2012, Chair Loomis sent an email to Committee members Lisa Miller and 
Lisa Danek-Burke, as well as to three school officials. Chair Loomis wrote: 
Attached is my revised FY13 budget questions. I have removed most non question 
statements and added my questions concerning Shared Expenses. As I stated in the 
attachment I have some more areas to review and will forward additional questions. 
Three District Agreement questions as they pertain to the FY13 budget will follow under 
another e-mail. 
Attached to the email was an eight-page document, presumably created by Chair Loomis, 
which included budget line items and his questions about those items. No members of the 
Committee responded to this email. 
On April 23, 2012, Chair Loomis sent an email to a number of individuals, but did not 
include the other members of the Committee. In that email, Chair Loomis provided his opinion 
on budget cuts. He then wrote, "Please forward to the other two school committee members. If I 
send it I will have to respond to my ninths [sic] or tenth Open Meeting violation." 
DISCUSSION 
The Open Meeting Law requires that "all meetings of a public body shall be open to the 
public," subject to a narrow list of exceptions that can be conducted in executive session. G.L. c. 
30A, § 20(a). The law's purpose is "to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding deliberations 
and decisions on which public policy is based." Ghiglione v. School Committee of Southbridge,

376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The law defines deliberation as "an oral or written communication 
through any medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on 
any public business within its jurisdiction; provided, however, that 'deliberation' shall not 
include the distribution of a meeting agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other 
procedural meeting or the distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a 
meeting, provided that no opinion of a member is expressed." G.L. c. 30A, § 18. 
Eight of the emails we reviewed were either sent by the Chair directly to a quorum of the 
Committee, or were sent to other individuals but copied the Committee members. All of these 
emails discussed public business within the jurisdiction of the Committee. This constituted 
deliberation. See G.L. c. 30A, § 18. In the December 2, 2011 email, for example, the Chair 
expressed his opinion regarding Rowe entering a regional school district. This was a matter 
within the jurisdiction of the Committee. The Chair therefore should not have copied the other 
two Committee members on the email. If he wished to convey the information therein to the 
other Committee members, he should have presented that information to them during an open 
meeting. 
In his January 25, 2012 email, the Chair sent the other Committee members a document 
containing his questions and opinions about a document under consideration by the Committee. 
A member of a public body may distribute a document for discussion at a meeting via email, so 
long as the document does not contain the opinion of a member of the public body. G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 18. Here, the attached document included the Chair's opinions. This email therefore 
constituted an impermissible deliberation outside of a meeting. 
The Open Meeting Law requires that the Attorney General determine "whether the public 
body, 1 or more of the members, or both, are responsible" for a violation. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c). 
Here, Chair Loomis' communications violated the Open Meeting Law's express prohibition on 
communication to a quorum about matters within the body's jurisdiction outside of a meeting. 
See id. § 18. Although Chair Loomis' communications could have resulted in further 
deliberation between or among members of the Committee outside of a meeting, the other 
members of the Committee demonstrated appropriate restraint in declining to respond. Because 
no other dialogue or exchange of opinions resulted between or among a quorum of the 
Committee, the Committee as a whole did not violate the Open Meeting Law. Therefore, we 
find an individual violation only. See OML 2011-37; OML 2011-35. 3  
As a final matter, we note for the Committee's future reference that serial 
communications may also constitute improper deliberation in violation of the Open Meeting 
Law. In his April 23, 2012 email to non-Committee members, Chair Loomis provided his 
opinion on budget cuts, and then wrote, "Please forward to the other two school committee 
members. If I send it I will have to respond to my ninths [sic] or tenth Open Meeting violation." 
This communication violated the Open Meeting Law because, by requesting that his opinions be 
forwarded to the other Committee members, the Chair was attempting to facilitate 
communication between and among a quorum of the Committee on public business within the 
Committee's jurisdiction. $ee G.L. c. 30A, § 18. Because it appears that no other members of 
the Committee responded to this email, we find this was also an individual violation of the Open

Meeting Law. While it appears that, at the time Chair Loomis sent this email, he understood that 
direct communication between and among a quorum of the Committee regarding public business 
within the Committee's jurisdiction was a violation of the law, he seems to have been unaware 
that indirect, serial communications also violated the law. We therefore do not find this to be an 
intentional violation, but we caution the Committee against similar activity in the future. 
CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that former Chair William Loomis violated the 
Open Meeting Law by deliberating over email. We order the Committee's immediate and future 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and caution that future similar violations may be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law. Furthermore, we order the Committee to 
publicly disclose the nine emails discussed in this letter at the Committee's next meeting. The 
Commission may redact portions or withhold the emails subject to the exemptions to the Public 
Records Law contained within clause twenty-sixth of G.L. c. 4, § 7, or where discussions may be 
protected by the attorney/client privilege. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f). 
We now consider this matter closed. This determination does not address any other 
complaints which may be pending with our office or the Committee. Please contact me if you 
have any questions regarding this letter.
