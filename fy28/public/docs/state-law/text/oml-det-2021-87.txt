 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Mejia: 
 
This office received a complaint from Steven Fitsgerald on May 19, 2021, alleging that 
the Everett City Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.1  The complaint was originally filed with the Council on April 19, and you responded, on 
behalf of the Council, by letter dated May 3.  The complaint alleges that during its April 12 
meeting, which all participating City Council members attended remotely, the Council did not 
conduct all votes by roll call. 
  
We resolve this matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Council’s response to the complaint, the complaint 
filed with our office requesting further review, and minutes of the Council’s April 12 meeting. 
We also corresponded with you by email.2   
 
On March 10, Governor Charles Baker declared a state of emergency in response to the 
public health emergency created by the outbreak of the 2019 novel Coronavirus (“COVID-19”).  
See Exec. Order No. 591 (Mar. 10, 2020).  On March 12, Governor Baker issued an executive 
order temporarily suspending certain provisions of the Open Meeting Law during the state of 
emergency.  See Order Suspending Certain Provisions of the Open Meeting Law, G.L.  c. 30A, 
§ 20 (Mar. 12, 2020) (the “Executive Order”).  The Executive Order temporarily suspends the 
requirements under the Open Meeting Law and the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law 
regulations that a quorum of a public body, including the chair, be physically present at the

meeting location and allows all members of a public body to participate in a meeting remotely.  
All other provisions of the law and regulations regarding remote participation remain in effect, 
including the requirement that “[a]ll votes taken during any meeting in which a member 
participates remotely must be by roll call vote.”  940 CMR 29.10(7)(c); see also OML 2021-9. 3   
 
On April 12, the Council held a meeting which all participating Council members 
attended remotely.  Although some votes taken during the meeting were conducted by roll call, 
many were not.  The Council explains that all votes that had a “binding effect,” such as a vote on 
a motion to approve the transfer of funds, a vote to ordain a measure to become an ordinance, or 
an approval of a mayoral appointment, were conducted by roll call.  Other votes, such as to refer 
an item to a subcommittee or to invite a speaker to speak before the Council, were conducted by 
visual affirmation, such as a “thumbs up.”  Although we acknowledge that votes on substantive 
matters were conducted by roll call, as required, the Attorney General’s Regulations require that 
“all votes taken during any meeting in which a member participates remotely must be by roll call 
vote.”  940 CMR 29.10(7)(c); see OML 2015-145, OML 2021-9.  While we recognize that 
members of the public who were viewing video of the meeting may have been able to discern 
how each Council member voted in each instance, the law requires that the Council conduct its 
votes by roll call when a member participates remotely, even where the result of a vote is 
unanimous or otherwise clear.  See OML 2021-9; OML 2016-132; OML 2015-131.  
Accordingly, because the Council failed to conduct some votes by roll call during the April 12 
meeting, we find that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law.  We order the Council’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law’s requirements, and we caution 
that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Council.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
