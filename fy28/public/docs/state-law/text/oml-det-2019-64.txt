This office received a complaint from Allen Waller on February 13 alleging that the 
Marblehead Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.1 The complaint was originally filed with the Board on January 14, and you responded, on 
behalf of the Board, by letter dated January 24.2 The complaint alleges that the notice for the 
Board’s January 15 meeting was not sufficiently specific because “the exemptions provided for 
executive session have been incorrectly applied.”
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged. In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s 
response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further review. We 
also reviewed the notices and open and executive session minutes of the Board’s and Board of 
Health’s joint meeting held on January 15. Finally, we spoke with Attorney Sarah Bellino,
Board legal counsel, by telephone on April 18.

DISCUSSION
A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not 
including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Notices must include “a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” Id. The 
list of topics shall have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting. 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). Executive session topics must be described, 
both in the meeting notice and in an announcement during open session, in as much detail as 
possible without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called. See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm, of Wavland, 
455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for convening in executive 
session is necessary ... because that is the only notification given the public that a [public body] 
would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the reason for 
doing so was proper or improper”).
The complaint alleges that the notice for the Board’s January 15 meeting was not 
sufficiently specific. When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly 
insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone. See OML 2015-109; 
OML 2014-155.3 Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if 
the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the public aware of the discussion topics. Here, the 
notice listed two executive session topics the chair reasonably anticipated the Board would 
discuss; those topics included the specific case name of the litigation matter it would discuss

under Purpose 3 and the two Open Meeting Law complaints it would discuss under Purpose 1 
including the name of the individual who filed the complaint. We find that, on its face, the 
notice for the January 15 meeting met the statutory requirements. Moreover, we note that the 
Board’s January 15 meeting had not yet occurred when the complaint was filed on January 14. 
We encourage the complainant, in the future, to reach out to the Board if he believes a notice 
may lack sufficient detail, rather than to file an Open Meeting Law complaint prior to the 
meeting. In this instance, where the notice was posted six days ahead of the meeting, there 
would have been ample time for the Board to revise and repost the notice, if indeed the notice 
was insufficiently detailed.
Next, we find that the discussions in executive session fell within a proper statutory 
purpose. A public body may convene in executive session to discuss strategy with respect to 
litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating 
position of the public body and the chair so declares. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”). 
This purpose offers the narrow opportunity to discuss strategy with respect to litigation that is 
pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise demonstrably likely. See Powers v. 
Freetown-Lakeville Reg'l Sch. Dist. Comm.. 392 Mass. 656, 659 (1984); Doherty v. School 
Commission of Boston. 386 Mass. 643, 648 (1982). Here, litigation is pending between the town 
and Maverick Construction and the Chair declared that public discussion of the litigation would 
compromise the litigating position of the Board; thus, discussions of litigation strategy were 
appropriate for executive session. Finally, our office has unequivocally stated in past 
determinations that public bodies may meet in executive session under Purpose 1 to review Open 
Meeting Law complaints against the body. See OML 2015-105; OML 2013-82; OML 2012-119; 
OML 2011-6. The individual filing a complaint does not have a right to be present in a Purpose 
1 executive session but may attend at the discretion of the public body. See OML 2013-50.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law. We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
