This office received a complaint from Jane Snow on February 3, 2025, alleging that the 
Newburyport City Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.  The complaint originally was filed with the Council on December 27, 2024.  Upon follow-
up from our office, you responded on behalf of the Council on March 3, 2025.  The complaint 
alleges that the Council (1) met in executive session on April 25, 2022, without a lawful purpose; 
and (2) improperly asserted the attorney-client privilege in response to a request for executive 
session minutes. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law when it 
met in executive session without a lawful statutory purpose.  We decline to review the allegation 
that the Council improperly asserted the attorney-client privilege in response to a request for 
executive session minutes.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the complaint and the 
complainant’s request for further review.  We reviewed the minutes of the open session of the 
meeting of April 25, 2022 (“the April 25 Meeting”).  We also reviewed, in camera, the minutes 
of the executive session of the April 25 Meeting, which have been released to the public with 
redactions.  We reviewed an article in the Daily News of Newburyport
1 and a title report 
authored by Edward Rainen, Esq.  Finally, we communicated via email with you and with the 
complainant.

FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  As it exists on the ground, Coffin Street is a dead-end street 
in the City of Newburyport.  Coffin Street continues beyond its terminus as an unconstructed 
way, or “paper street,” connecting to Jefferson Street.  The complainant lives toward the end of 
the street; motorists sometimes use her driveway as a turnaround.  In 2021, the complainant 
asked the City Council’s Public Safety Committee to revisit a 2002 proposal to extend the 
construction of Coffin Street to Jefferson Street, thereby providing an outlet on Ms. Snow’s end 
of Coffin Street.  The City hired an attorney, Edward Rainey, to conduct a title search.  Attorney 
Rainey issued his report on December 1, 2021.  Based upon his research, he concluded that 
“Coffin Street, south of Jefferson Street, is a private, partially unconstructed way, to which the 
fee title and easement use rights have not been abandoned by the abutters.”  He opined that the 
Council’s attempts, starting in 1972, to finalize the layout and taking of Coffin Street as a public 
way, have not fully complied with relevant statutes and that therefore, Coffin Street remains 
private. 
 
 Notice of the April 25 Meeting included the following topic:

After the April 25 meeting, the complainant made multiple requests for the minutes of the

executive session of the April 25 Meeting. 
 
DISCUSSION 
   
I. The complaint was timely filed. 
 
The Council has not responded substantively to Ms. Snow’s allegations, as it maintains 
that the complaint was untimely and therefore does not require a response.  Pursuant to the Open

Meeting Law, a complaint must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged 
violation, or, if the alleged violation could not have been known at the time it occurred, then the 
complaint must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the date on which the alleged 
violation could reasonably have been discovered.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(4).  
Complaints alleging violations that take place during an open session can reasonably be 
discovered at the time they occur.  See OML Declination 11-27-2023 (Wellfleet Selectboard); 
OML 2022-128; OML 2012-52.3  However, an alleged violation regarding the propriety of an 
executive session often will not be reasonably discoverable until the content of the discussions is 
divulged, in which case a complaint must be filed within 30 days of the date that the substance of 
the executive session discussions was reasonably discoverable.  See OML 2016-93 (complaint 
alleging improper purpose filed four years after executive session but less than 30 days after 
release of executive session minutes was timely); 2024-204; 2018-125; OML 2015-123.  
 
At the time that the complaint was filed, the minutes of the executive session of the April 
25 Meeting had not yet been released.  Ms. Snow alleges that the council convened the executive 
session for an improper purpose.  In this instance, we are satisfied that the violation that is 
alleged could not be reasonably discoverable by the public until minutes of the executive session 
were publicly released.  Because the complaint was filed prior to release of the executive session 
minutes, it was timely. 
 
II. The Council violated the Open Meeting Law when it invoked Purpose 6 to 
convene in executive session on April 25, 2022. 
 
All meetings of a public body must be conducted in an open session unless a closed-door 
executive session is convened for one of ten permissible purposes.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  
When meeting in executive session, a public body may only address subjects related to the 
narrow executive session purpose the public body cited when entering executive session.  See 
Dist. Att’y for N.W. Dist. v. Selectmen of Sunderland, 11 Mass. App. Ct. 663, 666 (1981).  
Pursuant to the Open Meeting Law, a public body may meet in executive session “[t]o consider 
the purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property if the chair declares that an open meeting 
may have a detrimental effect on the negotiating position of the public body;” (“Purpose 6”).  
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a).   
 
Executive session purposes are narrowly construed.  The public body bears the burden of 
demonstrating applicability of the claimed executive session purpose.  See District Attorney for 
the N. Dist. v. School Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 566 (2009).  Discussions under 
Purpose 6 must relate directly to the purchase, lease, exchange, or value of real property where 
confidentiality is necessary to protect a public body’s negotiating position.  See OML 2022-119; 
OML 2021-62.  The proper application of Purpose 6 is to protect a public body’s negotiating 
position vis-a-vis other parties to a transaction, not to escape the inconvenience of public 
scrutiny or opposition.  See OML 2023-241; OML 2023-81.  General discussions regarding real 
property or the use of real property, where there is no negotiating position to protect, do not fall 
within Purpose 6.  See OML 2023-81; OML 2016-93; OML 2012-98 (“Potential uses for land 
and whether or not to go ahead with a project are not proper discussions under Purpose 6.”).  
Similarly, discussions regarding the imposition of conservation restrictions, where there is no

negotiating position to protect and the discussion does not relate to the purchase, exchange, lease 
or value of property, do not fall within the executive session purpose.  See OML 2019-59.   
 
Although public bodies may not invoke Purpose 6 to engage in general discussions 
regarding the use of property, we have found that meeting in executive session under Purpose 6 
may be permissible in the absence of contemporaneous negotiations seeking to consummate a 
specific transaction.  For example, it was permissible to meet in executive session under Purpose 
6 to identify potential entities to approach regarding the purchase or exchange of real property.  
See OML 2022-119.  See also OML 2018-73 (finding no violation where public body discussed 
potential acquisition of alternate elementary school sites in executive session under Purpose 6).   
 
We have acknowledged that designating a road as a public way may require a 
municipality to either purchase land or take it by eminent domain and therefore could require a 
discussion of value of real property.  See OML 2021-14.  See also OML 2023-29 (declining to 
adopt a “blanket assertion that discussions of eminent domain are not appropriate for executive 
session under Purpose 6”); OML 2020-157 (finding Council could invoke Purpose 6 to discuss 
eminent domain after negotiations stalled; property owner was likely to dispute taking 
compensation).  However, where there has not yet been a decision to take a road by eminent 
domain and there is no discussion of the property’s value, a public body does not have a 
negotiating position to protect, and it is not appropriate to convene an executive session under 
Purpose 6.  See OML 2021-14.  See also OML 2020-63 (finding Purpose 6 inappropriate where 
discussion was about project funding and procedure, not about purchase or value of locus for 
project). 
 
Here, the Council had no negotiating position to protect when it convened in executive 
session under Purpose 6 during the April 25 Meeting.  During the executive session, the Council 
discussed the title report and methods by which a street may become a public way.  There was, 
however, no discussion of the value of Coffin Street, purchase of the fee in the way held by 
abutting property owners, or a taking by eminent domain.  Because deliberations about Coffin 
Street did not fall within Purpose 6 or any of the other executive session purposes allowed under 
the Open Meeting Law, the executive session at the April 25 Meeting was convened without a 
lawful purpose, in violation of the Open Meeting Law. 
 
III. We decline to review allegations that the Council improperly asserted the 
attorney-client privilege. 
 
The complainant alleges that the Council improperly relied on the attorney-client 
privilege when responding to her requests for executive session minutes of the April 25 Meeting.  
The Open Meeting Law does not authorize the Attorney General to determine whether the 
Council’s assertion of the attorney-client privilege was justified.  See OML 2024-199; OML 
2020-71; OML 2016-129.  Therefore, we decline to separately review this allegation.4

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it convened in convened in executive session without a lawful purpose.  We order the 
Council’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and we caution that 
similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  In addition, we 
order the Council, within 30 days of the date of this letter, to release to the public the minutes 
of the executive session of the April 25 Meeting, in their entirety and without any redactions.  
See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f) (“the minutes of any executive session ... may be withheld from 
disclosure to the public in their entirety ... as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes 
of the executive session, but no longer; provided, however, that the executive session was held in 
compliance with section 21.”).  See also OML 2021-14; OML 2020-63; OML 2015-120;  
District Atty. for Plymouth Dist. v. Bd. of Selectmen of Middleborough, 395 Mass. 629, 634 
(1985) (“We view [the Open Meeting Law] as a statutory public waiver of any possible privilege 
of the public client in meetings of governmental bodies except in the narrow circumstances 
stated in the statute.”); Accord Revere Retirement Bd. v. Attorney General, 93 Mass. App. Ct. 
1117 (2018) (Rule 1:28 Decision) (Further Appellate Review denied Sep. 13, 2018). 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the
