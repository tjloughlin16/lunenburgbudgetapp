 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Castro: 
 
This office received a complaint from Alan Dias on September 14, 2022, alleging that the 
Hanson Board of Health (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. 
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on June 30, 2022, and you responded, on 
behalf of the Board, by letter dated August 3, 2022, following an extension of time granted by 
this office.
1  The complaint alleges that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by posting an 
insufficiently specific notice for a meeting held on June 14.   
 
We resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(1), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review, and the notice and minutes of the Board meeting held 
on June 14, as well as an audio recording of that meeting.
2  Following our review, we find that 
the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that “a public body shall post notice of every meeting at 
least 48 hours prior to such meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Public bodies are required to post 
notices that “shall be printed in a legible, easily understandable format and shall contain the date, 
time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(b).  A public body 
must post notice that include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently

specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the 
anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2016-167, OML 2016-90; OML 
2015-196; OML 2014-155; OML 2011-11.3  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, 
unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone.  See 
OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting 
to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the public aware of the discussion 
topics. 
 
The complaint alleges that the notice for the Board meeting held on June 14 lacked 
sufficient specificity with respect to one topic, that relating to a Snow Street property owned by 
the complainant.  Here, the Board posted notice for a meeting to be held remotely on June 14 and 
included the following topic: “Map 1, Lot 1282 Snow Street – Alan Dias.”  During a previous 
Board meeting held on April 5, the Board also discussed the Snow Street property, noting that a 
sanitary system design plan for the Snow Street property had been approved in 2005, that the 
plan had expired, and that a new plan would have to be submitted and reviewed.  During the June 
14 meeting, the Board again briefly discussed the plan for the Snow Street property, which 
included a disposal works construction permit, and voted that any plan that expired after three 
years would need to be resubmitted with a new application and fee. 
 
We find that the notice with respect to the Snow Street property topic was insufficient as 
it failed to include any detail as to the specific type of plan or permit under consideration with 
respect to this property such that a reasonable member of the public could determine the 
anticipated nature of the Board’s discussion.  Although this topic may have been meaningful to 
Board members and regular Board meeting attendees, notice topics must be sufficiently specific 
to reasonably inform members of the public unfamiliar with the ongoing matters before the 
Board of the anticipated nature of the discussions to be held.  See OML 2020-137; OML 2013-
55.   
  
With respect to the complainant’s concern that the Board failed to provide individual 
notice to him that his Snow Street property would be discussed during the June 14 meeting, the 
Open Meeting Law does not require public bodies to provide additional notice of meetings to 
specific individuals or groups of individuals for topics discussed during a meeting, unless an 
individual will be discussed in executive session under G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1).  See OML 2019-
17; OML 2013-41.   
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
providing insufficient specificity in its June 14 meeting notice.  We order immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
