DISCUSSION 
 
 The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body respond to a request for minutes, both 
open and executive sessions, within 10 days of the request. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (g)(2). 
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public body 
within 30 days of the alleged violation, or, if the alleged violation could not reasonably have 
been known at the time it occurred, then within 30 days of the date it should reasonably have 
been discovered. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(4). To be considered timely, the 
allegation in the complaint regarding the Committee’s failure to respond to the complainant’s 
request for minutes must have been filed with the Committee no more than 30 days after the 
Committee’s failure to respond to the request, or 40 days after the Committee received the 
request. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(4); see also OML 2025-35; OML Declination 
8-16-16 (Swansea Board of Health); OML 2015-166.3 Here, the complainant, who serves as the 
Director of Facilities and Safety for the School District, requested minutes from the Committee 
on March 13, 2025. Thus, the Committee was required to respond to the request on or before 
March 23, 2025. Since the complaint was not filed until January 8, 2026, 291 days later, we find 
this allegation in the complaint to be untimely and decline to review it. 
 
We find the second allegation raised in the complaint, that the Committee failed to create 
and maintain sufficiently accurate minutes, to be timely and therefore review it. Here, the 
Committee provided the requested executive session minutes to the complainant on December 
10, 2025, and the complaint was then filed on January 8, 2026. As such, the complaint was filed 
within 30 days of the date it could have reasonably been discovered that the minutes were 
insufficient. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to “create 
and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions. G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(a). Minutes must set “forth the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a 
summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the 
meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all 
votes.” Id. When reviewing minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for 
substantial compliance with the accuracy requirement. See OML 2018-74. By substantial 
compliance, we mean that the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a 
member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear 
understanding of what occurred. See id.; OML 2012-106. While minutes must include a

 The complaint alleges that the minutes of the January 14, 2025, meeting are insufficient 
as they fail to accurately summarize the discussions that took place during the executive session. 
The Committee met in executive session on January 14, 2025, for approximately 30 minutes, but 
the original minutes provide no summary of the discussions that took place during the executive 
session. Rather, the minutes merely include two sentences explaining the purpose of the 
executive session, i.e., that the complainant requested to meet with the Committee in executive 
session to discuss a “Vote of No Confidence.” The Committee acknowledges, and we find, that 
the original minutes of the January 14, 2025, executive session were insufficient and did not 
fully comply with the Open Meeting Law. 
 
 In response to the Open Meeting Law complaint, the Committee revised the executive 
session minutes to include a more detailed summary of the discussions that took place. However, 
we find that the amended minutes of the January 14, 2025, executive session are still insufficient. 
The amended minutes note that “[the complainant] read a statement” and reference that the 
statement is “attached” but do not summarize the statement made by the complainant.

By failing to include in the original meeting minutes a summary of the discussions that 
occurred during the executive session, and by failing to include in the amended minutes any 
summary of the complainant’s statement made during the executive session, the Board violated 
the Open Meeting Law by creating insufficient minutes. See OML 2024-50. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to create sufficiently accurate and detailed minutes. We order the Committee’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and we caution that similar future 
violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law. In addition, we order the 
Committee, within 30 days of the date of this letter, to revise the minutes of the January 14, 
2025, executive session to include a summary of the complainant’s statement, and to notify our 
office that it has done so.
