 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Sumners: 
 
This office received a complaint from Kevin Maccioli on September 29, 2024, alleging 
that the Marlborough School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on or around 
August 2, 2024, and the Committee responded by letter dated August 28, 2024, following an 
extension of time granted by this office.  The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to 
provide meeting minutes in response to two separate requests for minutes of open and executive 
session meetings held from 2020 to 2023.  Implicit in the complaint is an allegation that the 
Committee failed to create and timely approve the requested meeting minutes.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to create and timely approve several sets of meeting minutes.  In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the complaint, the Committee’s response, and the Complainant’s 
request for further review filed with our office.  In addition, we reviewed the notice, minutes, and 
a video of the Committee’s August 27, 2024, meeting. 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On November 28, 2023, Mr. Maccioli sent an email request 
to the Committee for 13 sets of meeting minutes, for meeting dates spanning from 2020 to 2023.  
The request included minutes of 11 “workshop meetings,” one “budget workshop,” and one 
“special meeting.”  The Committee responded to the request on December 8, 2023, by providing 
draft minutes for one of the meetings and stating that it could not locate any other of the 
requested meeting minutes.  The Committee represented that it would create and approve any 
minutes that did not yet exist and provide them as they are approved.    
 
On March 24, 2024, Mr. Maccioli sent a written request for the minutes of the 
Committee’s December 13, 2023, executive session meeting during which the Committee

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The Law further states that “[m]inutes 
of all open sessions shall be created and approved in a timely manner,” while requiring that 
minutes of executive sessions be “review[ed]” “at reasonable intervals” to determine whether 
continued nondisclosure is warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c); G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1).  Although 
“reasonable interval” is not defined by the Law, our office has found a quarterly review or a 
review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2020-101; OML 2019-45; OML 2017-
104.2 
 
Furthermore, the Open Meeting Law specifies that “the minutes of an open session, if 
they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any 
person within 10 days.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  If the minutes do not yet exist at the time of a 
request, the public body is still required to respond to the request within 10 days with an 
explanation of whether the minutes do or do not exist in either approved or draft form.  See OML 
2023-82; OML 2018-98; OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; OML 2015-173. 
 
Likewise, a public body must respond to a request for executive session minutes within 
10 days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  If the public body or a designee has determined, prior to the 
request, that certain executive session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester 
directly, notifying him or her of the availability of those minutes.  See OML 2013-105; OML 
2013-99.  And if, at the time of a request, the public body has not conducted a review of the

As a preliminary matter, we decline to review allegations that the Committee failed to 
timely or properly respond to the two requests for meeting minutes, as such allegations were not 
timely filed.  To be considered timely, an Open Meeting Law complaint regarding the failure to 
timely respond to a request for meeting minutes must be filed within 30 days following the 
failure to timely respond, i.e., 40 days following the request.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); see also 
OML 2015-166; OML Declination 8-16-16 (Swansea Recreation Commission).  The complaint 
alleges that on November 28, 2023, the Complainant requested minutes of 13 Committee 
meetings dating back to 2020, and on March 24, 2024, the Complainant requested minutes of the 
Committee’s December 13, 2023, executive session meeting.  The complaint about the 
Committee’s failure to respond to the requests was not filed until August 2, 2024, and therefore 
is untimely.  Nonetheless, we remind the Committee of its obligations to respond to all requests 
for meeting minutes—including requests for executive session minutes—within 10 days, in 
accordance with the guidance provided above.   
 
Furthermore, our review is limited to allegations of violations of the Open Meeting Law 
that were clearly and sufficiently raised in the original complaint filed with the Committee.  
See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(1); OML 2019-133, n.4 (explaining that we decline to 
review allegations raised in a request to this office, which were not included in a complaint filed 
with the public body); OML 2021-10, n. 2; OML 2012-92.  Therefore, we do not address 
concerns raised with this office that some meeting minutes, which were first created and 
provided to Mr. Maccioli well after the complaint was filed with the Committee, are insufficient.   
 
We do, however, review the allegation that the Committee failed to create, maintain, and 
timely approve meeting minutes.  The Open Meeting Law imposes a continuing obligation to 
maintain minutes of all meetings.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2020-69 (reviewing a 
complaint regarding a committee’s failure to approve meeting minutes for a three-year period); 
OML 2019-133; OML 2018-115 (reviewing a complaint regarding the failure to create and 
maintain meeting minutes for meetings held four years prior); OML 2013-45.  Therefore, the 
alleged violation regarding a failure to create and maintain minutes for thirteen separate meetings 
held between 2020 and 2023 continued each day until the minutes were not created and 
approved, and the complaint was timely filed where the minutes still had not been approved as of 
the date the complaint was filed.  We find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it did not approve twelve sets of minutes—for meetings ranging from September 2020 
through May 2024—until August 27, 2024, which was more than 30 days and three meetings 
following each meeting.3

In recognition of the fact that the minutes that the Committee failed to create and timely 
approve were minutes of workshop and special meetings, we remind the Committee that it is 
obligated to create and timely approve minutes for all meetings,4 including workshop meetings 
and special meetings, and that workshop meetings or special meetings count toward the three 
meetings or 30-day period for purposes of timely approval of minutes.  The Open Meeting Law 
and regulations do not distinguish between “regular” meetings and other types of meetings such 
as workshops, public hearings, joint meetings, or special meetings for purposes of timely 
approval of minutes.  See OML 2017-103 (public body should have created complete minutes 
for its “workshop” meetings because topics discussed were matters within the public body's 
jurisdiction); OML 2019-115 (no exception for “single topic” meetings or meetings not 
“normally scheduled”). 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to create and timely approve meeting minutes.  We order the Committee’s immediate 
and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution that similar future violations 
could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law and may result in the imposition of civil 
penalties of up to $1,000 per violation.
