RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint  
 
Dear Mr. Vivori:  
 
This office received a complaint from Eileen Mooney on August 28, 2023, alleging that 
the Great Barrington Board of Assessors (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint originally was filed with the Board on July 31.  The Board did 
not respond to the complaint.  The complaint alleges that the Board failed to approve minutes for 
a meeting held on May 31, 2022, and that draft minutes that have been provided are 
insufficiently detailed. 
 
As a preliminary matter, we note that the Board did not respond to the Open Meeting 
Law complaint.  The Open Meeting Law requires that within 14 business days of receipt of an 
Open Meeting Law complaint, a public body must meet to review the complaint’s allegations; 
take remedial action, if appropriate; and send to the complainant and to the Attorney General a 
response and a description of any remedial action taken.  940 CMR 29.05(5).  Upon the filing of 
an Open Meeting Law complaint with a public body, the chair “shall disseminate copies of the 
complaint to the members of the public body,” and “the public body shall review the complaint's 
allegations.”  940 CMR 29.05(3), (5).  Thus, a public body must meet to review the complaint 
and formulate a response, or meet to delegate that authority, and respond to the complaint within 
14 business days. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); see also OML 2017-69; OML 2012-90.1  
 
 Turning to the merits of the complaint, we find, based on information provided by the 
Town Clerk and not disputed by the Board, that the Board has not approved minutes for the 
meeting held on May 31, 2022; minutes for that meeting remain in unapproved draft form,

although they have been posted publicly online.  The Open Meeting Law requires public bodies 
to create and approve meeting minutes, for both open and executive sessions, in a timely 
manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” means “within the next three public body 
meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for 
further delay.”  940 CMR 29.11; see OML 2018-48.  As three meetings and 30 days have passed 
since the May 31, 2022, meeting, the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely 
approve the minutes of that meeting.   
 
In general, we decline to review the sufficiency of meeting minutes that are still in draft 
form at the time a complaint is filed.  See OML 2018-121; OML 2014-7.  Accordingly, we 
decline to find that the draft minutes, as posted online and provided to the complainant, are 
insufficient, though we note that had the minutes been approved in their current form, they 
would be inadequate.  Therefore, we take this opportunity to provide guidance to the Board on 
the requirements for meeting minutes.   
 
A public body must create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including 
executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a 
summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the 
meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all 
votes.  G.L. c. 30A, §22(a).  Prior to convening in executive session, a public body must first 
convene in open session.  G.L. c. 30A, §21(b).  The chair must state the purpose of the executive 
session and publicly announce whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the 
executive session.  Id.  The public body must vote to go into executive session and the vote of 
each member must be recorded by roll call.  Id.  The entirety of this procedure must be reflected 
in the open session minutes.  Any vote taken at an executive session shall be recorded by roll call 
and entered into the executive session minutes.  G.L. c. 30A, §22(b).  Minutes of executive 
sessions must include the same level of detail as is required of open sessions; minutes should 
contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not attend the 
meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See OML 
2022-189; OML 2013-l6.  Although a transcript of the discussions is not required, and the 
minutes need not include every remark or opinion presented, minutes must include a substantive 
summary of the discussion on each topic. See OML 2020-18.  The Open Meeting Law requires 
that meeting minutes include more than a statement that a public body held a discussion about a 
specified topic; the Law requires that the minutes summarize the substance of the public body’s 
discussion.  See OML 2020-53; OML 2019-167; OML 2018-8.   
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely approve minutes of its May 31, 2022, meeting.  We order the Board’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution the Board that a 
determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the Open Meeting Law, and may result in a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per 
violation.  In addition, we order the Board to revise and approve minutes for its May 31, 2022, 
meeting, consistent with the guidance provided above, and to certify to this office that it has done 
so within 40 days from the date of this letter.  Finally, we order all current members of the Board
