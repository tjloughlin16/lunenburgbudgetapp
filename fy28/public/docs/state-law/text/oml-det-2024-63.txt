This office received a complaint from William Tinker on October 2, 2023, and a second 
complaint on October 5, 2023, alleging that the Hardwick Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) 
violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The first complaint was originally filed 
with the Board on or about August 13, 2023; the second complaint was originally filed with the 
Board on or about September 12, 2023; and you responded, on behalf of the Board, by separate 
letters dated August 24, 2023, and September 26, 2023.  The complaints allege that the Board i) 
failed to accommodate all members of the public who attempted to attend a meeting held on July 
18, 2023; ii) posted a notice with insufficient specificity for the meeting held on August 16, 
2023; iii) members failed to use video technology; iv) did not allow for public participation 
during the July 18, 2023, or August 16, 2023, meetings; v) did not comply with the state’s 
conflict of interest law; and vi) did not read meeting minutes that were reviewed and approved or 
acknowledge an Open Meeting Law complaint.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by holding 
a meeting on July 18, 2023, that was not adequately open and accessible to the public.  We find 
that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the original complaints, the Board’s responses to the complaints, and 
the complaints filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the 
notice, minutes and video recording of the Board meeting held on July 18, 2023, the notice and 
video recording of the Board meeting held on August 16, 2023, and the notices and minutes of

FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  On July 13, 2023, at 4:29 P.M., the Board posted notice of 
a meeting to be held on July 18, 2023, at 6:30 P.M.  The notice listed one main topic - 
“Presentation of a proposed project on Casella and Roach property located on Patrill Hollow 
Road.”  The notice listed the location of the meeting as Zoom and provided information 
necessary to access the virtual meeting, including a link to the Zoom meeting, several telephone 
numbers the public could use to dial into the meeting, and the Webinar ID number required to 
log into the meeting.   
 
 The July 18, 2023, meeting was held as planned and all three Board members participated 
remotely.  During the meeting, which lasted for approximately 20 minutes, the Board received a 
presentation from Casella, a research management company, which proposed constructing a 
“low emission landfill” on approximately 48 acres of land, which included the Roach & Sons 
gravel pit, on Patrill Hollow Land.  The Town Administrator initially acted as host of the 
meeting and admitted attendees into the meeting but then turned over the hosting duties to Brian 
Oliver, the Vice President of Casella, so that he could share his slide show presentation.  The 
Town Administrator explained that the zoom program that was being utilized for the meeting did 
not allow for co-hosts so Mr. Oliver would have to admit any attendees.  The Town 
Administrator then stated that “we might cap out at 100” but explained that the meeting was also 
being streamed live on YouTube.  Approximately ten minutes into the presentation, the Town 
Administrator interrupted and asked Mr. Oliver to admit individuals into the meeting.  
Eventually, the Town Administrator reclaimed the hosting duties and admitted individuals into 
the meeting but by that time the slide show presentation had concluded.  Over 100 people 
attempted to attend the meeting.  The Board unanimously approved a motion “acknowledging 
receipt of the proposal” and shortly thereafter adjourned the meeting. 
 
On August 11, 2023, at 3:07 P.M., the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on 
August 16, 2023, at 5:30 P.M.  The notice listed thirteen topics, including “New Business” and

the topics to be discussed under “New Business,” which was “Visitation: Angie Broussard; Re:

item descriptions listed under “Old Business.”  The notice further listed the location of the 
meeting as Zoom and provided information necessary to access the virtual meeting, including a 
link to the Zoom meeting, several telephone numbers the public could use to dial into the 
meeting, and the Webinar ID number required to log into the meeting.

DISCUSSION 
 
I. We Decl ine to Review Allegations That Would Not Constitute Violations 
of the Open Meeting Law. 
 
The complaints include several allegations that, even if true, would not constitute 
violations of the Open Meeting Law, and therefore we must decline to review.  First, the 
complaints allege that the Board did not allow members of the public to speak during the July 18, 
2023, meeting and muted the complainant during the August 16, 2023.  The Open Meeting Law 
does not require that a public body allow public participation, but rather provides that “[n]o 
person shall address a meeting of a public body without permission of the chair, and all persons 
shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”  G.L. c. 30A § 20(f).  If public participation will be 
permitted, the chair may determine the method for receiving public comment, who may speak or 
otherwise participate during a meeting, and may set limitations on participation.
2  See OML 
2020-114; OML 2017-189.3  Thus, as it is within the Chair’s discretion to allow public 
participation during a meeting, the Chair did not violate the Open Meeting Law by not allowing 
public participation during the July 18, 2023, meeting or impeding the complainant from 
addressing the Board during the August 16, 2023, meeting.  See OML 2020-114; OML 2017-
189; OML 2014-23; OML 2012-78.  We do, however, encourage public bodies to allow for as 
much public participation as time and circumstances permit.  See OML 2019-120; OML 2015-
12; OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59.   
 
Second, the complaints allege that Board members failed to comply with the “state’s 
conf
lict of interest law.”  The Division of Open Government’s statutory authority concerns 
compliance only with the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  Even if true, these 
allegations would not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  See OM: 2023-214; OML 
Declination 11-3-2023 (Massachusetts Board of Elementary and Secondary Education); OML 
2020-36.  Therefore, we decline to review these allegations and refer the complainant to the State

Finally, the complaints allege that the Board approved meeting minutes without reading 
them and did not acknowledge an Open Meeting Law complaint.  The Open Meeting Law does 
not dictate the content of a discussion at a meeting and once a meeting begins, the public body 
controls its own agenda.  See OML 2023-76; OML 2022-187.  With respect to the meeting 
minutes, the Open Meeting Law does not govern a public body’s process and procedure for 
approval of meeting minutes and does not require the public body to substantively discuss the 
meeting minutes prior to approving them.  See OML Declination 5-4-21 (New Marlborough 
Cable Advisory Committee); see also G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  With respect to the 
acknowledgement of an Open Meeting Law complaint, the Open Meeting Law requires that 
within 14 days of receipt of a complaint, the public body review the complaint’s allegations; take 
remedial action, if appropriate; and send to the Attorney General a copy of the complaint and a 
description of any remedial action taken.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  The Board 
received the first Open Meeting Law complaint on August 15, 2023, and was not necessarily 
required to acknowledge it during the August 16, 2023, meeting as the Board had 14 business 
days to review and respond to the complaint.  The Board reviewed the complaint during its next 
meeting on August 28, 2023, meeting and approved the response drafted by town legal counsel, 
all of which occurred within 14 business days.  The public body may review a complaint during 
a meeting and refer the complaint to a designee to respond, or a designee may receive and draft a 
response to the complaint in advance of a meeting for the public body to approve during a 
meeting.  See OML 2022-59; OML 2020-164; OML 2020-96.  Once a public body has reviewed 
a complaint during a meeting, its decision to simply refer the complaint, rather than discuss its 
substance, is the public body’s prerogative.  See OML 2019-40; OML 2017-96.   
 
II.  The Board’s July 18, 2023, Meeting Was Not Open and Accessi ble  To All 
Members of the Public Who Attempted To Attend. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Council of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The law requires that meetings of a public body be 
properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened.  
See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  Generally, access to a meeting must include the opportunity 
to be physically present, as well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the members of the 
public body.  See OML 2017-135; OML 2015-38; OML 2014-3; OML 2013-189.  In order for a 
meeting to be truly open to the public, the meeting venue must be accessible to the public for the 
duration of the meeting.  See OML 2018-51; OML 2017-172; OML 2014-18. 
 
Chapter 20 of the Acts of 2021, entitled An Act Relative to Extending Certain COVID-19 
Measures Adopted During the State of Emergency (“Chapter 20”), suspended the requirements 
under the Open Meeting Law and the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations that a 
quorum of a public body, including the chair, be physically present at the meeting location and 
allows all members of a public body to participate in a meeting remotely, provided that the 
public body provides “adequate, alternative means” of public access, such as through telephone, 
internet or satellite enabled audio or video conferencing or any other technology that enables the

public to clearly follow the proceedings of the public body while those activities are occurring.4  
See Chapter 20, § 20(b); OML 2021-135. 
 
The complaint alleges that all interested members of the public were not able to access 
the B
oard’s July 18, 2023, remote meeting, because the Board’s Zoom account only allowed up 
to 100 participants in the meeting.  For all meetings, whether held in person or remotely, public 
bodies must make reasonable accommodations for overflow crowds.  See OML 2018-27; OML 
2015-92; OML 2012-66.  The Board states that attendance reached only 96 individuals, which 
was below the Zoom’s meeting capacity of 100 people.  After reviewing the records of the Zoom 
meeting, we find that at least 109 individuals attempted to join the remote meeting.  While most 
individuals were successful in gaining access to the meeting, some individuals were unable to 
attend the meeting and some individuals remained in a waiting room for up to 15 minutes.  By 
the time those individuals gained access to the meeting, the presentation by Casella had 
concluded.   
 
The B
oard suggests that the “meeting was simulcast via YouTube, so that individuals not 
joining the Zoom could still observe the presentation from Casella in real time.”  While our 
office has published guidance explaining that “‘[a]dequate, alternative means of access’ could 
include Zoom, a high-capacity telephone conference line, Facebook Live, YouTube Live, and 
broadcasting on live TV, including local cable access television,” the notice for the July 18, 
2023, meeting only identified the location of the meeting as Zoom.
5  That meetings of public 
bodies be accessible to the public is fundamental to the Open Meeting Law; therefore, it is 
imperative that a meeting notice state clearly how the public may access the meeting.  Because 
the posted notice for the July 18, 2023, meeting only identified one method of access, Zoom, and 
this method of real-time access was not available to everyone who wanted to attend the meeting, 
we find that the Board did not provide adequate, alternative means of access.  See OML 2021-
63; OML 2021-17.  Furthermore, as the Board made no effort to address the capacity issue 
during the meeting, nor did the Board cancel and reschedule the meeting to another date when it 
could arrange to use a higher capacity platform, we find that the Board did not make reasonable 
efforts to accommodate the larger-than-usual attendance.  See OML 2022-122.  We therefore 
find that the Board’s July 18, 2023, meeting was not accessible to the public, in violation of the 
Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2021-91.

the dur

that he could share his screen with attendees and told him that he would have to admit attendees 
going forward.  If a public body uses a Zoom waiting room in order to manually admit members 
of the public to the meeting, it must ensure that attendees are promptly admitted to the meeting at 
the start of and throughout the duration of the meeting.  See OML 2021-147.  Otherwise, 
attendees are essentially locked out of the meeting, as was the case here.  See, e.g., OML 2023-

III. We Find that the Topics Listed on the Notices for the Meetings Held on 
July 18, 2023, and August 16, 2023, were Sufficiently Specific. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice 48 hours in advance of a 
mee
ting and include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the 
chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public 
bodies are required to list topics in a meeting notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably 
advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We 
generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could 
read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 
2018-7; OML 2015-35.  A public body need not list each and every detail about a topic in order 
to comply with the Law’s requirements.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134. 
   
The complaint alleges that the notices for the meetings held on July 18, 2023, and August 
16, 2023, wer
e insufficient.  Specifically, the complaint alleges that the July 18, 2023, notice was 
insufficient where it did not indicate the actual content of the presentation.  Here, the notice 
indicated that there would be a “Presentation of a proposed project on Casella and Roach 
property located on Patrill Hollow Road.”  The Open Meeting Law does not require a public 
body to list all tangential topics related to a discussion nor does the law require a public body to 
anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only that it identify the 
discussion topic with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2015-73; OML 
2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38.  We understand that the Board was not aware of the 
nature of the project prior to the presentation received from Casella during the July 18, 2023, 
meeting.  As such, we are satisfied that the topic as listed on the notice here was sufficient to 
reasonably advise the public of the issue to be discussed at the meeting.  See OML 2023-114; 
OML 2013-152.  
 
With respect to the August 16, 2023, notice, the complaint alleges that this notice was 
insuf
ficient where it included broad topics, such as New Business and Old Business, without 
listing any subtopics.  We have previously stated that boilerplate topics, such as “New Business” 
or “Old Business” are too vague to offer the public an adequate understanding of the anticipated 
topics to be discussed at that particular meeting.  See OML 2022-234; OML 2016-76; OML 
2013-14; OML 2012-58.  Here, the “New Business” topic listed on the notice included a specific

Finally, the Board was not required to post attachments pertaining to the presentation 
topic
 listed on the notice for the July 18, 2023, meeting.  The Open Meeting Law does not 
require that a public body attach supporting documents to a meeting notice or that it publicly post 
a “meeting packet” that contains such documents.  See OML 2022-24; OML 2022-19; OML 
2021-181.  To the contrary, the meeting notice itself must contain all information that the Open 
Meeting Law requires; posting attachments does not cure an otherwise defective notice.  See 
OML 2021-138; OML 2021-57; OML 2019-22.  Moreover, there is no requirement in the Open 
Meeting Law that the public body affirmatively provide copies of documents to persons in 
attendance at a meeting.  But see G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c) (minutes of an open session, if they exist 
and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person 
within 10 days); G.L. c. 66, § 10 (Public Records Law requires Board to permit inspection or 
provide copy of any public record, such as documents used during a meeting, within 10 business 
days of request).  We encourage public bodies, whenever feasible, to provide meeting attendees 
with copies of all documents it intends to discuss.  Particularly where viewing such documents 
would be helpful to following the conversation during the meeting, providing copies of 
documents that the Board intends to discuss promotes transparency and public access to 
government deliberations.  See OML 2016-94. 
 
IV. We Encourage Public Bodies to Use Video Technology During Remote 
Meetings and Decline to Find a Violation When Only Some Members are 
Visible When Speaking.  
 
Finally, we address the allegation that the Chair’s face was “shadowed” during the 
August 16, 2023, meeting and therefore was not clearly visible on camera.  The Attorney 
General’s 2017 Open Meeting Law regulations regarding remote participation state that “[w]hen 
video technology is in use, the remote participant shall be clearly visible to all persons present in 
the meeting location.”  940 CMR 29.10(6)(b).  These 2017 regulations contemplated a situation 
in which at least a quorum of the public body members, as well as members of the public, would 
attend a meeting at a physical meeting location, and one or more public body members could 
participate remotely if physical attendance at the meeting would be unreasonably difficult.  See 
940 CMR 29.10(4) and (5).  Chapter 20 allows public bodies to hold meetings at which some or 
all public body members attend remotely, and/or access to the public is provided via remote 
means rather than in-person.   
 
The Attorney General encourages public bodies to use video technology whenever 
possible to enhance the ability of the public to follow the proceedings of a public body remotely.  
However, we are cognizant that some public body members may not have personal technology 
===PAGE 8===
8 
 
available to participate in a meeting by video, and that public body members who utilize video 
may need
 to step off camera briefly.  A strict requirement that if video technology is in use all 
public body members must be visible on camera at all times could result in public bodies opting 
not to use video technology at all, which surely would not serve the Open Meeting Law’s 
transparency goals.  We note here that the Chair’s video camera was in use during the meeting, 
and while his silhouette was visible, his face was not clearly visible due to lighting.  However, 
the Chair’s tile box included his full name and was highlighted when he spoke making it possible 
to follow who was speaking.  Therefore, we decline to find a violation of the Open Meeting Law 
where, as here, video technology is generally in use for a meeting, but some members of the 
public body are not visible at all times.  See OML 2024-46.  Nonetheless, the Attorney General 
recommends, as a best practice, that members of public bodies turn on their video cameras, 
especially while speaking, whenever possible.  See OML 2021-2.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
holding a meeting on July 18, 2023, that was not adequately open and accessible to the public.  
We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we 
caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
