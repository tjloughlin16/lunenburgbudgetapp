DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). A foundational requirement of the Open Meeting Law is 
that public bodies post notice of each meeting “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding 
Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays” and that every notice include, among other things, “a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(b). Public bodies must list topics for discussion with “sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). 
A notice need not include every detail about an anticipated discussion, but the law requires that 
notice topics include “sufficient specificity” so that the public can decide whether to attend the 
meeting and observe the discussions regarding the specific topics anticipated for discussion. See 
OML 2023-108; OML 2018-22; OML 2018-7.3  
  
When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its 
face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone. See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155. 
Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was

sufficiently specific to make the public aware of the discussion topics. We have said that 
boilerplate notice topics such as “Old Business” and “New Business,” on their own, are vague 
and do not offer the public an understanding of the anticipated topics to be discussed. See OML 
2017-14; OML 2014-140; OML 2013-14; OML 2013-107. We have found that where a public 
body will review all articles for a Town Meeting warrant or the entire budget, it is not required to 
identify all warrant articles or all budgetary items on its meeting notice. See OML 2017-185; 
OML 2012-83. However, where a public body anticipates discussing specific Town Meeting 
warrant articles or budgetary items, it must specifically list them in its meeting notice. See OML 
2013-8; OML 2011-7.  
 
As a preliminary matter, we note that the Open Meeting Law requires that complaints be 
filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 
CMR 29.05(4). If the alleged violation could not have been known at the time it occurred, then 
the complaint must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the date on which the alleged 
violation could reasonably have been discovered. 940 CMR 29.05(4). Alleged violations that 
take place during an open session meeting can reasonably be discovered at the time they occur. 
See OML 2012-52. The complaint is untimely with respect to meetings held on April 6, 13, 18, 
and 20; however, we still reviewed the notices and minutes for those meetings as they provide 
relevant information with respect to notices for the remainder of the meetings. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Committee’s meeting notices were insufficiently specific 
where the notice for each of the Committee’s eight meetings included the exact same, generic 
topics. The Committee asserts “that any member of the public reviewing [these] agenda item[s] 
would understand that the Town’s budgets, as well as warrant articles for the annual and special 
town meetings, in their entirety, could be discussed and reviewed at the public meeting.” We are 
not persuaded. 
 
The Open Meeting Law clearly requires public bodies to list with specificity on their 
meeting notices those topics that the chair, and any other individual responsible for creating the 
notice, anticipates will be discussed at the particular meeting. After review of the minutes for 
Committee meetings held on April 6, 13, 18, 20, and 27, as well as May 4, 11, and 18, it is clear 
that the Committee did not anticipate discussing the entire Town Meeting and Special Town 
Meeting warrants nor the entire Town budget at each of these meetings. Indeed, on several 
occasions the Committee noted that specific warrant articles or budgets would be discussed at 
specific future meetings. Likewise, heads of Town departments attended certain meetings to 
present on their budgets; it is unlikely that this was unplanned and unanticipated. Finally, over 
time as the Committee voted to approve or support budgets and warrant articles it is less and less 
likely that the Committee anticipated discussing “the Town’s budgets, as well as warrant articles 
for the annual and special town meetings, in their entirety.” Based on the evidence before us, we 
find that the Committee posted insufficient notices for its meetings held on April 27 and May 4, 
11, and 18, where it posted the exact same list of generic topics for each meeting without 
providing sufficient specificity regarding any anticipated topics.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by posted insufficiently specific notices for its meetings held on April 27 and May 4, 11, and 18. 
We order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and 
caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of an intent to violate the 
Law. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Committee or 
with our office. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
