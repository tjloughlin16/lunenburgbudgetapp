DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
del
iberation and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  To that end, a public body must post notice of every 
meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(b).  Notices must include the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a “a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  Id.  The 
list of topics shall have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently 
specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the 
anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.

reviewed a letter from Mayor Macksey requesting that the Commission “rescind its vote” taken 
during the Commission’s October 15, 2024, meeting with respect to the Northeast Hangar.  The 
Commission then discussed the recommendations of the subcommittee, which was to reject the 
bid received from Milazzo Air and submit a new request for proposal for lease of the Northeast 
Hangar.  After a brief discussion, the Commission voted by majority vote to rescind its vote and

accept the recommendations of t he subcommittee.  The Open Meeting Law does not require a 
public body to list all tangential topics related to a discussion or list each and every detail about a 
topic, nor does the law require a public body to anticipate the course of deliberation or the 
outcome of a discussion, only that it identify the discussion topic with sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2022-98; OML 2015-73; OML 2014-127; OML 2014-58; 
OML 2014-38.  We are satisfied that the topics as listed on the notice here were sufficient to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); see also OML 2023-114; OML 2013-152. 
   
The complaint also alleges that the Commission deliberated outside of a properly posted 
mee
ting “to undermine” the vote taken during the October 15, 2024, meeting with respect to the 
Northeast Hangar.  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a public body be properly 
noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened.  See G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public 
body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The law 
defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including 
electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its 
jurisdiction.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” is a simple majority 
of the members of a public body.  Id.   
 
The Commission admits that three Commission members met individually with Mayor 
Macksey 
and Katherine Eade, Special Assistant to the Mayor, sometime after the October 15, 
2024, meeting but before the December 5, 2024, meeting.  Individual communications between 
the Mayor, Ms. Eade and a Commission member about the Northeast Hangar would not 
constitute improper deliberation as individual public officials are not public bodies and therefore 
are not subject to the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2023-208; OML 2021-3.  A non-member of 
a public body may communicate with public body members outside of a meeting as long as the 
non-member does not facilitate communication between or among a quorum of the public body. 
See OML 2017-125; OML 2017-86.  There is no evidence here that the substance of the 
individual conservations that Commission members might have had with the Mayor and/or Ms. 
Eade were subsequently communicated to a quorum of the Commission.  See OML 2024-187; 
OML 2014-103.  Rather, it appears that the Mayor and Ms. Eade separately conveyed the same 
information to each Commission member.  Moreover, the Commission asserts that Commission 
members did not discuss the Northeast Hangar among or between themselves prior to the 
December 5, 2024, meeting.  Although a public body may not use a non-member, such as a staff 
member, to facilitate communication on matters that the Commission would otherwise be 
required to save for discussion at an open meeting, we find no indication that occurred here.  See 
OML 2016-172; see also Dist. Atty for the Northern Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 451 Mass. 
561, 570-571 (2009).  We have stated in prior determinations that agreement without substantive 
discussion among public body members or even the lack of substantive discussion prior to a vote 
is insufficient to support the inference that those members deliberated outside of a noticed 
meeting.  See OML 2017-125; OML 2017-93; OML 2013-128.  Absent specific evidence to the 
contrary, we credit the public body’s version of events that a quorum of members did not 
deliberate outside of the December 5, 2024, meeting.  See OML 2021-30; OML 2015-8; OML 
2014-122.

Finally, to the extent the complaint alleges that t he Commission did not timely approve 
the minutes of the October 15, 2024, meeting, we do not find a violation.  The Open Meeting 
Law requires that a public body create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings and that 
those minutes be approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A § 22.  “Timely manner” means 
“within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the 
public body can show good cause for delay.”  940 CMR 29.11(2).  Whenever possible, we 
recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the following meeting.  See OML 2023-33.  
Here, the Commission approved the October 15, 2024, meeting minutes during its meeting held 
on January 21, 2025, which was its second meeting thereafter.  As such, we find that the 
Commission approved the minutes in a timely manner and thus did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law. 
   
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Commission did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  
This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or
