DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Council did not deliberate outside of a properly noticed me eting regarding a 
December 15, 2023, Open Meeting Law complaint. 
 
 The Open Meeting Law requires that all deliberations of a public body occur during a 
proper
ly noticed meeting and that such deliberations be open to the public unless a lawful 
executive session has been convened. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18, 20(a)-(b), 21. The Law defines 
“deliberation” broadly as any “oral or written communication through any medium, including 
electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its 
jurisdiction.” G.L. c. 30A, § 18. The Law defines “quorum” as “a simple majority of the 
members of the public body, unless otherwise provided in a general or special law, executive 
order or other authorizing provision.” Id.  
 
 The first complaint alleges that a quorum of the Council deliberated outside of a properly 
noticed meeting regarding a December 15, 2023, Open Meeting Law complaint. Specifically, the 
complaint alleges that “[o]n or before 12/18/23-1/2/24, Lowell City Councilors in multiple 
correspondence electronic formats, deliberated outside of an Open Meeting Law process.” 
Additionally, the complaint asserts that individuals from United States Representatives’ offices, 
news reporters, bloggers, and employees of the City, including employees of the law department, 
may have been present for some of the alleged improper deliberations.  
 
 In its response, the Council asserts that the allegation is untimely with respect to dates

an Open Meeting Law complaint.

Based on the complaint and additional documents provided to our office, it appears that 
the a
llegation of improper deliberation may be based on a mistaken belief that forwarding an 
Open Meeting Law complaint to a quorum of a public body, to legal counsel, or to third parties 
such as journalists violates the Open Meeting Law. First, the Attorney General’s Open Meeting 
Law regulations specifically require that the chair of a public body “disseminate copies of the 
complaint to the members of the public body”; simply disseminating an Open Meeting Law 
complaint to a quorum of members does not constitute deliberation. See G.L. c. 30A, § 18 
(excluding from the definition of “deliberation” the distribution of “documents that may be 
discussed at a meeting”); 940 CMR 29.05(3); OML 2020-88.5 Likewise, a public body may 
forward an Open Meeting Law complaint to its legal counsel in anticipation of delegating to 
legal counsel the authority to respond to the complaint. See OML 2020-164 (“The Board may 
review the complaint at a meeting and then authorize the Administrator to prepare and send the 
response, or the Administrator may receive the complaint and draft a response for the Board to 
review at a meeting, but the Board may not remove itself entirely from the process.”). Finally, 
there is nothing in the Open Meeting Law that requires a public body to keep an Open Meeting 
Law complaint confidential or that would prohibit a public body from providing an Open 
Meeting Law complaint to a journalist or other individual.
6  
 
The Complainant has provided no evidence to support the allegation that the Council 
deli
berated outside of a properly noticed meeting regarding the December 15, 2023, Open 
Meeting Law complaint, nor do we otherwise find any evidence to support this allegation. 
Absent sufficient evidence to the contrary, we credit the Council’s assertion that there were no 
communications outside of a properly noticed meeting between or among a quorum of the 
Council regarding the December 15, 2023, complaint. See OML 2016-141; OML 2017-93; OML 
2021-158; see also LaPointe v. License Board of Worcester, 389 Mass. 454 (1983) (“There is 
every presumption in favor of the honesty and sufficiency of the motives actuating public 
officers in actions ostensibly taken for the general welfare.”). Therefore, we find that the Council 
did not violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged.

II. Notice for the Council’s January 2 meeting was sufficiently s pecific with respect to 
the topic “Claims - (2) Property Damage” but was insufficient with respect to the 
executive session topic.  
 
 The Open Meeting Law requires that, “[e]xcept in an emergency, . . . a publ ic body shall 
post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to the meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays 
and legal holidays.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Meeting notices must include, among other things, a 
list of the topics that the chair, and any other individual authorized to create the notice, 
reasonably anticipates 48 hours (excluding weekends and legal holidays) in advance of the 
meeting will be discussed at the meeting. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); OML 2022-206; OML 2020-
152. The topics for discussion must be listed on the notice with “sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). 
We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently detailed when a reasonable member of the public 
could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion. See 
OML 2015-35. A public body need not list each and every detail about a topic in order to comply 
with the Law’s requirements. See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134. When 
reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not 
review a meeting notice standing alone. See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155. Rather, we review 
what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to 
make the public aware of the discussion topics. See OML 2021-22; OML 2020-137; OML 2019-
64. 
 
With respect to executive session discussions, the Open Meeting Law requires that prior 
to conve
ning in executive session, a public body announce in open session the purpose or 
purposes for convening in executive session, with the chair “stating all subjects that may be 
revealed without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called.” G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 
Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“A precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is 
necessary . . . because that is the only notification given to the public that [a public body] would 
conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the reason for doing so 
was proper or improper.”). This level of detail about the executive session topic must also be 
included on the meeting notice. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(1)(b); OML 2016-72; 
OML 2018-11; OML 2021-186. When convening in executive session to discuss an Open 
Meeting Law complaint, the meeting notice and the open session announcement should identify 
the Open Meeting Law complaint to be discussed by, for example, including the complainant’s 
name or other descriptive information about the complaint. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(b), 21(b); 940 
CMR 29.03(1)(b); OML 2019-34; OML 2019-64.

First, during the January 2 meeting, the Council did not substantively dis cuss the topic 
“Claims - (2) Property Damage” at all. Rather, Mayor Daniel Rourke, acting as chair of the 
meeting, read the notice topic aloud and the Council then voted to refer the matters to the law 
department. We find that the notice topic “Claims - (2) Property Damage” was sufficiently 
specific where the Council simply voted to refer the matters to the law department and did not 
substantively discuss the matters. See OML 2023-167 (finding notice topics sufficient and noting 
“that the Council did not engage in substantive discussion regarding any of the 25 topics on the 
agenda on which it took action”); see also OML 2022-206 (explaining that “we have stated that 
consent agenda topics, which do not require the public body’s discussion but instead are listed as 
perfunctory administrative tasks, do not need to include the same level of specificity as other 
notice topics”). Although including additional detail on the notice, for example the address of the 
properties at issue, may have been helpful to the public, we are satisfied that the topic as listed 
was sufficient to reasonably advise the public of the anticipated discussion. See G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 20(b); see also OML 2013-152.  
 
Next, with respect to the executive session notice topic and open
  session announcement, 
we find that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to include descriptive 
information identifying the specific Open Meeting Law complaint to be discussed. Additionally, 
we have consistently explained that the proper executive session purpose for discussing an Open 
Meeting Law complaint is G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1) (“Purpose 1”), not G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) 
(“Purpose 3”). See OML 2012-119 (“An Open Meeting Law complaint, when filed with a public 
body, is not litigation.”); OML 2019-57 (“Open Meeting Law complaints result in an 
administrative review process conducted by our office, rather than a proceeding before a judge or 
an adjudicatory hearing officer, and are therefore not considered litigation . . . .”).  Furthermore, 
by characterizing the Open Meeting Law complaint as “litigation,” the Council may 
inadvertently have created confusion regarding the nature of the discussion. See OML2024-168, 
n.8 (“By describing the January 11 and 18 Open Meeting Law complaint discussions as ‘strategy 
with respect to collective bargaining or litigation,’ which suggested a discussion under executive 
session Purpose 3, the Committee unnecessarily created confusion.”).  
 
III. The minutes of the Council’s December 19, 2023, and January 2, 9, and 16, 2024, 
meetings fail to summarize public comment received during the meetings and are 
insufficient. 
 
The first and second complaints allege that the minutes of Council meetings held on 
December 19, 2023, and January 2, 9, and 16, 2024, are insufficiently detailed. Specifically, the 
complaints allege that the minutes are insufficient where they identify the registered or other 
non-councilor speakers and note the agenda items on which they spoke but do not include a 
substantive summary of those speakers’ comments. Our office recently addressed the same 
allegation with respect to the minutes of different Council meetings in a determination resolving 
two different Open Meeting Law complaints filed with the Council by the Complainant. OML 
2024-189. For the same reasons as explained in OML 2024-189, which we incorporate by

 Although not raised in the complaints, we remind the Council that when v oting by roll

IV. We decline to review whether the Council responded to the Complainant’s January 2 
request for meeting minutes. 
 
The first complaint alleges that the Council failed to respond to a request the 
Complainant made for the minutes of the Council’s January 2 meeting. The Council’s January 2 
meeting was scheduled for 6:30 p.m. Based on information provided by the Complainant, we 
understand that on January 2 at 2:13 p.m. the Complainant sent an email requesting the minutes 
of the January 2 meeting. The Open Meeting Law requires that “minutes of an open session, if 
they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any 
person within 10 days.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). We decline to review whether the Council 
responded to a request for meeting minutes that could not have possibly existed at the time of the 
request because the meeting had not yet occurred. We note that the Council has a practice of 
posting its meeting minutes online and that the minutes of its January 2 meeting are available 
online.
9 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to provide sufficient notice of the executive session discussion held on January 2 and by 
creating insufficiently detailed minutes for meetings held on December 19, 2023, and January 2, 
9, and 16, 2024. We find that the Council did not otherwise violate the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged. We order the Council’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law 
and caution the Council that a determination by our office of similar violations in the future may 
be considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law. Additionally, we order the 
Council, within 45 days of the date of this letter, to amend the minutes of its meetings held on 
December 19, 2023, and January 2, 9, and 16, 2024, to summarize public comment received 
during the meetings.
