This office received a complaint from Patrick McPhee on January 11, 2023, and a second 
complaint on March 3, 2023, alleging that the Ashby Finance Committee (the “Committee”) 
violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The first complaint was originally filed 
with the Committee on or about December 20, 2022; the second complaint was originally filed 
with the Committee on or about January 4, 2023; and former Chair Alan Ewald1 responded to 
both complaints, on behalf of the Committee, by separate emails dated January 11, 2023, and 
January 25, 2023, respectively.  The first complaint alleges that the Committee i) discussed a 
topic that was not listed on the notice of the November 1, 2022, meeting; ii) deliberated outside 
of a properly posted meeting; and iii) failed to include in the minutes of the November 1, 2022, 
meeting a list of the documents used during that meeting.  The second complaint alleges that the 
Committee failed to create sufficient minutes and timely approve and provide minutes of the 
meeting held on December 20, 2022.  
  
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
discussing a topic that was not listed on the notice of the November 1, 2022, meeting, and by not 
providing draft open session minutes in a timely manner.  We find that the Committee did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaints, the responses to the complaints, and the complaints filed with

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Committee is a five-member public body; thus, three 
members constitute a quorum.  Currently, the Committee has no serving members because, on or 
about January 31, 2023, all members of the Committee resigned.   
 
On October 20, 2022, Eldon Garhart, who was Chair of the Committee at that time, sent 
an email to all Committee members informing them that the next Committee meeting was 
scheduled for November 1, 2022, and asking that any topics for the next meeting be sent to him 
by October 26, 2022.  Committee member Kathy Panagiotes responded with one item, which 
was that she had decided not to attend another public body meeting that was to take place on 
November 5, 2022. 
 
On October 24, 2022, Mr. Garhart sent another email to all Committee members in which 
he explained that he was unsuccessful in accessing weekly expense reports, asked each member 
to make a request for the reports, and suggested members could raise issues with any reports

did not want to assign specific members to attend other public body meetings.  Committee 
member Rebecca Walsh responded later that same day to all members and asked the Chair to call 
her so that she could update him on her conversation with the Town Administrator and asked that 
access to the weekly reports be added to the notice for the November 1, 2022, meeting. 
 
The Committee posted notice for a meeting to be held on Tuesday, November 1, 2022.  
The notice included the following five topics: “Vote on meeting minutes; Choose a Finance 
Committee member to represent the Finance Committee at the NMRSD school budget meetings;

On November 1, 2022, several hours prior to the Committee’s meeting, Mr. Garhart

separate responses to two of the Committee members, Mr. Garhart mentioned that “I and a 
consensus of the committee agree it should be addressed at the beginning.”

DISCUSSION 
 
I.  The Committee Violated the Open Meeting Law by Discussing a Topic 
that was Not Included on the Notice of the November 1, 2022, Meeting. 
 
A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not 
including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Notices must include 
the d
ate, time and place of the meeting, as well as a “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably 
anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  Id.  The list of topics shall have sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 
29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member 
of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s 
discussion.  See OML 2015-35.
3 
 
The first complaint alleges that the Committee discussed a topic, the appointment of a 
chai
r, that was not included on the notice for the meeting held on November 1, 2022.  The 
Committee suggests that this topic was unanticipated and therefore was properly discussed under 
the following topic that was listed on the notice – “Any business not adequately anticipated by 
the Finance Committee.”  A public body may discuss a topic not listed on a meeting notice if the 
topic was not anticipated by the chair 48 hours in advance of the meeting.  See OML 2017-2; 
OML 2013-87.  While a public body may consider a topic at a meeting that was not listed in the 
notice if it was not reasonably anticipated at least 48 hours before the meeting, we encourage 
public bodies to postpone discussion of topics that were not listed in the meeting notice until the 
topic may be included in a meeting notice, unless the circumstances demand immediate action by 
the public body.  See OML 2011-23; OML 2013-197.  We find here that the discussion regarding 
the appointment of a chair was reasonably anticipated where Mr. Garhart knew on October 28, 
2022, that he would need to resign from the Commission during the November 1, 2022, meeting.  
Since Mr. Garhart knew 48 hours prior to the November 1, 2022, meeting that a new chair would 
need to be appointed, such topic should have been listed on the notice.  We acknowledge that 
other Committee members did not know of Mr. Garhart’s resignation until the day of the 
meeting.  However, the Open Meeting Law requires that notices must include “a listing of topics 
that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b) 
(emphasis added).  Since Mr. Garhart was chair at the time of the November 1, 2022, meeting

II.  The Committee Created Sufficient Minutes of the November 1, 2022, and  
December 20, 2022, Meetings and Approved Those Minutes in a Timely 
Manner.  However, the Committee Failed to Provide Copies of Draft Open 
Session Minutes in a Timely Manner. 
  
 The complaints also allege that the Committee c reated insufficiently detailed minutes and 
failed to timely approve the minutes for the meetings held on November 1, 2022, and December 
20, 2022.  The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The meeting minutes must be reviewed 
and approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” means “within the 
next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can 
show good cause for further delay.”  See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.  Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2018-67; 
OML 2017-133.  
 
First, we find that the Committee approved the minutes of the November 1, 2022, and 
Dec
ember 20, 2022, at the next meeting, i.e., the November 1, 2022, minutes were approved at 
the November 29, 2022, meeting and the December 20, 2022, minutes were approved at the 
January 17, 2023, meeting.  As such, we find that the Committee approved these two sets of 
minutes in a timely manner and therefore did not violate the Open Meeting Law as the minutes 
were approved within the later of the next three Committee meetings or 30 days following each 
Committee meeting.  See OML 2023-4.  We note that the Open Meeting Law does not require 
that public bodies post their minutes to a website or that minutes be submitted to the Town Clerk, 
but rather that meeting minutes be made available within 10 days of a request.  See G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 22(c); OML 2013-103.4   
 
Next, the minutes of the November 1, 2022, and December 20, 2022, include the date, 
time and place of the meeting, the members who were present, a summary of the discussions, 
and the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including a record of all votes.

With respect to the allegation that the minutes do not include a list of documents, we 
ackno
wledge that the two sets of minutes do not contain such a list.  The Open Meeting Law 
does not define what it means for a document to be used at a meeting but, at a minimum, it is 
clear that where a document is physically present, verbally identified, and the contents are 
discussed by the members of a public body during an open meeting, it has been “used” for 
purposes of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2014-12; OML 2012-42.  After reviewing a video 
recording of the November 1, 2022, meeting, we find that, although the Board discussed minutes 
during the meeting, the documents were not physically present during the meeting.  Therefore, 
the Board was not required to list the documents in the minutes of the November 1, 2022, 
meeting and we find that those minutes are sufficient detailed.  It is not clear from the minutes of 
the December 20, 2022, meeting whether documents discussed during that meeting were 
physically present or verbally identified.  Since a video recording of that meeting is not available 
and the minutes do not provide clear evidence of whether documents were physically present or 
verbally identified, we cannot arrive at a formal finding of whether the minutes are sufficiently 
detailed.  Accordingly, we are constrained to find that the Board did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law and that the minutes of the December 20, 2022, meeting are sufficiently detailed.  
See OML 2018-50.  We remind the Board of its obligations under the law and that it is generally 
not enough that those documents are simply referenced in the body of the minutes or that the 
minutes include a digital link to the documents.  See OML 2019-29; OML 2018-70. 
 
With respect to the concerns raised in the complaints that documents were not provided 
to the
 complainant, we note that a request for meeting minutes that is directed to a public body 
will trigger the Open Meeting Law.  On the other hand, a request for minutes that is directed to a 
municipal employee, such as the Town Clerk, will likely trigger the Public Records Law.  See 
OML 2019-126; OML 2019-68.  A request for meeting minutes made under the Public Records 
Law requires a response within 10 business days, whereas the Open Meeting Law requires that 
public bodies respond to a request for open session minutes, whether approved or in draft form, 
within 10 calendar days.  See G.L. c. 66, § 10; G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (g).  Here, the Committee 
acknowledged that draft open session minutes of the December 20, 2022, meeting were not made 
available within 10 days of a request.  Accordingly, the Committee violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to timely respond to a request for minutes.  We note that minutes have since been 
provided to the complainant and posted to the municipal website. 
 
The Open Meeting Law does not govern a public body’s response to a request for records 
other than meeting minutes.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (e); OML Declination 8-19-13 
(Southwick Board of Selectmen).  Therefore, failure to timely respond to requests for documents 
other than minutes, such as emails or notes, concerns a potential violation of the Public Records 
Law, rather than the Open Meeting Law. 
 
III.   The Committee Did Not Deliberate Outside of a Properly Posted Meeting 
via Email on October 24, 2022, or November 1, 2022. 
 
Finally, the first complaint alleges that the Committee deliberated outside of a posted 
mee
ting.  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a public body be properly noticed 
and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
20(a)-(b), 21.  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public body with 
===PAGE 6===
6 
 
respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The law defines 
“del
iberation” as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including electronic 
mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction; 
provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of a meeting agenda, 
scheduling information or distribution of other procedural meeting [information] or the 
distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided than no 
opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” 
is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id.    
 
Although Mr. Garhart shared Committee member’s input that his resignation and 
disc
ussion of appointing a new chair should be “addressed at the beginning” of the November 1, 
2022, meeting with a quorum of the Committee, we find that this email communication did not 
constitute deliberation in violation of the Open Meeting Law where it simply concerned the order 
in which the Committee would discuss a specific topic, that is, whether the topic should be 
addressed at the beginning or end of the meeting.  See e.g., OML 2022-109 (Open Meeting Law 
does not prohibit public body from taking topics in a different order than listed on the notice); 
OML 2014-35 (once a meeting begins, a public body controls its own agenda and is not required 
to adhere to indicated times on a notice for the discussion of individual agenda items); see also 
OML 2016-7 (communication about adding a topic for discussion at a meeting is an 
administrative task that falls outside the definition of deliberation).  Although we do not find that 
the emails here constituted deliberation, we remind the Committee that discussions regarding 
procedural or administrative matters may relate to public business within a body’s jurisdiction, 
including whether the body should consider or take action on specific topics during a meeting.  
See OML 2013-27; OML 2011-38.  The Open Meeting Law does not carve out an exception to 
the definition of “deliberation” for procedural discussions among public body members about 
how its meetings should be run.  See 2017-91.  We have previously found discussions 
concerning the leadership of a public body and the process by which it would elect members to 
those positions to be matters of public business that should be reserved for an open meeting.  See 
OML 2015-3; OML 2014-108.  
 
With respect to the allegation in the complaint that the Committee also improperly 
deli
berated by email on October 24, 2022, we find that these communications do not constitute 
deliberation where the matters discussed either fell outside the Committee’s jurisdiction as they 
did not involve the public body’s exercise of governmental business, policy, or administration, or 
were minor administrative housekeeping matters.  See OML 2021-112; OML 2018-1; OML 
2011-38.   We recognize that it is sometimes difficult to determine whether, under the 
circumstances a given communication constitutes deliberation under the Open Meeting Law, and 
therefore our office specifically cautions public bodies on the use of electronic communications.  
See OML 2017-88; OML 2014-80.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by discussing a topic that was not listed on the notice of the November 1, 2022, meeting, and by 
not providing draft open session minutes within 10 days of a request.  We order the Committee’s 
immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
 
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions
