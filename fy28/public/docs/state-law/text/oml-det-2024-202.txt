RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Samee: 
 
 This office received a complaint from Bruce Friedman on August 23, 2024, alleging that 
the Malden Licensing Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.
1  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on July 22, and you responded, on behalf 
of the Board, by letter dated August 13.  The complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely 
respond to a request for meeting minutes and created insufficient minutes.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
providing only a partial response to the request for meeting minutes.  We decline to review 
whether the Board created insufficient minutes for the reasons discussed herein.  In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the 
complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the Board’s supplemental response 
dated September 10.   
 
Public bodies must create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings.  G.L. c. 30A,    
§ 22(a).  The minutes of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, 
shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  If 
minutes do not yet exist at the time of a request, the public body is still required to respond to the

request within 10 days with an explanation of whether the minutes do or do not exist in either 
approved or draft form.  See OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; OML 2015-173.2 
 
On July 9, Mr. Friedman emailed the Board and requested minutes of Board meetings 
held 
between 2010 and 2025.  Mr. Friedman also requested notices, videos, or transcripts of, and 
documents and other exhibits, photographs, recordings or maps used during Board meetings held 
between 2010 and 2025.  As previously explained in OML 2024-146, the Open Meeting Law 
does not govern a public body’s response to a request for records other than meeting minutes.  
See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (e); OML 2024-146; OML 2023-145; OML Declination 8-19-13 
(Southwick Board of Selectmen).  Therefore, failure to timely respond to requests for documents 
other than minutes concerns a potential violation of the Public Records Law, rather than the 
Open Meeting Law, and we thus decline to review this allegation.   
 
With respect to the request for minutes, a public body must make minutes of an open 
session available within 10 days of a request, if they exist.  However, the Open Meeting Law 
does not specify in what format minutes must be made available.  Therefore, directing a 
requester to a website where the requested minutes are posted and accessible complies with the 
requirement to make the minutes “available.”  See OML 2024-24; OML 2022-205.  On July 19, 
the Board responded to the request and advised that requested documents “may be viewed at 
WWW.cityofmalden.org/AgendaCenter/Licensingboard-7.”  Here, the Board timely responded 
to the request for minutes as the response was provided within 10 days, and directed the 
complainant to the municipal website where the minutes were available.  However, the 
municipal website only contains minutes from 2016 to the present.  The Board did not provide 
any information with respect to the availability of minutes from 2010 through 2015.  Therefore, 
although the Board responded by properly referring the requester to the eight years’ worth of 
meeting minutes that were available online, we are constrained to find that the Board violated the 
Open Meeting Law by only providing a partial response to the request for minutes when it failed 
to explain whether minutes of meetings from 2010 to 2015 did or did not exist.  We note that by 
email dated September 10, the Board advised Mr. Friedman that it had located minutes of 
meetings from 2011 to 2016, and that such minutes were available for review.  We commend the 
Board for following up with the complainant and advising him of the availability of additional 
minutes. 
 
We must determine whether the Board’s
  violation was, as the complainant urges, 
intentional.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public 
body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02. 
An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of 
the law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open 
Meeting Law.  Id.  Here, we have not previously warned the Board against the violation found 
here, nor do we find other evidence that would support a finding of an intentional violation by 
the Board.  We therefore decline to find that the violation was intentional.

2021-133; OML Declination 4-22-15 (Natick Economic Development Committee); OML 
Declination 3-20-12 (Wilmington Board of Assessors).  We remind the Board that the Open 
Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, 
including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members present or 
absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used 
at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of 
all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).   
 
When reviewing minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for 
subs
tantial compliance with the accuracy requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.  By 
substantial compliance, we mean that the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so 
that a member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a 
clear understanding of what occurred.  See OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a 
summary of the discussion on each topic, a transcript is not required, and the minutes do not 
need to include every remark or opinion presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55.     
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
providing only a partial response to the request for meeting minutes.  We order immediate and 
future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations 
could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  In addition, we order the Board, within 
10 days of the date of this letter, to provide Mr. Friedman with an explanation of whether 
minutes of meetings from the year 2010 do or do not exist.   
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
