This office received a complaint from Stephen Granlund on January 14, 2026, alleging 
that the Ware Hospital Review Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on November 13, 
2025, and the you responded by letter dated December 5, 2025.
1  The complaint alleges that the 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when (1) it failed to timely approve meeting 
minutes; (2) it approved minutes before they were written; (3) it met without a quorum, as 
members’ conflict of interest certificates had expired; (4) the Chair had “ex parte” meetings with 
executives of Baystate Health; and (5) the Chair received payments from Baystate Health until 
September 2020. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when 
it failed to approve minutes in a timely manner.  We decline to review the other allegations of the 
complaint.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the complaint, the Committee’s 
response, and the complainant’s request for further review filed with our office.  We also 
reviewed documents attached to the parties’ filings, including minutes of a meeting the 
Committee held on October 16, 2025.

DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Committee failed to timely approve meeting minutes. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body 
“create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth 
the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each 
subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the 
actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The Law 
further states that “[m]inutes of all open sessions shall be created and approved in a timely 
manner.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner,” for purposes of approval of open session 
meeting minutes, means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, 
whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 
29.11; see OML 2024-67; OML 2023-179; OML 2018-48.

The Open Meeting Law imposes a continuing obligation to maintain minutes of all 
meetings.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2025-35; OML 2020-69 (reviewing a complaint 
regarding a committee’s failure to approve meeting minutes for a three-year period); OML 2018-
115 (reviewing a complaint regarding the failure to create and maintain meeting minutes for 
meetings held four years prior).  The alleged violation continued each day until the minutes were 
created and approved.  See OML 2025-35; OML 2024-41; OML 2023-31.  A complaint alleging 
untimely approval must be filed within 30 days of the meeting at which the minutes were 
approved.  See OML 2025-96.

October 6, 2021, were first approved at the next meeting, held on December 1, 2021.  Therefore,

II. We decline to review the remaining allegations of the complaint. 
 
The Division of Open Government’s review concerns compliance with the Open Meeting 
Law.  We decline to review allegations that the Chair met individually with executives of 
Baystate Health or that he received payments from Baystate Health, as these allegations fall 
outside the Open Meeting Law.  The Open Meeting Law governs only the actions of a public 
body, not those of members acting in their individual capacity in a way that does not reach a 
quorum of the public body.  See G.L. c. 30A, §18.  Moreover, to the extent that your complaint 
alleges a conflict of interest on the part of the Chair, we decline to review this allegation as the 
Division of Open Government does not enforce rules against conflicts of interest.  The Open 
Meeting Law also does not revoke membership in a public body upon expiration of a conflict of 
interest certificate.  Even if less than a quorum had discussed a matter within the public body’s 
jurisdiction, that discussion would not be subject to the Open Meeting Law, as noted above.   
Finally, we decline to review the allegation that minutes were approved before they were 
written.  The Open Meeting Law does not govern the method for approving minutes.  As such, 
public bodies may choose the method for approving their minutes, including approval by the 
public body chair or another designee alone, by majority vote of the public body, or by consent 
of the body (approved unless there is an objection).  See OML 2024-186; OML 2023-204.  
Although the approval of the October 16 minutes was in the form of a vote held on October 16 
(the Committee’s last meeting), the vote in practice delegated authority to member Nancy Talbot 
to prepare and finalize the minutes after the meeting.  We decline to review an allegation that this 
violated the Open Meeting Law, as the Law does not prohibit this practice.4   
CONCLUSION 
 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee  violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to create and timely approve meeting minutes.  As the Committee has been disbanded, 
and minutes of all meetings at issue have been created and approved, we order no remedial 
action.   
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee.  Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions.
