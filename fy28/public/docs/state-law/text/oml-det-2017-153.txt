This office received four related complaints from Patrick Fliggins alleging that the 
Swansea Recreation Commission (the "Commission") violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25. The first two complaints were filed with the Commission on September 26, 
2016, and the Commission responded to both by letter dated October 7, 2016. These complaints 
were then filed with our office on October 11, 2016. The third and fourth complaints were filed 
with the Commission on October 4, 2016. The Commission responded to these complaints on 
October 24, 2016, and they were filed with our office on that same date. In his complaints, Mr. 
Higgins alleges that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law by: (i) failing to properly

We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter. Following our 
review, we find that the Commission failed to appropriately respond to the complainant's request 
for executive session minutes. However, we find that the Commission did not violate the law in 
the other ways alleged. In reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaints, the 
Commission's response, and the request for further review filed with our office. We spoke with 
the Commission's legal counsel by telephone on April 4 and 25, June 23, and August 1, 2017.'

DISCUSSION 
I. The Commission Failed to Appropriately Respond to the Request for Executive 
Session Minutes. 
A public body must respond to a request for executive session meeting minutes within 10 
days. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). Upon receiving the request, the public body must review the 
executive session minutes to determine whether the purpose for the executive session continues 
to require non-disclosure. Id. This review must occur at the public body's next regular meeting, 
or within 30 days, whichever comes first. Id In such circumstances, the body should respond to 
the requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her that it is conducting this review. See OML 
2013-99.3 
The complaints allege that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law's 
requirements with respect to executive session minutes in several ways. First, the complaints 
allege that the Commission failed to respond to the two requests within the appropriate time 
frame. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f). The complainant sent his first request on September 14, 2016. 
The Commission held a meeting two days later, on September 16, 2016, and it does not appear 
that the Commission reviewed the requested minutes during that meeting. The complainant then 
sent a second modified requested on September 20, 2016. Again, the Commission held a 
meeting on October 4, 2016, and it appears that the Commission did not review the requested 
minutes during that meeting either. Instead, the Commission responded by letter dated October 
3, 2016 and eventually sent a second letter on October 31, 2016. The Commission's legal 
counsel stated to our office that the minutes at issue had been recently reviewed at the times the 
September 2016 requests were made. Nevertheless, the Commission failed to respond within 10 
days of each request. This violated the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2013-99. 
Next, the complaints allege that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law by 
assessing a fee for the search and segregation of minutes. Indeed, we have stated in a prior 
determination letter that a public body may not assess such fees for search and segregation. See 
OML 2015-164. Rather, it is incumbent on public bodies to periodically review executive 
session minutes to determine their suitability for release. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1).4 While the 
scope of the September 20, 2016 request was large, the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law by assessing the fee found in the October 3, 2016 letter. See OML 2015-164. We 
commend the Commission for sending the October 31, 2016 letter stating that the fee estimate 
had been reduced to $84.00 for the cost of providing copies, however we note that the both the 
September 14 and 20, 2016 requests stated that the complainant sought solely to inspect and 
review the minutes, not to obtain copies of them. Further, the Commission's October 3 and 31, 
2016 letters are internally inconsistent, as they state that the executive session minutes are not 
subject to release and yet also state a fee for their production. We understand from conversation

While not raised in the complaints, we note some additional issues with regard to the

II. The Board's Meeting Notices were Sufficiently Detailed. 
A public body must post notice 48 hours in advance of a meeting and include the date, 
time, and location of the meeting, and "a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates 
will be discussed at the meeting." G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Those topics must be listed with 
"sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting." 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). Generally, "sufficient specificity" requires that a reasonable 
member of the public could understand the anticipated nature of the discussion based on the 
notice. See OML 2017-62: OML 2015-35. 
The complaints allege that the Board's meeting notices lacked sufficient detail. As 
examples, the complaints point to the references to "Movie Night" and "Fall Festival" on the 
notices. Further, the complaints argue that the Board should have provided more information 
about what donations, entertainment, and vendors would be discussed during the October 3, 2016 
meeting. We disagree, and we conclude that the Commission satisfied its obligations under the 
Open Meeting Law. While additional information, if available, may have been useful to the 
public, we find that the notices were sufficiently detailed. See OML 2017-82. 
III. The Commission Approved its Meeting Minutes in a Timely Manner. 
Minutes of all open sessions shall be approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). 
The Open Meeting Law does not provide a definition of "timely manner," but we have 
recommended that minutes be approved at the next meeting whenever possible. See OML 2015-
75. For bodies that meet regularly—that is, weekly or every other week—we have concluded 
that a delay of two to three months in approving minutes does not comply with the law. See 
OML 2016-118. 
Here, the complaints allege that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to time approve meeting minutes when it approved the minutes of its August 9, 2016 
meeting during its September 20, 2016 meeting. Because the Commission approved the minutes 
within approximately six weeks, we find that the Commission satisfied its obligations under the 
Open Meeting Law. See OML 2017-137.

CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law. We order the Commission's immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting 
Law's requirements, and we note that similar future violations may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law. 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Commission. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any 
questions.
