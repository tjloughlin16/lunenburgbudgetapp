This office received a complaint from Stephen Dewhurst on May 8 alleging that the 
Carver Police Station Advisory Building Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.1 The complaint was originally filed with the Committee

In his complaint, Mr. Dewhurst alleges that the Committee posted insufficiently detailed

posting an insufficiently detailed notice for its March 28 meeting. We decline to review any 
allegations regarding meetings prior to March 28 for the reasons discussed below. In reaching 
this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Committee’s response to the

FACTS
We find the facts as follows. During a February 27, 2018, meeting, the Board of 
Selectmen created the Committee to assist the Town in replacing its police station building, 
which is located at 112 Main Street. The Committee consists of seven members, including 
the Police Chief, a police officer, one member of the Board of Selectman, the Town’s 
Director of Facilities, a real estate developer, and two citizens. The Committee met 
approximately twice per month between March 2018 and April 2019.
During the Committee’s first meeting on March 8, 2018, it discussed three possible 
locations for a new police station - around or behind the existing police station on Main 
Street, behind the EMS building on Main Street, or near a playground behind the library on 
Center Street.
During an October 16, 2018 meeting, the Committee discussed three building designs 
for a new police station to be located on Center Street. The Committee agreed that the third 
design, which would include building a station horizontally on Center Street with public 
parking to the left and police access to the right, was the better long-term option. However, 
this design would require removal of the playground that was currently on the site.
At its next meeting on November 6, 2018, the Committee reviewed nine plans for a 
new police station. Three of the plans were for a new building to be located near the existing 
police station on Main Street; two plans were for a new building on Center Street; and the 
remaining four plans detailed either a one- or two-story building. The Committee voted for a 
one-story building to be located on Center Street. The Committee then discussed relocating 
and upgrading the Center Street playground.
The Committee then held a meeting on December 3, 2018, at which approximately 80 
residents attended. The Committee presented and discussed its recommendation that the 
Town build a one-story police station on Center Street, currently the location of the 
playground. The Committee explained that the playground would be upgraded and relocated 
near Town Hall.
During its December 4, 2018 and December 20, 2018, meetings, the Committee 
continued to review and discuss designs for a new police station on Center Street and 
relocation of the existing playground.
On March 25 at 11:28 AM, the Committee posted notice for a meeting to be held on 
March 28 at 8:00 AM. The notice listed nine topics, including “Review project financing to 
date for building and additional funds needed to complete site work, demo, out-building, and 
fit-out”; and “Next Meeting and any other business not anticipated within 48 hours.”
The March 28 meeting was held as planned. The Committee approved meeting 
minutes; discussed layouts of local area police stations, relocation of the Center Street 
playground and demolition of the current police station on Main Street; and reviewed various

DISCUSSION
I. We Decline to Address Any Alleged Open Meeting Law Violations that
Occurred Prior to the Committee’s March 28 Meeting.
The complaint alleges that the Committee posted insufficiently detailed meeting 
notices and created inaccurate minutes of meetings held on March 8, 2018; October 16, 2018; 
November 6, 2018; and November 20, 2018. Complaints alleging violations of the Open 
Meeting Law must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation. See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b). If the alleged violation could not reasonably have been known at the 
time it occurred, then the complaint must be filed within 30 days of the date it should 
reasonably have been discovered. See 940 CMR 29.05(3). When an alleged violation occurs 
during an open meeting, the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable at the time it occurs. 
See OML 2014-85; OML 2012-52.3
Here, the March, October and November meetings all took place entirely in open 
session. Therefore, any complaint concerning discussions occurring during the March 8, 
2018; October 16, 2018; November 6, 2018; and November 20, 2018 meetings should have 
been filed with the Committee within 30 days of each of those meetings. See G.L. c. 30A,
§ 23(b). Moreover, the minutes of the March 8, 2018, meeting were approved on March 22, 
2018; the minutes of the October 16, 2018, meeting were approved on November 6, 2018; the 
minutes of the November 6, 2018, meeting were approved on November 20, 2018; and the 
minutes of the November 20, 2018, meeting were approved on December 4, 2018. You state 
that you delayed in filing the complaint because you were working with the Committee to try 
and resolve these issues. When the Committee failed to resolve the issues, you filed your 
Open Meeting Law complaint. We commend your efforts to resolve this matter outside of the 
complaint process. See OML Declination 3-11-15 (Fairhaven Board of Selectmen).
However, the law does not toll the time for filing a complaint in situations like the one you 
describe. Because the complaint was filed with the Committee on April 8, five to nine 
months after the meetings at issue, and four to eleven months after the minutes at issue were 
approved, we find that this portion of the complaint is untimely and we decline to review it. 
See OML Declination 2-1-17 (West Bridgewater Board of Water Boarders); OML 
Declination 11-21-16 (Middleton Planning Board); OML Declination 12-15-14 (Ware 
Conservation Board).
II. The Committee’s March 28 Meeting Notice Contained Insufficient Detail.
A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not 
including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Notices must include 
“a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” Id.

The list of topics shall have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues 
to be discussed at the meeting. 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). We generally consider a topic 
sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and 
understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion. See OML 2018-13; OML 
2015-35.
The complaint alleges that one of the topics on the March 28 meeting notice was 
insufficiently detailed. Specifically, the topic “Review project financing to date for building 
and additional funds needed to complete site work, demo, out-building, and fit-out” failed to 
specify which demolition project would be discussed during the meeting. When planning to 
discuss a specific piece of property, public bodies should include sufficient information in the 
meeting notice to identify the property, either by listing the specific address, bordering streets, 
intersections or landmarks. See OML 2010-2. During the March 28 meeting, the Board 
discussed project financing for a new police station, demolition of the current police station 
on Main Street, and relocation of the Center Street playground, which would involve . 
demolition of the existing playground. Because the Committee had been discussing two 
property locations, Main Street and Center Street, over the course of the past several 
meetings, we find that it should have included on the notice additional detail about which 
property its discussions regarding demolition pertained to. See OML 2013-39; OML 2012- 
52. The Committee’s failure to include such detail violated the Open Meeting Law.
Finally, we note that public bodies may include a topic in their notices for the 
discussion of matters not reasonably anticipated by the chair. See OML 2015-115; OML 
2015-127. Here, the Committee indicated explicitly, with the heading “any other business 
not anticipated within 48 hours,” that certain time was reserved for topics not anticipated by 
the chair and thus we find that the Committee’s use of this language on the March 28 meeting 
notice did not violate the law’s requirements.4 See OML 2013-13. We also acknowledge that 
the Committee did not have any discussions under this topic during the March 28 meeting.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting 
Law by posting an insufficiently detailed notice for its March 28 meeting. We order the 
Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and we caution 
that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Committee 
or with our office. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any 
questions regarding this letter.
