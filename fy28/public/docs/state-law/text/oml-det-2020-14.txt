Dear Attorney Goldberg:
This office received a complaint from Owen Murphy on June 20, 2019, alleging that the 
Weston Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The 
complaint was originally filed with the Board on April 19, 2019, and you responded on behalf of 
the Board by letter dated May 15, 2019. In his complaint, Mr. Murphy alleges that the Board did 
not timely respond to his request for 11 sets of executive session minutes which all related to the 
Boston Properties development project at 133 Boston Post Road in Weston. Additionally, after 
reviewing the released executive session minutes, Mr. Murphy alleges that the original executive 
session discussions were not held under a permissible purpose.1
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
meeting in executive session without a permissible purpose. With respect to the timely response 
allegation, we find that the complainant’s request for minutes fell outside the Open Meeting Law 
and therefore find no violation in that regard. In reaching a determination, we reviewed the 
complaint, the Board’s response and the request for further review. We also viewed a video of 
the May 14 meeting in which the Board reviewed the complaint. Finally, we reviewed a sample 
of the executive session minutes at issue, which you provided to us.2

FACTS
We find the facts as follows. For several years, starting approximately in November of 
2016, a developer known as Boston Properties sought local approval from the Weston Zoning 
Board of Appeals (“ZBA”) for a comprehensive permit to build a large mixed-use residential and 
commercial development in Weston on the property at 133 Boston Post Road.3 The Town of 
Weston holds a development restriction on the property at 133 Boston Post Road in Weston from 
a previous settlement.
The Boston Properties proposal was reviewed and discussed with the Planning Board, 
Housing Trust, Select Board, School Committee and the ZBA. Relevant here, the Select Board 
met in executive session eleven times under the following notice topic: “Executive Session, 
Exemption 6, purchase, exchange, or value of an interest in real property related to Boston 
Properties.” These meetings were held on September 12, 2018, April 3, 2018, March 27, 2018, 
February 13, 2018, October 26, 2017, October 10, 2017, September 12, 2017, August 21, 2017, 
May 23, 2017, March 28, 2017, and November 3, 2016.
In February 2019, Boston Properties informed the Select Board that it wished to 
withdraw its proposal to build residential units and additional commercial space, citing uncertain 
demand for the commercial portion of the property, which had been the primary driver of the 
project. Boston Properties asked to withdraw its application for a permit which was before the 
ZBA and the ZBA granted the request without prejudice on March 11.
On February 21, Mr. Murphy wrote to the Town Clerk to request the eleven sets of Select 
Board executive session minutes which related to the proposed Boston Properties development 
project at 133 Boston Post Road. The Board met on February 26 and March 12, but did not 
review the requested minutes until its March 19 meeting. On March 20, the Board sent nine out 
of the eleven sets of executive session minutes to Mr. Owen. The September 12, 2018, and 
November 3, 2016, minutes were withheld in their entirety and a separate topic in the October 
10, 2017, minutes was redacted.
DISCUSSION
I. The Complainant’s Request for Minutes Did Not Fall Under the Open Meeting Law.
A request for meeting minutes under the Open Meeting Law must be made to the public 
body. The Open Meeting Law requires that “[mjinutes of an open session, if they exist and 
whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 
days.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). Under the Open Meeting Law, a public body has 10 calendar days 
from the date a request is received to provide a response. If minutes do not yet exist at the time 
of a request, the public body is still required to respond to the request within 10 calendar days 
with an explanation of whether the minutes do or do not exist in either approved or draft form. 
The Public Records Law, on the other hand, allows 10 business days for responding to requests 
for records.

We have stated that a request for minutes that is directed to a public body will fall within 
the Open Meeting Law. A request for minutes that is directed to a clerk or other employee 
serving as Records Access Officer will likely fall under the Public Records Law. See OML 
2019-125; OML 2019-68; OML 2018-32.4 Here, the complainant emailed his request for minutes 
to the Town Clerk, only, without any indication that it was intended for the Board nor a request 
that it be forwarded to the Board. We therefore find that the request for meeting minutes was not 
a request that fell under the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c), (g)(2), and did not impose 
upon the Board the obligation to review its executive session minutes for possible public release.
II. The Board’s Use of Purpose 6 to Discuss the Property at 133 Boston Post Road was
Improper.5
All meetings of a public body must be conducted in an open session, unless a closed-door 
executive session is convened for one of ten permissible purposes. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). 
When meeting in executive session, a public body may only address subjects related to the 
narrow executive session purpose the public body cited when entering executive session. See 
District Attorney for the Northwestern District v. Selectmen of Sunderland. 11 Mass. App. Ct. 
663, 666 (1981) (executive session may not be used as a blanket reason to deliberate on matters 
other than those pertaining to the specific purpose for which executive session was called).
One such statutory purpose for executive session is to “consider the purchase, exchange, 
lease or value of real property if the chair declares that an open meeting may have a detrimental 
effect on the negotiating position of the public body.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6) (“Purpose 6”). 
Purpose 6 is intended to preserve confidentiality in negotiating the value of the property to be 
purchased, exchanged or leased to avoid putting the public body at a disadvantage in its 
negotiations for the property. See District Attv. for the Plymouth Dist. v. Selectmen of 
Middleborough. 395 Mass. 629, 631 (1985); OML 2016-50; OML 2019-10. We construe 
Purpose 6 narrowly to permit a public body to meet in executive session to protect its negotiating 
position when seeking to purchase, exchange, or lease real estate, or to discuss the value of real 
estate, where it has a negotiating position relative to that property. See Allen v. Board of 
Selectmen of Belmont. 58 Mass. App. Ct. 715, 719-721 (2003); OML 2018-49. General 
discussions regarding real property or the use of real property, where there is no negotiating 
position to protect, do not fall within the executive session purpose. See OML 2016-93, OML 
2015-167.
Here, although the Town of Weston did not own the property at 133 Boston Post Road, 
the Town had obtained a development restriction on the property from a settlement agreement 
that predated the executive sessions at issue. The Board urges us to find that the Town had a 
negotiating position to protect regarding its development restriction vis-a-vis Boston Properties 
and its proposed development. However, we need not reach this issue, as we find, as a threshold

matter, that the Board’s discussions were not about the “purchase, exchange, lease or value of 
real property.”6 Rather, from our review of the executive session minutes, we find that the Board 
discussed how the proposed development at 133 Boston Post Road could help the Town reach its 
10% affordable housing minimum, in accordance with General Laws Chapter 40B. Since the 
Board’s discussion did not relate to the purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property, we 
find that the Board entered executive session without a proper purpose. We therefore find that 
the Board violated the Open Meeting Law.
III. We Decline to Find the Board’s Violation of the Open Meeting Law to be Intentional.
Finally, we must determine whether the Board’s violation was, as the complainant urges, 
intentional. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c). An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public 
body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].” 940 CMR 29.02. 
An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of 
the law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open 
Meeting Law. Id. Here, we had not previously warned the Board against the violation found here 
as of the time they took place, nor do we find other evidence that would support a finding of an 
intentional violation. We therefore decline to find that the violation was intentional.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
entering executive session without a valid executive session purpose. We order the Board’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution the Board that a 
determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the Open Meeting Law.
If the Board has not already done so, we order it to release all eleven sets of executive 
session minutes that relate to discussion of the Boston Properties project to the public. However, 
this order does not extend to the portions of the October 10, 2017, August 21, 2017, May 23, 
2017, and November 3, 2016, executive session minutes that were convened under a separate 
executive session purpose, as we take no position on the permissibility of the Board continuing 
to withhold minutes on topics unrelated to Boston Properties.
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any questions.
