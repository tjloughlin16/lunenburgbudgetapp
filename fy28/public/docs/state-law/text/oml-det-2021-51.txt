 RE:        Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Howayeck: 
 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on February 3, 2021, alleging that 
the Fall River Board of Park Commissioners (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. 
c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on February 26, 2021, and 
you responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated March 17, 2021.  The complaint alleges 
that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to announce the name of each member 
who participated remotely in the Board’s February 3, 2021, meeting and by posting an 
insufficiently detailed notice for the February 3, 2021, meeting.   
 
We resolve this matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review, and the notice and video recording of the Board 
meeting held on February 3, 2021.1  We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law in the ways alleged.     
 
On March 10, 2020, Governor Charles Baker declared a state of emergency in response

19”).  See Executive Order No. 591 (Mar. 10, 2020).  On March 12, 2020, Governor Baker 
issued an executive order temporarily suspending certain provisions of the Open Meeting Law

during the state of emergency.  See Order Suspending Certain Provisions of the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, § 20 (Mar. 12, 2020) (the “Executive Order”).  The Executive Order 
temporarily suspends the requirements under the Open Meeting Law and the Attorney General’s 
Open Meeting Law regulations that a quorum of a public body, including the chair, be physically 
present at the meeting location and allows all members of a public body to participate in a 
meeting remotely.  See OML 2020-82.2  All other provisions of the law and regulations 
regarding remote participation remain in effect, including the requirement that “[a]t the start of 
the meeting, the chair shall announce the name of any member who will be participating 
remotely.  This information shall also be recorded in the meeting minutes.”  940 CMR 
29.10(7)(b). 
 
On February 3, 2021, the Board held a meeting in which all five members participated 
remotely via Zoom.  At the beginning of the meeting, Chair Joseph DaSilva announced that the 
meeting “will be held virtually” and then asked for a roll call of the members who were present.  
Each member, including the Chair, then announced his or her own name.  We find that the 
combination of the Chair’s announcement that the meeting was being held virtually followed by 
a roll call in which each Board member stated his or her name clearly and effectively conveyed 
to the public the identity of all Board members who were participating remotely, and fulfilled the 
transparency goals of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2020-168.  Therefore, we do not find a 
violation of the Open Meeting Law.   
 
Where this office previously explained in another determination on a complaint filed by 
the same complainant, and now holds again, that a public body is not in violation of the Open 
Meeting Law when it announces that the meeting is being held virtually and then completes a 
roll call, we discourage the complainant from filing Open Meeting Law complaints containing 
such allegations in the future.  Filing complaints containing frivolous allegations wastes both 
municipal and Commonwealth resources. 
 
Next, we consider whether the February 3, 2021, meeting notice was sufficiently detailed.  
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at least 48 
hours prior to such meeting” and that the notice include “the date, time and place of such 
meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the 
meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are required to list topics on a notice with 
“sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1) (b).  We generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a 
reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of 
the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35; OML 2012-71.    
 
Here, the Board duly posted notice for its February 3, 2021, meeting and the notice listed 
six topics, including Roll Call of Commissioners, Acceptance of Minutes from January 25, 2021, 
Meeting, Citizen Input, New Business, Commissioner Inquiries, and Adjournment.  Under the 
topic of New Business, the notice listed three sub-headings, including Proposed Cathy Assad 
Playground design/PARC grant, Request for filming at Oak Grove Cemetery, and Architect re:

Oak Grove Cemetery Expansion plans.  The complaint does not identify any specific topics that 
are insufficiently detailed.  Complainants must allege violations with a degree of specificity, as 
our office will not conduct broad audits of public bodies based on generalized allegations.  See 
OML Declination 3-20-12 (Wilmington Board of Assessors).  Nevertheless, we find that the 
topics listed on the notice for the Board’s February 3, 2021, meeting provided the public with 
sufficient information and reflected the actual discussions held during the meeting.  See OML 
2018-22.  First, the Board included the specific minutes that it anticipated approving during the 
meeting.  See OML 2020-34; OML 2017-92.  Second, while we have stated that boilerplate 
topics, such as “Old Business” or “New Business,” are too vague to offer the public an adequate 
understanding of the anticipated topics to be discussed at that particular meeting, if the chair 
anticipates discussing a topic under “Old Business” or “New Business” more than 48 hours prior 
to the meeting, then that specific topic must be listed in the meeting notice.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 
see OML 2015-127; See OML 2016-76; OML 2013-168; OML 2013-14.  We find that the Board 
did that here as the notice listed three specific topics under New Business.  The Open Meeting 
Law does not require public bodies to list all tangential topics related to a discussion nor does the 
law require a public body to anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, 
only that it identify the discussion topic with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public 
of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); 
OML 2015-73; OML 2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38.   
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
