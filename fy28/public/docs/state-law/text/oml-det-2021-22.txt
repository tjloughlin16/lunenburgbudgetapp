 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints 
 
Dear Attorney Rennard: 
 
This office received five complaints from Heather Famico on January 21, 2021, alleging 
that the Salem Licensing Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The first four complaints were originally filed with the Board on August 23, 2020, and 
you responded, on behalf of the Board, by email dated September 17, 2020.  The fifth complaint 
was originally filed with the Board on November 1, 2020, and you responded, on behalf of the 
Board, by email dated November 23, 2020.1  The complaints generally allege that the Board 
posted insufficiently detailed meeting notices, failed to provide copies of documents, failed to 
post meeting minutes to a website, and failed to update its webpage with a current list of 
members. 
 
We resolve this matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaints, the Board’s responses to the complaints, the complaints 
filed with our office requesting further review, and after speaking with the complainant by 
telephone on February 12, 2021.  We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law 
in the ways alleged.

The first complaint alleges that the Board’s “agenda items are not meaningful, clear or 
specific.”  Specifically, the complaint alleges that an “‘amendment’ to an entertainment license” 
did “not specify current hours, nor what is being requested.”  The complaint does not identify 
any specific notices that are insufficiently detailed and we will not conduct broad audits of a 
public body based on generalized allegations.  See OML 2016-57.2  However, we note that the 
notice of the Board’s June 5, 2020, meeting included an amendment to an entertainment license 
to allow live music on an outdoor patio.3  The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an 
emergency, “a public body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such 
meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice 
must be printed in a legible, easily understandable format and must contain the date, time and 
place of the meeting, as well as a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed.  Id.  The list of topics shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public 
of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a 
topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and 
understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.   
 
Here, the Board’s June 5, 2020, meeting notice included the identity of the specific 
applicant for the entertainment license, the specific type of application to be reviewed, and the 
address that was the subject of the application to be considered by the Board.  See OML 2016-
167; OML 2016-90; OML 2015-196; OML 2014-155; OML 2011-11.  The Open Meeting Law 
does not require public bodies to list all tangential topics related to a discussion nor does the law 
require a public body to anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only 
that it identify the discussion topic with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of 
the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 
2015-73; OML 2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38.  We find that the Board did not violate 
the Open Meeting Law with respect to the sufficiency of the entertainment license topic on the 
June 5, 2020, meeting notice.   
 
The remaining complaints allege that the Board failed to provide copies of documents, 
failed to post meeting minutes to a website, and failed to update its webpage with a current list of 
members.  None of these allegations constitute violations of the Open Meeting Law.  The Open 
Meeting Law governs requests for meeting minutes, only.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22.  Requests for 
other documents are governed by the Public Records Law.  See G.L. c. 66, § 10.  Accordingly, 
we decline to review an allegation that concerns a potential violation of the Public Records Law, 
rather than the Open Meeting Law.  See OML Declination 7-16-20 (Greater Lowell Technical 
School Committee); OML Declination 8-9-13 (Southwick Board of Selectmen). 
  
Moreover, the Open Meeting Law does not require that public bodies post their minutes 
to a website or that minutes be submitted to the Town Clerk, but rather that meeting minutes be

take this opportunity to remind the Board that the Open Meeting Law requires public bodies to 
create and approve meeting minutes, whether for open or executive session, in a timely manner,

approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2018-67; OML 2017-133.  
 
Finally, the Open Meeting Law does not require that public bodies maintain a list of 
current members on its webpage.  Therefore, even if true, this allegation would not constitute a 
violation of the Open Meeting Law.  As such, we decline to review this matter.  See OML 
Declination 1-25-16 (Nahant Housing Authority Board of Commissioners); OML Declination 5-
21-12 (Hampden-Wilbraham Regional School Committee).  
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Board or with 
our office.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions
