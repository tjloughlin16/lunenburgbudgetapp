This office received a complaint from Kimberly Wetherell on February 21, 2023, and 
another complaint on April 24, 2023, alleging that the Peru Select Board (the “Board”) violated 
the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.

The first complaint alleges that the Board posted an insufficiently specific notice and approved 
insufficiently detailed minutes for its December 19, 2022, meeting.  The second complaint was 
originally filed with the Board on or around March 6, 2023, and the Board responded by letter 
dated March 13, 2023.  The second complaint alleges that the Board posted an insufficiently 
specific notice and approved insufficient minutes for its January 9, 2023, meeting.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaints, the 
Board’s responses, and the requests filed with our office for further review of the complaints.   In 
addition, we reviewed the notices and minutes of the Board’s December 19, 2022, and January 9, 
2023, meetings, as well as other correspondence related to the second complaint. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at least 
48 hours prior to such meeting” and that every notice include “the date, time and place of such 
meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the 
meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).   If a public body chair delegates the task of preparing meeting 
notices to another individual, such as a town administrator, the meeting notice must include all 
topics that the chair as well as all topics that the person tasked with creating the meeting notice

reasonably anticipate for discussion.  See OML 2023-148; OML 2020-152; OML 2015-190.2  
Public bodies are required to list topics on a notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably 
advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(1) (b).  We 
generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could 
read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 
2022-206; OML 2015-35.  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly 
insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2015-109; 
OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if 
the notice was sufficiently specific to make the public aware of the discussion topics.   
 
A public body may discuss a topic not listed on a meeting notice if the topic was not 
rea
sonably anticipated by the chair 48 hours in advance of the meeting.  See OML 2017-2; OML 
2013-87.  Although a public body may consider a topic at a meeting that was not listed in the 
notice if it was not reasonably anticipated at least 48 hours before the meeting, we encourage 
public bodies to postpone discussion of topics that were not listed in the meeting notice until the 
topic may be included in a meeting notice, unless the circumstances demand immediate action by 
the public body.  See OML 2013-197; OML 2011-23.   
 
In addition, the Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain 
acc
urate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and 
place, the members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of 
documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at 
each meeting, including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes 
for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the 
accuracy requirement.  See OML 2013-64.  By substantial compliance, we mean that the minutes 
should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not attend the 
meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See OML 
2012-106.  A transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark or 
opinion presented.  See OML 2017-104; OML 2016-40. 
 
January 2 Complaint 
 
The first complaint alleges that during the Board’s December 19, 2022, meeting, the 
Board discussed and approved a request from the Town Treasurer to borrow $100,000 from the 
Stabilization Fund, despite no such topic appearing on the meeting notice.  The notice for the 
Board’s December 19 meeting included the topic “Updates from Departments,” and the Board 
explains that the Treasurer’s request was made during that portion of the meeting, when the 
Treasurer informed the Board that it may be necessary to transfer funds from the Stabilization 
Fund to the General Fund to cover expenses.  In the past we have not found a violation of the 
Open Meeting Law when a meeting notice includes as a topic a report or presentation by 
somebody who is not a member of the public body, such as “Superintendent’s Report” or 
“Administrator’s Report” or “Report from Department Heads,” where the public body chair did 
not anticipate the specific topics that would be discussed.  Nonetheless, we encourage public 
body chairs, as a best practice, to solicit from those individuals and include on the meeting notice

more detail regarding the precise matters to be discussed.  See OML 2019-94; OML 2018-33; 
OML 2012-19.  Here, we find no indication that the Chair anticipated the Treasurer’s requested 
transfer from the Stabilization Fund, and find no violation of the Open Meeting Law.  We also 
note that although the Board authorized the transfer, ultimately no transfer was necessary.    
 
The first complaint also alleges that the minutes of the December 19 meeting are 
insuf
ficient because they do not include an explanation as to why minutes of the prior meeting 
were placed on hold rather than approved, and do not include an explanation as to the purpose 
for the requested transfer from the Stabilization Fund.  After review, we find no indication that 
the reasoning for either matter was discussed in any detail during the meeting.  The Open 
Meeting Law does not require that a public body discuss any particular matter nor that it discuss 
a matter in any particular depth.  See OML 2023-47; OML 2023-37; OML 2022-231.  Instead, 
the Law requires that meeting minutes accurately summarize what was actually discussed.  See 
OML 2023-76; OML 2023-35; OML 2022-189; OML 2016-155 (explaining that minutes should 
reflect what was actually discussed at a meeting, not matters that were not discussed).  If a matter 
was not discussed during a meeting, or the reasoning behind a particular decision was not 
explained, then the minutes appropriately would not include such an explanation.  We find no 
violation with regard to the sufficiency of the minutes of the December 19 meeting. 
 
March 6 Complaint 
 
The second complaint alleges that during the Board’s January 9, 2023, meeting, the 
Boar
d discussed a request from The Mensing Group to appear before the Board at a future 
meeting to discuss a Host Community Agreement for a marijuana cultivation and manufacturing 
operation, despite no such topic appearing on the meeting notice.  Again, the notice for the 
meeting included the topic “Updates from Department.”  During that portion of the meeting, the 
Town Administrator explained that he had received a letter from The Mensing Group requesting 
to appear before the Board; that he had responded to The Mensing Group inviting them to attend 
any regular meeting; and that he had not yet received any response.  The Board explains that it 
was not aware of the correspondence prior to the January 9 meeting when the Town 
Administrator informed the Board as part of his weekly update that he had received The Mensing 
Group’s request, and furthermore, that there was no discussion regarding the request beyond the 
Town Administrator’s brief update.  We have previously explained that when an administrator 
merely provides updates or information on a matter, and the public body does not engage in 
deliberation, the public body cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with regard to the specificity 
of the meeting notice.  See OML 2023-219; OML 2019-94; OML 2016-76.  Here, we credit the 
Board’s explanation that it was not aware of the correspondence prior to the January 9 meeting 
and that it did not engage in discussion regarding the request, and we find that the Board did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law. 
 
Finally, the second complaint alleges that the minutes of the Board’s January 9, 2023, 
meetin
g fail to list the December 22, 2022, letter from The Mensing Group and the Town 
Administrator’s January 7 response email as documents used during the meeting.  The Open 
Meeting Law does not define what it means for a document to be “used” at a meeting.  We have 
explained that a document is “used” at a meeting when it is, at a minimum, physically present, 
verbally identified, and its contents are discussed by members of the public body.  See OML

 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting
