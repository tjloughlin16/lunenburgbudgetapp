 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Pusateri: 
 
This office received a complaint from Paul DeMeo on May 28, 2024, and a second 
complaint on July 29, 2024, alleging that the Gardner City Council (the “Council”) violated the 
Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The first complaint was originally filed with the 
Council on or around May 3, 2024, and the Council responded by letter dated May 24, 2024.  
The first complaint alleges that the Council convened in executive session on August 7, 
September 18, and December 4, 2023, without a proper purpose under the Open Meeting Law to 
do so and that the Council failed to review and release the minutes of the December 4 executive 
session.
1  The second complaint was originally filed with the Council on or around June 27, 
2024, and the Council responded by letter dated July 17, 2024.  The second complaint further 
alleges that the Council convened in executive session on December 4, 2023, without a proper 
purpose under the Open Meeting Law to do so and that the minutes of the December 4 executive 
session were not read aloud or distributed when they were approved for release with redactions 
on June 3, 2024.  We address the complaints’ allegations collectively below. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting Law 
with regard to the December 4 meeting.  We decline to review the allegations regarding the 
August 7 and September 18 meetings.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open 
Meeting Law complaints, the Council’s responses, and the requests for further review of the 
complaints.  We also corresponded by email with former Council President Elizabeth Kazinskas 
and spoke by phone with you.

DISCUSSION 
 
I. We Decline to Review Allegations Regarding the Propriety of the Council’s 
Executive Session Discussions held on August 7 and September 18. 
 
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public 
body within 30 days of the alleged violation, or if the alleged violation could not have been 
known at the time it occurred, then within 30 days from when the alleged violation reasonably 
could have been discovered. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(4). When an alleged violation 
occurs during an open meeting, the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable at the time it 
occurs. See OML 2024-127; OML 2014-85; OML 2012-52.  
 
The first complaint alleges that the Council convened in executive session on August 7 
and September 18 without a proper purpose under the Open Meeting Law.  As of the date the 
complaints were filed, the minutes of those meetings had not yet been approved for release to the 
public.  Therefore, the allegation that the topics as listed on the notices for each meeting were not 
appropriate for discussion in executive session was based on the information available to the 
public at the time of each meeting.  The Board posted notice for each of the meetings, identifying 
the topics to be discussed in executive session along with a citation to the Open Meeting Law’s 
executive session purpose relied on for each discussion topic.  In particular, notices for the 
Board’s August 7 and September 18 meetings both included the topic “Entered under Exemption 
3 of the Open Meeting Law: ‘To discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining or 
litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating 
position of the public body and the chair so declares,’ (MGL c.30A, §21(3))…”  The complaint 
asserts that the executive sessions were improper because there was no identified pending or 
imminently threatened litigation that would justify convening in executive session on those 
dates.  Any violation concerning the notices and topics to be discussed during these meetings 
could have been discovered at the time each meeting occurred. See OML Declination 12-18-20 
(Pembroke Board of Health); OML Declination 3-3-14 (Sterling Board of Selectmen).2  Where 
the complaint regarding the August 7 and September 18 meetings was not filed until May 3, 
2024, we find that it was untimely and we decline to review the allegations.   
 
II. The Council Convened in Executive Session on December 4 for a Permissible 
Purpose. 
 
Next, both complaints allege that the Council convened in executive session on 
December 4 without a lawful identified purpose under the Open Meeting Law.  After review, we 
find that the Council permissibly convened in executive session on December 4.3

The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
noticed and open to the public, unless an executive session is convened.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a) 
(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive or closed session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that 
a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each 
member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly 
announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.  
 
The chair of the public body must announce in open session the purpose for the executive  
session, “stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which 
the executive session was called.”  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b)(3); see also DA for the N. Dist. 
v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for 
convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification given the 
public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would 
know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”). This level of detail about the executive 
session topic must also be included in the meeting notice. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); OML 2016-
72. 
 
 When posting notice for an executive session, the public body must include sufficient 
information on the notice about the topic(s) to be discussed from which a member of the public 
could determine that the posted topic falls within a proper executive session purpose.  See OML 
2022-21; OML 2016-95.  A public body is not, however, required to provide the specific 
statutory citation to the executive session purpose on which it relies, as long as it otherwise 
provides a sufficient description of the anticipated topic.  See id.  Indeed, we have determined 
that public bodies did not violate the Open Meeting Law when meeting notices lacked a statutory 
citation to an executive session purpose altogether, see, e.g., OML 2022-115, and also when the 
notice cited to an incorrect statutory purpose where a different purpose clearly applied, see, e.g., 
OML 2022-21.  We have consistently explained that a public body may convene in executive 
session to review and approve executive session minutes, and in doing so may point either to the 
original executive session purpose relied on during the meetings for which minutes are to be 
reviewed or to G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(7) (“Purpose 7”), which authorizes convening in executive 
session “to comply with, or act under the authority of, any general or special law or federal 
grant-in-aid requirements,” namely, the Open Meeting Law.  See, e.g., OML 2024-204; OML 
2023-131; OML 2020-120; OML 2013-199. 
 
Notice for the Board’s December 4 meeting included the topic “To approve the Executive 
Session meeting minutes of August 7, 2023, and September 18, 2023.”  Although including a 
statutory citation to the specific executive session purpose that authorized such a discussion may 
have been helpful to the public, we find that the topic as listed was sufficient where it described 
 
redactions.  Therefore, unlike the allegations regarding the August 7 and September 18 meetings which were based 
on the notices alone, information regarding the December 4 executive session became discoverable on June 3, 2024, 
thus rendering the complaint filed less than 30 days later on June 27, 2024, timely.

III. The Council Timely Reviewed the Minutes of its December 4 Executive Session on 
June 3, 2024, and Was Not Required to Read Aloud or Distribute the Minutes. 
 
Finally, the first complaint alleges that the Council failed to review and release the 
minutes of the December 4 executive session, while the second complaint alleges that, when the 
Council eventually reviewed and voted on June 3, 2024, to release the minutes to the public it 
did not read the minutes aloud or otherwise distribute the minutes. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Minutes must set 
“forth the date, time and place [of the meeting], the members present or absent, a summary of the 
discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the 
decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all votes.”  Id.  The 
Law further states that “[m]inutes of all open sessions shall be created and approved in a timely 
manner,” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c), while requiring that minutes of executive sessions be “create[d] 
and maintain[ed]” and “review[ed]” “at reasonable intervals” to determine whether continued 
nondisclosure is warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a), (g)(1).  See also OML 2024-158; OML 2024-
29; Swansea Board of Selectmen v. Attorney General, Civil Action No. 2173CV00906 (Bristol 
Sup. Ct. December 27, 2023).  Executive session minutes may be withheld for “as long as 
publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer; provided, 
however, that the executive session was held in compliance with section 21.”  G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 22(f).  Although “reasonable interval” is not defined by the Law, our office has found a 
quarterly review or a review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2020-101; OML 
2019-45; OML 2017-104.  
 
The Open Meeting Law does not require that public bodies publish their meeting minutes 
to a website or otherwise disseminate meeting minutes absent a request.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22; 
OML 2025-17; OML 2023-135; OML 2017-25; OML 2016-17.  Instead, the Open Meeting Law 
provides that “upon request by any person to inspect or copy the minutes of an executive session 
or any portion thereof, the body shall respond to the request within 10 days following receipt and 
shall release any such minutes not covered by an exemption under subsection (f); provided, 
however, that if the body has not performed a review pursuant to paragraph (1), the public body 
shall perform the review and release the nonexempt minutes, or any portion thereof, not later 
than the body’s next meeting or 30 days, whichever first occurs.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). 
 
On June 3, 2024, the Council reviewed the minutes of the December 4 executive session 
and voted to release the minutes to the public, with redactions.  We find that this review, exactly 
six months after the meeting in question, satisfied the Council’s obligations under the Open 
Meeting Law.  Furthermore, in the absence of any request for the minutes, the Council was not 
required to read aloud or otherwise distribute the December 4 executive session minutes.  
Therefore, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting Law.
