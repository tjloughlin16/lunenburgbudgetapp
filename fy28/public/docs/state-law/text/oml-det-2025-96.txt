DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Board failed to approve meeting minutes in a timely manner. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body 
“create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth 
the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each 
subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the 
actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The Law 
further states that “[m]inutes of all open sessions shall be created and approved in a timely 
manner.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner,” for purposes of approval of open session 
meeting minutes, means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, 
whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 
29.11; see OML 2024-67; OML 2023-179; OML 2018-48.
4  Whenever possible, we recommend 
that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2023-179; OML 2018-67;

The complainant alleges that the Board did not approve in a timely manner minutes of 
October 29, 2024, December 4, 2024, and December 12, 2024.  As a preliminary matter, we 
decline to review allegations that the Board failed to timely approve minutes of meetings held on 
October 29, 2024, and December 4, 2024.  Pursuant to the Open Meeting Law, a complaint must 
be filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b).  If the 
alleged violation could not have been known at the time it occurred, then the complaint must be 
filed with the public body within 30 days of the date on which the alleged violation could 
reasonably have been discovered.  940 CMR 29.05(4).  Complaints alleging violations that take 
place during an open session can reasonably be discovered at the time they occur.  See OML 
Declination 11-27-2023 (Wellfleet Selectboard); OML 2022-128; OML 2012-52.  The minutes 
of the October 29, 2024, meeting were approved at an open session on December 12, 2024; the 
minutes of the December 4, 2024, meeting were approved at an open session on January 22,

2025.  A complaint alleging untimely approval should have been filed within 30 days of the 
meetings when the minutes were approved.  As the instant complaint was filed with the Board on

We do, however, review the allegation that the Board failed to timely approve minutes of 
the December 12, 2024, meeting.  The Open Meeting Law imposes a continuing obligation to 
maintain minutes of all meetings.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2025-35; OML 2020-69 
(reviewing a complaint regarding a committee’s failure to approve meeting minutes for a three-
year period); OML 2018-115 (reviewing a complaint regarding the failure to create and maintain 
meeting minutes for meetings held four years prior).  The alleged violation continued each day 
until the minutes were created and approved.  See OML 2025-35; OML 2024-41; OML 2023-31.  
The Board approved the December 12, 2024, minutes at an open meeting held on February 12, 
2025.  Because the complaint was filed with the Board less than thirty days later, on March 6, 
2025, the complaint was timely as to that allegation.  We find that the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law when it did not approve minutes of the December 12, 2024, meeting until February 
12, 2025, more than 30 days and three meetings following the meeting.

II. Notice of the February 12, 2025 meeting was not sufficiently specific. 
 
The Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a public body shall post notice of every 
meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal 
holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice must be printed in a legible, easily understandable 
format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a listing of topics that 
the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed.  Id.  The list of topics shall have “sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 
CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable 
member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public 
body’s discussion.  See OML 2023-199; OML 2019-134; OML 2015-35.  When a public body 
anticipates reviewing and approving minutes, notice of the meeting should identify the specific 
meeting minutes that the public body anticipates reviewing and approving.  See OML 2025-57; 
OML 2020-34; OML 2013-199.  As the notice of the February 12 meeting did not list the date of 
minutes to be approved, we find that it was not sufficiently specific.  We decline to review 
allegations that previous notices were also insufficiently specific for failure to identify the dates 
of minutes to be approved, as those alleged violations were reasonably discoverable at the time 
of the subject meetings, and thus the allegations are untimely.  We appreciate the Board’s 
commitment to “now include the dates of any minutes to be approved” in future meeting notices.

Although not raised by the complainant, we note an additional insufficiency that 
consistently appears in notices of the Board’s executive session meetings.  The Board has posted 
notices of executive sessions without indicating that the Board will first convene in open session.  
See, e.g., notice of executive session of June 23, 2025, posted on June 18, 2025.  Such notices do 
not comply with the Open Meeting Law.  Notwithstanding a meeting notice’s inclusion of an 
executive session, there is never a guarantee that a public body will enter into executive session.  
The public body must meet in open session to vote to enter into executive session; this vote 
could fail, or the public body could otherwise choose not to convene in executive session.  See 
OML 2025-80; OML 2024-176; OML 2013-85.  When the only business to be discussed during 
an open session is compliance with the procedural requirements for entering executive session, a 
public body must still list “Open Session” on the meeting notice, as this is the only means by 
which members of the public are informed that the public body will, in fact, hold an open 
meeting that they are permitted to attend.  See OML 2019-160; OML 2013-9; OML 2012-68.  
Without such notice, members of the public are essentially denied the opportunity to attend and 
learn the reasons for the executive session.  See OML 2013-195.  Moreover, the public has a 
right to witness these procedural steps in order to observe the vote and learn the purpose for the 
executive session.  Id.  Going forward, the Board should take care to always include an open 
session on the notices of its executive session meetings. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to create and timely approve meeting minutes and by posting insufficiently specific notice 
of its February 12 meeting.  We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the 
Open Meeting Law and caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law and may result in the imposition of civil penalties of up to $1,000 per 
violation. 
 
 We further order the Board to approve all open session minutes that have not yet been 
approved in a timely manner in accordance with the above guidance, including the minutes of 
August 23, 2024, if they have not yet been approved, and to certify to this office within sixty 
(60) days of the date of this letter that it has done so.  See 940 CMR 29.07(4).
