This office received a complaint from Manda Day on December 20, 2023, alleging that 
the Brimfield Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about November 12, 2023, and you 
responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated November 21, 2023.  The complaint alleges 
that the Board did not “follow through” with decisions made during meetings held on September 
25, 2023, and October 23, 2023; and that the Vice Chair acted “on behalf” of the Board “with no 
open discussion and vote.” 
   
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review.  In addition, we reviewed the notices and minutes of the Board meetings held on 
September 25, 2023, October 23, 2023, and November 20, 2023, as well as a video recording of 
the November 20, 2023, meeting.

The Division of Open Government’s review concerns compliance with the Open Meeting 
Law
, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  Even if the allegations listed in the complaint were true, they would 
not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2021-133; OML Declination 7-
20-17 (Edgartown Planning Board).2  Specifically, with respect to the allegation that the Board 
did not “follow through” with decisions made at previous meetings, the Open Meeting Law 
requires that a public body post a timely notice listing the topics the chair reasonably anticipates 
will be discussed, and that such discussions take place during meetings.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
20(a), (b); OML 2013-64; OML 2012-23.  While the Open Meeting Law governs the 
transparency and accessibility of a public body’s deliberations, it does not govern whether or 
when a public body must discuss a particular topic.  See OML 2015-73; OML 2014-98; OML 
2013-64; OML 2012-23.  Once a public body has discussed a topic during a meeting, whether 
the public body completes the task that it discussed and/or voted on during the meeting, is 
outside the scope of the Open Meeting Law. 
 
With respect to the allegation that the Vice Chair conducted Board business on his own, 
the O
pen Meeting Law applies when a quorum of members of a public body meet and deliberate 
on matters within its jurisdiction.  See G.L. c 30A, § 18.  For purposes of the Open Meeting Law, 
a “quorum” is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id.  To the extent that the 
complaint may be construed as alleging that the Vice Chair effectively made decisions that 
should have been made by the Board as a whole, this allegation goes to the authority of 
individuals such as the Vice Chair versus the Board to take action on particular matters, which is 
not a matter governed by the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-144.  In addition, because 
only two out of five Board members, a subquorum, met with the Veteran’s Agent and the 
complainant on or about October 29, 2023, and having been provided with no evidence showing 
communication between a quorum of the Board, we find no improper deliberation by the Board 
outside of a meeting.  See OML 2018-28; OML 2017-193.  We offer no opinion on whether any 
actions taken by the Vice Chair could be a violation of some other town bylaw, law or regulation 
outside the scope of the Division’s review.
