 RE:        Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Ms. Makepeace-O’Neil: 
 
This office received a complaint from Amanda Alix on May 13, 2024, alleging that the 
Shutesbury Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about March 28, 2024, and the Board 
responded to the complaint by letter dated April 9, 2024.  The complaint alleges that the Board 
did not timely respond to a request for executive session meeting minutes and did not review the 
minutes at its next meeting following the request. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to timely respond to a request for meeting minutes.  We find that the Board did not violate the 
Open Meeting Law in the other way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the 
original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Upon request by 
any person to inspect or copy the minutes of an executive session or any portion thereof, the 
body must respond to the request within 10 days following receipt and release any such minutes 
where publication would not defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session; provided, 
however, that if the body has not performed a review, the public body shall perform the review 
and release the non-exempt minutes, or any portion thereof, not later than the body’s next

meeting or 30 days, whichever first occurs.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2); OML 2012-106; OML 
2012-91; OML 2012-86.1  
 
On February 28, 2024, the complainant sent an email to Board Co-Chair Melissa 
Makepeace-O’Neil2 requesting a copy of the executive session minutes for the Board meeting 
held on October 24, 2023.  On March 14, 2024, Ms. Makepeace-O’Neil responded to the request 
and advised the complainant that the minutes of the October 24, 2023, meeting would not be 
disclosed.  A request for meeting minutes made directly to the public body falls under the Open 
Meeting Law and requires a response within 10 calendar days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2); 
OML 2019-125.  Here, the request for meeting minutes was directed to the Board’s Chair, but 
the Board did not respond until the 15th calendar day.  As such, we find that the Board violated 
the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely respond to a request for meeting minutes.   
 
With respect to the allegation that the Board failed to review the October 24, 2023, 
minut
es during its meeting held on March 11, 2024, which was the next meeting following the 
request, we do not find a violation.  The Open Meeting Law does not require that review of 
executive session minutes for release be conducted in a noticed meeting.  See OML 2023-242; 
OML 2023-186.  Indeed, the statute itself authorizes a public body’s chair or designee to review 
executive session minutes.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); see also OML 2020-81 (city solicitor 
and city clerk designated to review executive session minutes); OML 2015-166 (public body’s 
administrative assistant and legal counsel reviewed minutes); OML 2013-99 (chair of Zoning 
Board of Appeals reviewed minutes).  Rather, the requirement that the review occur “not later 
than the body’s next meeting or 30 days, whichever first occurs” establishes a timeframe for 
doing the review and in fact contemplates that the review may not occur during a meeting at all, 
since the review must occur within 30 days even if no meeting is held within that time.  G.L. c. 
30A, § 22(g)(2); see also OML 2023-186.  Because the chair on her own may review minutes of 
an executive session to determine if continued non-disclosure of minutes is warranted and here 
the Board Chair reviewed the requested minutes and informed the complainant that the “Board is 
not required to disclose the minutes,” we find that the failure to do the review during a meeting 
did not violate the Open Meeting Law.   
 
Although not raised in the complaint, we remind the Board that executive session minutes 
may onl
y be withheld from disclosure to the public “as long as publication may defeat the lawful 
purposes of the executive session, but no longer.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).  When the purpose for 
the executive session has concluded, the Open Meeting Law no longer authorizes the continued 
nondisclosure of the minutes, although the public body may assert an exemption under the Public 
Records Law to withhold the minutes, in whole or in part, from disclosure.  See OML 2024-166; 
OML 2021-143.  Here, the Board met in executive session on October 24, 2023, under Purpose 1 
to discuss a complaint brought against the Town Administrator.  We remind the Board that if the 
purpose for the executive session has concluded, i.e. if the Board has taken all the action it was 
authorized to take with respect to that employee, then the Open Meeting Law would no longer 
provide a basis for withholding the executive session minutes.  However, even if the Open 
Meeting Law no longer authorizes the withholding of the minutes, the minutes may be exempt

 reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely respond to a request for meeting minutes.  We order the Board’s immediate and
