DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session 
is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  Public bodies must post notice of each meeting 
“at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays” and 
every notice must include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that 
the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The list 
of topics shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to include 
sufficient specificity when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and 
understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2022-206; OML 
2015-35; OML 2012-71; OML 2011-44.2   
 
A public body may enter a closed, executive session for any of ten enumerated purposes, 
provided that it has first convened in open session, that a majority of the members of the body 
have voted to go into executive session, and that the vote of each member is recorded by roll call 
and entered into the minutes.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21 (a)-(b); OML 2019-32; OML 2014-94.  Among

the ten permissible purposes for convening in executive session are Purpose 2, “[t]o conduct 
strategy sessions in preparation for negotiations with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective 
bargaining sessions or contract negotiations with nonunion personnel” and Purpose 7, “[t]o 
comply with, or act under the authority of, any general or special law or federal grant-in-aid 
requirements.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2), (7).   
 
The complaint alleges that the notice for the Committee’s June 6 meeting was 
insu
fficiently specific with regard to the first three executive session topics as it did not “refer to 
specific court cases or matters ... to be discussed.”  The Committee did not discuss court cases or 
other litigation; rather, it approved minutes of three prior executive sessions, as was clearly 
stated on the meeting notice.  We have consistently explained that a public body may convene in 
executive session to review and approve executive session meeting minutes, and when doing so 
may cite on the meeting notice either to the original purpose for the executive session for which 
minutes will be reviewed or to Purpose 7, referencing the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
22(f), (g).  See OML 2024-204; OML 2020-120; OML 2019-81; 2013-199.  Here, the 
Committee properly specified on the meeting notice that it would convene in executive session 
pursuant to “M.G.L. c. 30A, § 2l(a)(7) ‘to comply with, or act under the authority of, any general 
or special law or federal grant-in-aid requirements’ (‘Purpose 7’); Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 22(f), (g) review and approval of executive session minutes” and identified each of the 
three meeting dates for which minutes would be reviewed.  Therefore, the notice was sufficiently 
specific. 
 
The complaint also alle
 ges that the notice for the Committee’s June 6 meeting was 
insufficiently specific with regard to discussion of Superintendent Ligia Noriega-Murphy’s 
contract because the notice did not specify that the Committee would discuss a cost of living 
adjustment.  We have explained that when convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 2, 
a public body must identify the specific nonunion personnel that is the subject of discussion, 
unless disclosure would compromise the public body’s negotiating position.  See OML 2021-
187; OML 2015- 193; OML 2016-70.  Here, the meeting notice specified that the Committee 
would discuss “Superintendent Noriega-Murphy Salary Review 2023-2024.”  A public body 
need not list each and every detail about a topic in order to comply with the law’s requirements.  
See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134; OML 2014-122.  We find that the 
description of the matter to be discussed was adequate, as it identified the employee to be 
discussed and, moreover, informed the public that the Committee would review her salary for the 
2023-2024 term.  The Committee was not required to specify that it would consider a cost of 
living adjustment.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. 
This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the
