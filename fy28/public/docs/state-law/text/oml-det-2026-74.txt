This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on February 18, 2026,
1 alleging 
that the Springfield School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. 
c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on or about January 13, 
and you responded on behalf of the Committee by letter dated March 18.2 The complaint alleges 
that the Committee failed to respond to a request for minutes within 10 calendar days.   
 
Following our review, we resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 
940 CMR 29.07(2)(a) and find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law in the way 
alleged. In reaching this determination we reviewed the original Open Meeting Law complaint, 
the Committee’s response along with its attachments, and the complainant’s request for review.

The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Open Meeting Law requires public bodies to create 
and approve minutes of all open sessions in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, §22(c). The minutes 
of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available 
upon request by any person within 10 days. Id. When a public body receives a request for 
executive session minutes, the body must respond to the request within 10 days. See G.L. c. 30A, 
§22(g)(2). If at the time of the request the public body has not recently performed a periodic 
review of the requested minutes, as provided by G.L. c. 30A, §22(g)(1), the body must perform 
the review and release the non-exempt minutes, or any portion thereof, not later than the body’s 
next meeting or 30 days, whichever occurs first. Id. If this review is required, the public body 
must still respond to the requester within 10 days of receiving the request, informing the 
requester that the public body will conduct a review to determine whether the minutes may be 
withheld or must be released. See OML 2023-62; OML Declination 10-15-2023 (Hubbardston 
Select Board).3 
 
Requests for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law or the Open 
Meeting Law, depending on how the request is made, and the requirements for responding differ. 
A request for meeting minutes made directly to the public body falls under the Open Meeting 
Law and requires a response within 10 calendar days. See OML 2019-163. However, a request 
made to a municipal employee, such as a Town Clerk, would likely trigger the Public Records 
Law and requires a response within 10 business days. See G.L. c. 66, § 10;
 OML 2019-126.  
 
Here, on January 2, the complainant sent an email to Springfield Mayor Domenic Sarno, 
as the Chair of the Committee, requesting copies of “both open and executive session minutes [of 
the Committee], for calendar years 2024 and 2025.” Because the request for meeting minutes 
was directed to the Committee’s Chair, the request fell under the Open Meeting Law and 
required a response within 10 calendar days. After receiving no response, the complainant filed 
an Open Meeting Law complaint with the Committee on January 13. The Committee 
acknowledges, and we find, that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
respond to the January 2 request for minutes within 10 days. We note that the Committee 
provided the complainant with copies of responsive minutes on February 2 and we commend the 
Committee’s efforts to ensure future compliance.  
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law. 
We order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and 
caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the Law.
