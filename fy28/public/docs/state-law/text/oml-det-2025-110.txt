This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on April 15, 2025, alleging that the 
Kingston Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on March 26, 2025, and you responded to 
the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated April 15, 2025.1  The complaint alleges 
that the Board posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a meeting held on February 25 and 
failed to conduct roll call votes during that meeting.2

Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to include on the February 25 meeting notice the name of the individual to be appointed to the 
Kingston Educational Fund Trustees.  We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original 
complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice, minutes and video recording of 
the Board meeting held on February 25.
3 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on 
February 25 at 6:00 P.M.4  The notice listed several main topic headings, two of which were 
“Appointments” and “Additional Items.”  These two topic headings included specific item 
descriptions that further advised the public of the topics to be discussed.  Specifically, five 
specific items descriptions were listed under “Appointment,” one of which was “Renewal of 
Razor Clam Licenses;” and six specific item descriptions were listed under “Additional Items,” 
including “Acceptance of Donation to the Kingston Public Library and the Council on Aging” 
and “Re-appointment to the Kingston Education Fund Trustees.”  
 
The February 25 meeting was held as planned.  One Board member, Donald 
Alcombright, participated in the meeting remotely while the remaining members were present in 
the physical meeting location.  During the meeting, the Board welcomed a new police officer and 
held two public hearings.  The Board then briefly discussed razor clam licenses, whether the 
town bylaw with respect to such licenses should be revised, and whether to reduce the number of 
available licenses.  Ultimately, the Board tabled further discussion to a future meeting and did 
not review or approve the renewal of any licenses.  The Board accepted, by a roll call vote, a 
donation in the amount of $2,400 from the Kingston Public Library Foundation to repair the 
library’s electronic sign, as well as a $25.00 donation to the Council on Aging.  Finally, the 
Board unanimously reappointed, by a roll call vote, William Alberti to the Educational Fund 
Trustees. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at least 
48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays” and that every 
notice include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are 
required to list topics on a notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of 
the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally consider a topic

The complaint alleges that the Board posted a notice with insufficient specificity for the 
February 25 meeting.  Specifically, the complaint alleges that the notice did not list the names of 
the individuals being considered for renewal of Razor Clam licenses, did not list the name of the 
candidate to be appointed to the Educational Fund Trustees, and did not list the specific 
donations to the library or Council on Aging.  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, 
unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone.  See 
OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting 
to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the public aware of the discussion 
topics. 
 
First, we find that the notice reasonably informed members of the public of the 
anticipated nature of the Board’s discussion with respect to the Razor Clam license renewals.  
During the February 25 meeting, the Board briefly discussed with the Shellfish Constable the 
number of individuals seeking renewal of razor clam licenses and whether the number of 
available licenses should be reduced.  The brief discussion of the possible reduction of available 
licenses naturally flowed from the Board’s consideration of the posted agenda topic and 
therefore the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law by discussing a topic not listed on the 
notice.  Where a public body’s discussion naturally flows from its consideration of the posted 
agenda topic, the notice was sufficient to inform the public of the discussion that occurred.  See 
OML 2018-14; OML 2014-44; OML 2014-38.  Moreover, the Open Meeting Law does not 
require a public body to anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only 
that it identify the anticipated discussion topic with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 
29.03(l)(b); OML 2019-102; OML 2017-186; OML 2014-38.  
 
In addition, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law where the 
notic
e did not list the names of the individuals being considered for renewal of Razor Clam 
licenses because the Board did not review or approve specific license renewals.  Where a public 
body does not actually engage in any deliberation on a particular topic, it cannot violate the Open 
Meeting Law with respect to the sufficiency of the meeting notice.  See OML 2016-76.  
Nonetheless, we remind the Board that, to be sufficiently specific, a meeting notice must identify 
the specific applicants or license holders that will be considered during the meeting, as well as 
the specific type of license renewal to be reviewed.  See OML 2020-112; OML 2020-20; OML 
2017-33; OML 2016-90.  
 
Next
, we find that the Board should have included on the notice the name of the 
individual being appointed to the Educational Fund Trustees.  The Board contends that the notice 
was sufficient where it listed the positions to be filled and the candidates up for appointment, i.e. 
the exiting trustees.  We disagree.  During the meeting, the Board reappointed only one trustee,

William Alberti.  We have consistently stated that the names of applicants for appointment

h are known 48 hours in advance of a meeting should be included on a meeting notice.  See 
OML 2022-217; 2019-24; OML 2016-150; OML 2013-163.  The Board has not claimed that the 
specific reappointment was not reasonably anticipated 48 hours in advance of the February 25 
meeting.  As such, we find that the notice for the Board meeting held on February 25 should 
have included the name of the individual to be considered by the Board.  See OML 2025-23.  
Accordingly, the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to include this information on 
the notice. 
 
With respect to the list of donations, we have said that a public body does not need to 
incl
ude the specific names of the donors on the notice where such a topic does not require the 
Board’s discussion and/or approval but is listed instead as a perfunctory administrative task.  See 
OML 2018-22.  Here, the Board did not discuss the donations, although it did approve the 
donations.  We find that the Board’s approval of the donations was essentially a perfunctory 
administrative task and therefore the notice did not need to include the specific donors; rather, 
the description of the matter included on the notice was sufficient.  See OML 2023-17; OML 
2019-161; OML 2018-22.  A public body need not list each and every detail about a topic in 
order to comply with the Law’s requirements.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-
134.   
 
Finally, the complaint alleges that the Board did not conduct roll call votes.  Because one 
Boar
d member participated remotely during the February 25 meeting, all votes taken during that 
meeting were required to be taken by roll call vote, even unanimous votes.  See 940 CMR 
29.10(7)(c); OML 2016-132; OML 2015-145.  After watching a video recording of the February 
25 meeting, we find that the Board conducted all votes taken during the meeting by roll call.  As 
such, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law.
6   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to include on the February 25 meeting notice the name of the individual to be appointed 
to the Kingston Educational Fund Trustees.  We order the Board’s immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
 
 
 
 
 
 
6 We remind the Board that meeting minutes must include, among other things, “the record of all votes.”  G.L. c.

listing the names of each public body member and how each voted.  Even unanimous votes should be recorded by 
roll call in the minutes.  See OML 2015-131; OML 2013-195.
