FACTS 
We find the facts as follows.   On February 4, 2024, the complainant sent an email to the 
Board requesting all minutes “that have not yet been posted to the Richmond website.”  The 
Board did not issue any response or make any outstanding minutes available within ten days of 
the complainant’s request.  On or about February 20, Town Administrator Danielle Fillio (“TA 
Fillio”)
3 spoke in person with the complainant.  TA Fillio acknowledged the delay in approving 
minutes and explained that she was in the process of addressing the backlog.  During the summer 
and fall of 2023, TA Fillio had been “in and out of the office” due to a family medical issue.  
Additionally, the Town experienced staffing shortages around this time.   
At the Board’s February 28 meeting, the Board approved minutes from meetings held on 
November 15, November 29, December 13, and December 27, 2023, and January 10, 2024.  At 
its May 1 meeting, the Board approved minutes of its February 14 and February 28 meetings.  
On May 29, the Board approved minutes of all its March meetings (March 7, March 13, and 
March 27), as well as its only April meeting, April 10.  Minutes of the January 24 meeting have 
been approved but we are unable to discern the date of approval.  Recordings of meetings are 
available online.  The Board recognizes, however, that they are “not intended to replace actual 
written minutes.” 
DISCUSSION  
 
I. The Board failed to create and approve minutes in a timely manner. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  Public bodies are required to “create and maintain 
accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); 940 CMR 
29.11(1).  The minutes must be created and approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  
“Timely manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, 
whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 
29.11. Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next 
meeting.  See OML 2023-179; OML 2018-67; OML 2017-133.
4  The Open Meeting Law does 
not require public bodies to post meeting minutes online.  See OML 2023-179; 2015-173; OML 
2013-117.   
 
The complaint alleges that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law because it did not 
post m
inutes to the “Richmond website.”  In light of the complainant’s reference to language of 
the Open Meeting Law regarding the “timeliness” of minutes, and the concern he expresses 
about the delay in approving meeting minutes, we construe the complaint as alleging that the 
Board failed to timely approve minutes of its 2024 meetings.

Of the minutes approved during this calendar year, at least eight sets were not approved 
within three meetings or 30 days.  Although the Board does not explicitly claim that it had good 
cause for the delay, TA Fillio references family health issues and staffing shortages in the 
Board’s response.  We previously have found good cause for a delay of one week where, for 
example, the person tasked with preparing minutes was struck ill.  See OML 2022-186.  Here, 
however, the delay persisted for months.  It is also significant for purposes of evaluating good 
cause that none of the Board members themselves were prevented from preparing minutes.  
Indeed, TA Fillio prepared minutes of the May 1 meeting in anticipation of the May 29 meeting, 
but it was the Board that deferred approval of those minutes to a later date.  Ultimately, the duty 
to comply with the deadline to approve meeting minutes rests with public bodies, not with 
administrative staff or other public employees.  See OML 2023-179; 2023-117; OML 2018-105.  
Staffing shortages of the type that all governmental bodies with limited resources experience, 
including periodic staff vacancies, do not constitute good cause for failing to approve minutes in 
a timely manner.  See OML 2023-179; OML 2023-117.  A public body must ensure that staff 
have adequate assistance to complete the minutes in a timely manner, having members of the 
public body assist in the creation of minutes if necessary.  See OML 2023-117; OML 2021-177; 
OML 2015-86.  Therefore, we find that the Board did not have good cause for its delay in 
approving meeting minutes, and violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely approve 
meeting minutes.   
 
II. The Board failed to respond to the complainant’s request for minutes. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that minutes of an open session, if they exist and 
whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 
days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  When open session minutes are requested, a public body must 
within ten days either make the requested minutes available or respond to the requestor that no 
minutes exist.  See OML 2019-35; OML 2017-173.  When, for example, a request for minutes is 
received just one day or a few days after a meeting, and no minutes have yet been put in draft 
form by the time of the request, the public body must nonetheless respond to the request to 
explain whether the minutes do or do not exist in either approved or draft form.  See OML 2019-
20; OML 2018-154. 
 
On February 4, the complainant made a wide-ranging request for “all [Board] meeting 
minutes. . . that have not yet been posted to the Richmond website.”  Our office will not conduct 
broad audits of public bodies based on generalized allegations.  See OML 2023-242; OML 2021-
129; OML 2016-57.  We would be inclined to decline investigation of an allegation that the 
Board failed to make minutes available, where the dates of the minutes are not specified.  
However, other documents filed in this matter clarify the dates of at least some of the minutes 
sought by the complainant.   
 
By the time the Board was required to respond to the complainant’s request of February 
4, it had not yet approved minutes of November 15, November 29, December 13, and December 
27, 2023, and January 10, 2024.  The minutes could not be produced within the 10-day deadline 
because they did not exist.  The Board should have, however, responded to the request with an 
explanation of whether the minutes existed and whether they were in approved of draft form.

CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to approve meeting minutes in a timely manner and failing to respond within ten days to 
the complainant’s request for minutes.  We order the Board to create and approve all outstanding 
open session meeting minutes within 90 days of the date of this letter, and to certify to our office 
that it has done so.  We further order immediate and future compliance with the law's 
requirements and we caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent 
to violate the law. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  Please 
feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions.
