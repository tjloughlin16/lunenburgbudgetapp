DISCUSSION 
 
I. We Decline to Review Meetings that Took Place More Than 30 Days Before the 
Complaint was Filed. 
 
The complaint alleges, bas ed on the information listed on public meeting notices, that the 
Board convened in executive session without a proper purpose during 29 meetings held between 
September 12, 2023, and March 4, 2025.  As a preliminary matter, we decline to review the 27 
meetings that took place more than 30 days before the complaint was filed on March 13. 
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public body 
within 30 days of the alleged violation, or if the alleged violation could not have been known at 
the time it occurred, then within 30 days from when the alleged violation reasonably could have 
been discovered.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(4).  When an alleged violation occurs 
during an open meeting, the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable at the time it occurs. 
See OML 2024-127; OML 2014-85; OML 2012-52.  
 
The Board poste
 d notice for each meeting, identifying topics to be discussed along with a 
citation to the Open Meeting Law’s executive session purpose relied on for each discussion 
topic; the complainant now points to those notices as evidence that the Board lacked a proper 
purpose for convening in executive session.  Furthermore, any member of the public could have 
attended the open session portion of the meetings and learned the stated purpose for the 
executive session.  Any violation concerning the notices and topics to be discussed during these 
meetings could have been discovered at the time each meeting occurred.  See OML Declination 
12-18-20 (Pembroke Board of Health); OML Declination 3-3-14 (Sterling Board of Selectmen). 
The complaint was therefore not timely filed with respect to meetings that took place more than 
30 days before it was filed, and we decline to review those meetings. See OML Declination 4-7-
22 (Mendon Board of Assessors); OML Declination 9-27-21 (Ashland Select Board); OML 
Declination 2-19-21 (Everett City Council).
3  Moreover, our office will not conduct broad audits

II. The Board Permissibly Discussed Tax Exemption and Abatement Applications in 
Executive Session on February 18 and March 4. 
 
The notice for the Board’s February 18 meeting included the topics “Executive Session – 
Pursuant to G.L. c.30A s. 21(a)(7), to comply with G.L. c. 59 s. 60”; “Personal Exemptions”; and 
“Real Estate Abatement Applications.”  Additionally, the notice for the Board’s March 4 
meeting included the topics “Executive Session – Pursuant to G.L. c.30A s. 21(a)(7), to comply 
with G.L. c. 59 s. 60”; “Real Estate Abatement Applications”; “Real Estate Abatement – Carol 
O’Brien”; and “Personal Exemptions.”  
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
noticed and open to the public, unless an executive session is convened.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a) 
(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that 
a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each 
member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly 
announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.  
 
The chair of the public body must announce in open session the purpose for the executive  
session, “stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which 
the ex
ecutive session was called.”  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b)(3); see also DA for the N. Dist. 
v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for 
convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification given the 
public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would 
know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  This level of detail about the 
executive session topic must also be included in the meeting notice.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 
OML 2016-72. 
 
One permissible reason for executive session is Purpose 7, which permits a public body 
to conve
ne in executive session “to comply with, or act under the authority of, any general or 
special law or federal grant-in-aid requirements.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(7).  If a public body 
enters executive session under Purpose 7, it must identify the specific general or special law, or 
federal grant-in-aid requirement, that requires confidentiality or requires the public body to meet 
behind closed doors.  See Dist. Atty. for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 
569 (2009) (explaining that the proper use of Purpose 7 to enter executive session requires the 
enumeration of the enabling law).  Massachusetts General Laws state that applications for tax 
abatement or exemption may only be disclosed to a select group of public officials, though 
certain information about the application must be made public if it is granted. G.L. c. 59, § 60.  If 
a Board of Assessors plans to discuss the content of an application for tax abatement or 
exemption, the Board may convene in executive session under Purpose 7, “to comply with, or act 
under the authority of, any general law,” citing to G.L. c. 59, § 60, as the statute requiring 
confidentiality.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(7).   Accordingly, we find that the Board’s discussions 
of tax exemption and tax abatement applications during its February 18 and March 4, 2025,

meetings were appropriate for executive session under Purpose 7.  See OML 2013-144; OML

III. The Board Violated the Open Meeting Law When it Discussed Personnel Matters in 
Executive Session on February 18 and March 4. 
 
Another permissible purpose for convening in executive session is “to discuss strategy 
with r
espect to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental 
effect on the bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).  Purpose 3 offers the narrow opportunity to discuss strategy 
with respect to litigation that is pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise 
demonstrably likely; the mere possibility of litigation is not sufficient to invoke Purpose 3.  See 
Doherty v. School Committee of Boston, 386 Mass. 643, 648 (1982); Perryman v. Sch. Comm. 
of Boston, 17 Mass. App. Ct. 346, 352 (1983); OML 2020-76; OML 2019-164; OML 2012-5.  
Purpose 3 is intended to protect discussions of strategy with respect to the public body’s own 
litigating position; the fact that another town entity or official is involved in litigation does not 
give the public body license to discuss matters not affecting the public body’s own litigating 
positions out of public view.  See OML 2015-1. 
 
The notices for the Board’s February 18 and March 4 meetings each included the topic 
“Executive Session- Pursuant to G.L. c.30A s. 21 (a)(3) Personnel Discussion Continued - 
Wendy Jones.”  After investigation, it is clear that the Board’s discussions pertaining to Wendy 
Jones did not relate to pending or clearly and imminently threatened litigation, or indeed any 
litigation involving the Board that would justify convening in executive session.  Therefore, the 
Board violated the Open Meeting Law by convening in executive session on February 18 and 
March 4 to discuss such matters. 
 
IV. The Board Vi
 olated the Open Meeting Law When it Purportedly Discussed an 
Open Meeting Law Complaint in Executive Session on February 18 and March 4.  
 
One permissible reason to convene in executive session is “[t]o discuss the reputation, 
char
acter, physical condition or mental health, rather than professional competence, of an 
individual, or to discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or charges brought against, 
a public officer, employee, staff member or individual.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1) (“Purpose 1”).  
It is well-established that public bodies may meet in executive session under Purpose 1 to review 
Open Meeting Law complaints filed with the body.  See OML 2024-191; OML 2024-108; OML 
2023-109; OML 2013-82.  An Open Meeting Law complaint is by its nature a “complaint[] ... 
brought against[] a public officer,” namely, the members of the public body.  See OML 2024-
191; OML 2021-194; OML 2012-119.  We have explained that a public body may convene in 
executive session to discuss an Open Meeting Law complaint filed with the body; however, the 
proper executive session purpose for such discussion is Purpose 1, not Purpose 3, as the filing of 
an Open Meeting Law complaint does not constitute litigation nor is it an adversarial proceeding 
akin to litigation.  See OML 2024-168; OML 2023-39; OML 2013-82; OML 2012-119. 
 
The notice for the Board’s February 18 meeting included the topic “Executive Sessions – 
Pursuant to G.L. c.30A s. 21 (a)(1)[:] Open Meeting Law Complaint – Select Board response

dated February 5, 2025.”  Additionally, the notice for the Board’s March 4 meeting included the 
topic “Executive Session – Pursuant to G.L. c.30A s. 21 (a)(3)[:] Open Meeting Law Complaint 
update.”  Notwithstanding that it is generally permissible for a public body to discuss an Open 
Meeting Law complaint filed against it in executive session pursuant to Purpose 1, no such 
complaint had been filed with the Board as of the February 18 or March 4 meetings.  The only 
Open Meeting Law complaint that has been filed with the Board is the instant complaint, filed 
with the Board on March 13.  Instead, it appears the Board may have been discussing a 
complaint that Principal Assessor Wendy Jones filed with the Whitman Select Board in January.  
Where that complaint was not a complaint brought against the members of the Board, it was not 
appropriate for the Board to discuss it in executive session, and in so doing the Board violated 
the Open Meeting Law.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
discussing matters in executive session on February 18 and March 4 that did not fall within any 
of the Open Meeting Law’s enumerated executive session purposes.  We order the Board’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Law’s requirements and caution the Board that a 
determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the Open Meeting Law.
