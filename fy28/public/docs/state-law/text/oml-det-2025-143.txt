DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minute
s of all meetings, including executive sessions.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). Minutes of open 
sessions must be created and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). The Attorney 
General’s Open Meeting Law regulations define “timely manner” as being “within the next three 
public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show 
good cause for further delay.” 940 CMR 29.11(2). The Open Meeting Law requires that “the 
minutes of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made 
available upon request by any person within 10 days.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). If the minutes do not 
yet exist at the time of a request, the public body is still required to respond to the request within 
10 days, explaining that the minutes do not exist. See OML 2018-98; OML 2017-50; OML 
2016-71; OML 2015-173.3

Executive session minutes may be withheld from disclosure to the public “as long as 
public
ation may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer.” G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 22(f). Once the purpose for a valid executive session has been served, the minutes and any 
documents or exhibits used at the session must be disclosed unless the attorney-client privilege 
or an exemption to the Public Records Law applies to withhold them, in whole or in part, from 
disclosure. See id. The burden of justifying continued nondisclosure of executive session minutes 
lies with the public body. See Foudy v. Amherst-Pelham Regional Sch. Comm., 402 Mass. 179, 
184 (1988); OML 2017-151.  
 
Public bodies are required to review executive session minutes at reasonable intervals to 
dete
rmine if continued non-disclosure is still warranted. G.L. c. 30A, § 22 (g)(1). The review can 
be performed by the body as a whole, by the chair alone, or by a designee. Id. The public body 
must announce the result of this review at its next meeting and record the announcement in the 
minutes of that meeting. Id. Although the Open Meeting Law does not define “reasonable 
intervals,” we have found that quarterly reviews or reviews every six months satisfy the Law. 
Compare OML 2015-166 (finding a six-month review sufficient) and OML 2024-64 (finding a 
three-month review sufficient), with OML 2024-82 (finding a nine-month review insufficient). A 
public body’s obligation to review executive session minutes for possible release is ongoing. If a 
public body reviews executive session minutes and determines that the purpose for the executive 
session remains ongoing, it must continue to review those same executive session minutes at 
reasonable intervals until it determines that the reason for secrecy has expired. See OML 2019- 
133. If executive session minutes have been released subject to redactions made under a claim 
that an executive session purpose remains ongoing, then the public body must continue to review 
those redactions at reasonable intervals until it determines that the executive session purpose is 
no longer ongoing; however, if the redactions are made pursuant to an exemption to the Public 
Records Law or a claim of the attorney-client privilege, then the body is not required to continue 
to periodically review those redactions.

Upon receipt of a request for executive session minutes, a public body must respond to 
the r
equester within ten days. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). If the public body has recently performed 
the review required under G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1), then it should respond to the requester within 
ten days providing any minutes it determined should be released and explaining if any minutes 
continue to be withheld. See OML 2013-105; OML 2013-99. If, however, at the time of a request 
the public body has not recently conducted a periodic review of its executive session minutes, 
then the body must review the requested minutes to determine if the minutes must be released. 
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). This review must be performed by the body’s next meeting or within 30 
days, whichever occurs first. Id. In such circumstances, the body must still respond to the 
requester within ten days and should notify them that the body is conducting a review of the 
requested executive session minutes. See OML 2013-99.

I. The February 5, 2024, Executive Session Minutes 
 
We understand the complaint to allege that the Committee failed to review redactions 
made to its February 5, 2024, executive session minutes because “[i]n the response of April 2, 
2025, Malden states that the records from February 5, 202[4], continue to require redactions, 
however the body has failed to review these redactions in any meeting since.” First, as the 
Committee notes in its response to the complaint, the Complainant does not challenge the 
Committee’s bases for continuing to redact portions of the February 5, 2024, minutes. 
Furthermore, the Committee explains that prior to its April 2 response to the request for minutes, 
legal counsel reviewed the redactions and determined that they were still warranted. 
Additionally, the Committee explains that its legal counsel, as designee, periodically reviews its 
executive session meeting minutes, including redactions thereto, to determine if they may 
continue to be withheld or must be released. Because the redactions to the February 5, 2024, 
executive session minutes were reviewed in response to the Complainant’s March 24 request, we 
find no violation with respect to this allegation. 
 
II. The March 3, 2025, Open Session Minutes 
 
The complaint alleges that in response to the Complainant’s request, the Committee 
failed to provide draft minutes for its open session meeting held March 3, 2025. In its response to 
the complaint, the Committee clarifies that at the time of the March 24 request and the 
Committee’s April 2 response to the request the minutes for its March 3, 2025, meeting did not 
yet exist in either draft or final form. Where, at the time of the request, the minutes of the 
Committee’s March 3, 2025, meeting did not yet exist, in either draft or final form, we find that 
the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged. However, we note that the 
Committee’s response to the request may have caused unnecessary confusion where it stated at 
various points both that the minutes did not exist and that the minutes had not yet been reviewed, 
making it somewhat unclear whether the minutes simply did not exist or existed but had not been 
reviewed. 
 
III. The March 4, April 1, May 6, May 29, June 6, July 11, August 12, September 9, 
October 10, and November 4, 2024; and January 6, January 13, and February 3, 2025, 
Executive Session Minutes 
 
The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to review for release at reasonable 
intervals the minutes of executive sessions held on March 4, April 1, May 6, May 29, June 6, 
July 11, August 12, September 9, October 10, and November 4, 2024; and January 6, January 13, 
and February 3, 2025. The basis for this allegation is that in its response to the March 24 request, 
the Committee explained that, with respect to the above minutes, it was in the process of 
scheduling review of the minutes in compliance with the Open Meeting Law. In its response to 
the complaint, the Committee clarifies that the review referenced in its response to the March 24 
request was the review required under G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2)—done in response to a request—
and not the periodic review required under G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1). The Committee further 
explains that its legal counsel, as designee, periodically reviews its executive session minutes to 
determine if they may continue to be withheld or must be released to the public. The 
Complainant does not dispute that the Committee’s legal counsel periodically reviews the

Committee’s executive session minutes for release. Absent evidence to the contrary, we credit 
the Com
mittee’s explanation and find no violation with respect to this allegation. See OML 
2025-10 (“Absent sufficient evidence to the contrary, we credit a public body’s account of the 
facts.”); OML 2023-121; OML 2017-93; OML 2016-141. However, we remind the Committee 
that following periodic review of its executive session minutes, the results of such review must 
be announce in an open session meeting and recorded in the minutes of that meeting.   
 
IV. The December 4, 2023, Executive Session Minutes 
 
 Finally, the complaint alleges that the Committee failed to provide minutes of an 
executive session held on December 4, 2023, and that the version of these minutes posted online 
include redactions that the Supervisor of Records ordered be removed. As the Committee notes 
in its response to the complaint, the March 24 request for minutes at issue here did not include a 
request for December 4, 2023, executive session minutes. Nonetheless, the Committee explains 
that it released the December 4, 2023, executive session minutes, in compliance with an order 
from the Supervisor of Records, but that the Committee inadvertently failed to update the version 
of the minutes posted online; this has since been rectified. Where the request for minutes at issue 
here did not include a request for December 4, 2023, executive session minutes and where the 
Open Meeting Law does not require that public bodies post meeting minutes online, we find no 
violation with respect to these allegations.  
  
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law in the ways alleged. We now consider the complaint addressed by this 
determination to be resolved. This determination does not address any other complaints that may 
be pending with the Committee or with our office. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 
963-2540 if you have any questions regarding this letter.
