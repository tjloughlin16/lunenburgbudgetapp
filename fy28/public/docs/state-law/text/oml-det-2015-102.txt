Dear Rev. Dr. Schmidt: 
This office received a complaint from Steven Hierman, dated March 28, 2015, alleging 
that the Harvard Municipal Affordable Housing Trust Fund (the "Trust") violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 3OA, §§ 18-25. Specifically, the complaint alleges that the Trust: 1) failed 
to make executive session minutes available within 10 days of request; and 2) failed to limit 
certain executive session discussions to the stated topic. The complaint was originally filed with 
the Trust on March 2, 2015. The Trust responded by letter dated March 19, 2015. 
Following our review, we find that the Trust violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
respond to Mr. Hierman's request for executive session minutes within the timeframe required 
by the law. The Trust did not violate the Open Meeting Law by meeting in executive session on 
eight separate occasions. In reaching this determination, we reviewed the complaint and 
attached documents; the Trust's response and attached documents; and the request for further 
review, filed with our office, along with the attached documents. Additionally, we spoke with 
the Trust's Chair, the Rev. Dr. Gregory W. Schmidt,1 by telephone on May 5, 2015 and reviewed 
a May 6, 2015 letter from Chair Schmidt, addressed to our office. 
We find the facts to be as follows. The Trust is a seven-member public body dedicated to 
the development of affordable housing in the Town of Harvard. In 2013, the Trust began to 
consider purchasing two pieces of property in Harvard for the purpose of developing affordable 
housing. The Trust met in executive session on January 14, 2013; August 5, 2013; November 9, 
2013; April 28, 2014; September 8, 2014; September 22, 2014; December 22, 2014; and January

DISCUSSION 
1. The Trust Failed to Respond within 10 Days of a Request for Executive Session 
Minutes. 
The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to "create and maintain accurate minutes 
of all meetings, including executive sessions." G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2013-47; OML 2013-
16; OML 2012-29.4 Upon request by any person to inspect or copy the minutes of an executive 
session or any portion thereof, the public body must respond to the request within 10 days 
following receipt and must release any such minutes not covered by an exemption. G.L. c. 3OA, 
§ 22(g)(2). If the public body has determined, prior to the request, that the requested executive 
session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester directly, notifying him or her 
of the availability of those minutes. See OML 2013-105; OML 2013-99. If the body previously 
determined that executive session minutes should remain confidential because publication would 
defeat the lawful puiposes of the executive session, it should respond by stating the reason why 
the minutes continue to be withheld. Id. And if, at the time of a request, the public body has not 
conducted a review of the requested minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure is 
warranted, the body must perform such a review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no later 
than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). In such 
circumstances, the body should still respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her 
that it is conducting this review. See OML 2013-99.

2. The Trust's Executive Session Discussions were Lawful under G.L. c. 30A. § 
21(a)(6). 
A public body may enter into executive session for any of ten enumerated purposes. G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21(a). One such purpose allows a public body to enter into executive session to 
"consider the purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property if the chair declares that an 
open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the negotiating position of the public body." 
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6) ("Purpose 6"). 
Over the course of eight executive sessions held during the period from January 2013 to 
January 2015, the Trust discussed the purchase, value and sale of real property. In the early 
executive sessions, the Trust identified and discussed properties to purchase for the purpose of 
developing housing. In the later executive sessions, it discussed selling a property. These 
discussions fit squarely within Puipose 6. See G.L, c. 30A, § 21(a)(6). Therefore, we find that 
the Trust did not violate the Open Meeting Law by meeting in executive session during these 
eight meetings. Although not raised in the complaint, we remind the Trust that it must record in 
the minutes of its meetings the purpose for each executive session. See G.L. c, 30A, § 21(b)(3). 
CONCLUSION 
We find that the Trust violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to Mr. 
Hierman's request for executive session minutes within the timeframe required by the law. The 
Trust did not violate the Open Meeting Law by meeting in executive session on eight separate 
occasions. We order the Trust's immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, 
and caution the Trust that a similar future violation may be considered evidence of an intentional 
violation of the law.
