Dear Attorney Talerman: 
This office received a complaint from Daniel Harlow, dated January 31, 2017, alleging 
that the Kingston Board of Selectmen (the "Board") violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on November 14, 2016, and 
the Board responded by letter dated November 28, 2016. In his complaint, Mr. Harlow alleges 
that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by: (i) entering executive session without a proper 
statutory purpose, (ii) failing to release executive session minutes, and (iii) failing to respond 
appropriately to a request for executive session minutes. 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law. In 
reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board's response, and the 
request for further review filed with our office. We also reviewed supplementary e-mail 
responses sent by Town Counsel on March 16, 2017, and by the Town Administrator on March 
20, 2017.' Finally, we spoke with the Town Administrator by telephone on March 22, 2016. 
We find the facts as follows. The complaint concerns the minutes of the Board's July 12, 
2016 meeting, during which the Board entered executive session to approve and release the 
minutes of its February 11, 2014 meeting. On October 2, 2016, the complainant sent an e-mail 
to Town Administrator Robert Fennessy requesting "all supporting documents and discussions 
([i.e.,] notes of any kind as well as documents official or otherwise) for the approval and 
authorized release of the February 11, 2014 James Judge Executive Session." On October 11, 
2016, Mr. Fennessy responded by e-mail, stating that "the executive session minutes of July 12, 
2016 have not been approved and released by the Board of Selectmen." The Board voted to

DISCUSSION 
I. The Board Entered Executive Session for a Proper Purpose. 
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of public bodies be held in open 
session, with some exceptions. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). Public bodies may enter executive 
session for any of ten statutory purposes. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a). Here, the complaint alleges that 
the Board improperly entered executive session during the July 12, 2016 meeting to approve and 
release the February 11, 2014 executive session minutes. A public body may enter executive 
session to review the minutes of prior executive sessions to determine if they are suitable for 
release. See OML 2016-57; OML 2015-25.2 Accordingly, we find that the Board did not violate 
the Open Meeting Law with respect to this allegation. 
II. The Board was Permitted to Withhold Executive Session Minutes. 
The minutes of executive sessions may be withheld from disclosure so long as release 
may defeat the lawful purpose for the executive session, but no longer, provided that the 
executive session at issue was held in compliance with the Open Meeting Law. G.L. c. 3OA, § 
22(f); OML 2012-115. Here, the complaint alleges that the July 12, 2016 executive session was 
not held for a proper purpose and the Board was therefore not entitled to withhold the minutes of 
that executive session until their release on November 29, 2016. As discussed above, we find 
that the July 12, 2016 executive session was held for a proper puipose, and we therefore find that 
the Board was entitled to withhold the minutes for the duration of the executive session purpose. 
III. The Board Responded Appropriately to the Request for Executive Session 
Minutes. 
A public body must respond to a request for executive session minutes within 10 days. 
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). If the public body or a designee has determined, prior to the request, 
that certain executive session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester 
directly, notifying him or her of the availability of those minutes. See OML 2013-105; OML 
2013-99. If the body previously determined that executive session minutes should remain 
confidential because publication would defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, it 
should respond by stating the reason why the minutes continue to be withheld. Id. And if, at the 
time of a request, the public body has not conducted a review of the minutes to determine 
whether continued nondisclosure is warranted, the body must perform such a review and release 
the minutes, if appropriate, no later than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs 
first. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). In such circumstances, the body should still respond to the 
requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her that it is conducting this review. See OML 2013-
99. 
Here, at the time the complainant requested the July 12, 2016 executive session minutes, 
the Board had recently determined that the purpose for the executive session was still ongoing.
