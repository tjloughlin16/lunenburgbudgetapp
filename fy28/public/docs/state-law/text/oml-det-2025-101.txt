RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint  
 
Dear Attorney Quinn:  
 
This office received a complaint from Stephanie Duclos on April 24, 2025, alleging that 
the Greenfield City Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.  The complaint originally was filed with the Council on March 241 and you responded on 
behalf of the Council by letter dated April 18, pursuant to an extension granted by our office.   
The complaint alleges that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law when it discussed topics 
contained in an amended notice posted less than 48 hours prior to its March 19 meeting. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Council’s response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  In 
addition, we reviewed the notice and amended notice, and the minutes, of the March 19 meeting.  
We communicated with you via email, and reviewed documents forwarded to us at our request, 
including questions which Councilor Katherine Golub emailed to the Mayor and School 
Superintendent on March 18.  Finally, we viewed portions of a recording of the March 19 
meeting.

take place on March 19 at 6:30 p.m.  On March 18, at 8:11 p.m., Councilor Katherine Golub sent 
an email to Mayor Ginny Desorgher and School Superintendent Karin Patenaude, attaching

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at 
the meeting.”  Id.  The list of topics must have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).   
 
A public body may amend a meeting notice within 48 hours of a meeting to include a 
topic that was not reasonably anticipated 48 hours before the meeting and then discuss that topic 
during the meeting.  See 940 CMR 29.03(1)(d).  See also OML 2024-160; OML 2020-122; OML 
2019-131.3  Indeed, a public body may discuss and take action on an unanticipated topic even if 
the topic does not appear on the meeting notice at all.  See OML 2024-57; OML 2020-172; OML 
2019-131.  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on 
its face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2022-148; OML 2015-
109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what the public body actually discussed at the meeting 
to determine whether the notice was sufficiently specific to reasonably inform the public of the 
topic to be discussed.  See OML 2015-109.  When a public body does not actually deliberate on 
a noticed topic, it cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with respect to that topic, even if the 
notice would have been insufficiently specific had the public body proceeded with its 
deliberation.  See OML 2020-53; OML 2015-63; OML 2014-81.   
 
We need not decide whether Councilor Golub’s questions were actually discussed during 
the March 19 meeting when Superintendent Patenaude acknowledged Councilor Golub’s 
questions during her statement at the March 19 meeting.  Because the topics were unanticipated, 
the Open Meeting Law did not prevent the Council from discussing them during the meeting.  
We have seen no information suggesting that Council President Lora Wondolowski, i.e. the chair 
of the public body, anticipated the questions that Councilor Golub circulated the night before the 
meeting.  If the chair does not anticipate a topic and therefore does not include it on the notice, 
that notice is not rendered insufficient by another member who raises the topic during the

meeting, notice of the meeting was not insufficiently specific.  Although we find no violation, we

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law to the extent that it discussed any of the topics that were included in the amended notice of 
the March 19 meeting that was posted on March 18.   
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This
