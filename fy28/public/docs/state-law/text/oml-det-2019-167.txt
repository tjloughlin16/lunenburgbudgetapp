RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint
Dear Mr. Henry:
This office received seven complaints from Mary Ellen Sikes on October 31, 2019, 
alleging that the Weston School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaints were originally filed with the Committee on October 1, 
and you responded on behalf of the Committee by email on October 22.' In her complaints, Ms. 
Sikes alleges that on September 4, in response to a public records request, she received seven 
sets of insufficiently detailed executive session minutes.1 2 In addition, Ms. Sikes alleges that an 
executive session was held during the Committee’s April 9, 2018, meeting, even though the 
meeting notice did not include an executive session.
Following our review, we find that the seven sets of executive session meeting minutes 
were insufficiently detailed, and the Committee therefore violated the Open Meeting Law. 
However, we find that the executive session that was held on April 9, 2018 was unanticipated by 
the Chair and was therefore not required to be listed on the meeting notice. In reaching a 
determination, we reviewed the complaints, the Committee’s responses, and the requests for 
further review. We also reviewed the seven sets of executive session minutes and the April 9, 
2018, meeting notice.

seven sets of executive session minutes. On September 4, she received the minutes, which 
contain the date, time and place of each executive session as well as the members present. The

DISCUSSION
I. The Committee Failed to Create Sufficiently Detailed Minutes for the Seven Executive
Sessions.
The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to “create and maintain accurate minutes 
of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members 
present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other 
exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including 
the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30 A, § 22(a).

When reviewing minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for 
substantial compliance with the accuracy requirement. See OML 2017-104; OML 2013-64.4 By 
substantial compliance, we mean that the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so 
that a member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a 
clear understanding of what occurred. See OML 2019-145; OML 2012-106. Although a 
transcript of the discussions is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark 
or opinion presented, minutes must include a substantive summary of the discussion on each 
topic. See OML 2018-8; OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55.
Here, the Committee’s executive session minutes fall far short of the accuracy 
requirement in the Open Meeting Law. The minutes generally list the topics that were discussed, 
sometimes merely restating the executive session purpose that was included on the meeting 
notice, without any actual summary of the discussion. The Open Meeting Law requires that 
meeting minutes include more than a statement that a public body held a discussion about a 
specified topic; the Law requires that the minutes summarize the discussion that was held. See 
OML 2018-8. We therefore find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
draft and approve accurate minutes for the executive sessions held on October 16, 2017;
February 26, 2018; April 9, 2018; April 24, 2018; November 19, 2018; December 11, 2018; and 
May 7, 2019.
In addition, we take this opportunity to advise the Committee of the Attorney General’s 
recommendations as to best practices for drafting meeting minutes. In particular, when 
summarizing discussions, we encourage public bodies to include dissenting or minority opinions 
whenever possible. Where significant debate on a matter occurs and it is clear that one or more 
members of the body and/or the public disagree with the decision ultimately taken by the public 
body, public bodies should note the opposing viewpoints in the meeting minutes. While it is not 
necessary to record the comments of every speaker, and we acknowledge the difficulty of doing 
so where there are many speakers whose remarks may even overlap, if a particular individual 
speaks at some length or is the only one to offer an argument for or against a proposal, that 
person and his or her comments should be identified in the minutes. See OML 2012-29.
For the reasons stated above, we order the Committee to draft and approve minutes for 
the seven executive sessions that contain a summary of each discussion, with sufficient detail 
and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the 
minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.
We also take this opportunity to remind the Committee that executive session minutes 
may be withheld from disclosure to the public “as long as publication may defeat the lawful 
purposes of the executive session, but no longer.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f). When the purpose for a 
valid executive session has been served, the minutes and any documents or exhibits used at the 
session must be disclosed, unless the attorney-client privilege or an exemption to the Public 
Records Law applies. Id The burden of justifying continued nondisclosure of executive session 
minutes lies with the public body. Foudv v. Amherst-Pelham Regional School Committee. 402 
Mass. 179, 184 (1988). A public body, or its chair or designee, must, at reasonable intervals, 
review the minutes of executive sessions to determine if continued nondisclosure of those

II. The Chair did not Anticipate the Topic That was Discussed in Executive Session on April
9. 2018, and Therefore the Topic Was Not Required to be Listed on the Meeting Notice.
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge. 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed 
at the meeting.” Id (emphasis added). The list of topics shall have sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting. 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). 
Public bodies may discuss a topic not listed on a meeting notice if the topic was not anticipated 
by the chair 48 hours in advance of the meeting. See OML 2017-51; OML 2013-87. However, 
the Attorney General strongly recommends that public bodies, as a matter of best practice, table 
unplanned topics that might be of significant interest to the public in order to include them in a 
meeting notice for a subsequent meeting. See OML 2019-70; OML 2018-119.
Here, the Chair asserts, and we credit the Chair’s statement, that he was unaware of the 
developments in the litigation matter until just before the meeting. Therefore, the executive 
session was unanticipated by the Chair at the time the meeting notice was posted. For this reason, 
we find that the Committee’s notice for its April 9, 2018, meeting did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that that the Committee violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to create accurate executive session minutes for the seven meetings in question. 
However, we find that the April 9, 2018, executive session was unanticipated by the Chair and 
was therefore not required to be listed on the meeting notice. We order the Committee’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution the Committee that a 
determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the Open Meeting Law.
In addition, we order the Committee to re-create and approve minutes for the executive 
sessions held on October 16, 2017; February 26, 2018; April 9, 2018; April 24, 2018; November 
19, 2018; December 11, 2018; and May 7, 2019, and certify to this office within 60 days from 
the date of this letter that it has done so. Although significant time has passed since some of the 
meetings, the Committee should create the minutes to the best of its ability, through whatever 
means are available, including the memories and individual notations of the attendees.
Finally, we order each member of the Committee to view the Attorney General’s 5- 
minute training video on meeting minutes, available at https://www.mass.gov/service- 
details/open-meeting-law-training-videos. and certify to this office within 60 days from the date 
of this letter that they have done so.
