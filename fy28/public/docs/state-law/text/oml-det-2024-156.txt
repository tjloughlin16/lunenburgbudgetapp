 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Superintendent Lynch: 
This office received a complaint from Lisa Medina on March 14, 2024, alleging that the 
Whittier Regional Technical School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on or about 
April 8, 2024, and you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated 
April 30, 2024. The complaint alleges that the public was not provided notice that the Committee 
would consider and vote on budget motions during its March 13, 2024, special meeting. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law. 
In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Committee’s response to 
the complaint, the complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the notice and 
minutes of the Committee’s March 13, 2024, special meeting. 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts to be as follows. On March 6, 2024, the Committee posted a meeting 
notice to its website that stated, “The School Committee will meet on Wednesday, March 13, 
2024 at 5:30 p.m. in the School Committee Room for a budget work session at Whittier Regional 
Vocational Technical High School....” Additionally, the notice included the address and an 
internet link where the public could view the meeting live. The Committee convened as 
scheduled for the budget work session. During the meeting, the Committee considered and voted 
on budget matters. Among topics the Committee discussed were reasons for the budget increases 
and recommended expenditures. Additionally, the Committee voted on three budget items and, 
ultimately, accepted the remainder of the budget as proposed.

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
The notice must be printed in a legible, easily understandable format and must contain the date, 
time and place of the meeting, as well as a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates 
will be discussed. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). The list of topics shall have “sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). 
The Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to anticipate the course of deliberation or 
the outcome of a discussion, nor does the “specificity” requirement generally require a meeting 
notice to indicate that a vote will be taken on a listed topic. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 
29.03(l)(b); OML 2023-186; OML 2019-157; OML 2017-186; OML 2014-58; OML 2012-75.1 
A notice need not include every detail about an anticipated discussion, but the law requires that 
notice topics include “sufficient specificity” so that the public can decide whether to attend the 
meeting and observe the discussions regarding the specific topics anticipated for discussion. 
See OML 2023-108; OML 2018-22; OML 2018-7.  
 
When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its 
fac
e, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone. See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155. 
Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was 
sufficiently specific to make the public aware of the discussion topics. See OML 2021-22; OML 
2020-137; OML 2019-64. We have explained that when a public body anticipates reviewing an 
entire budget or an entire town meeting warrant, for example, it is not required to list all the 
budgetary items or all the warrant articles or all on its meeting notice. See OML 2024-110; OML 
2017-185; OML 2012-83. However, when a public body anticipates discussing particular 
budgetary items or articles, it must specifically list them in its meeting notice. See OML 2024-
27; OML 2013-8; OML 2011-7. 
 
Here, the Committee posted notice of its March 13 special meeting. Alt
 hough the notice 
did not include a separately enumerated list of topics, it described the meeting as a “budget work 
session.” After review, we are satisfied that the Committee anticipated reviewing—and did 
review—the full budget; although it considered and voted on modifications to a few items the 
discussion focused on the budget as a whole. Regarding the complainant’s concern that the 
notice did not sufficiently apprise the public that votes would be taken, we have consistently 
explained that it is reasonably foreseeable that a public body may take action and hold a vote 
following discussion of a topic listed on a meeting notice; the Open Meeting Law does not 
require that the notice separately indicate the possibility of a vote. See, e.g., OML 2023-186; 
OML 2021-153; OML 2019-157; OML 2019-36; OML 2015-66; OML 2012-75.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law in the way alleged. We now consider the complaint addressed by this 
determination to be resolved. This determination does not address any other complaints that may 
be pending with our office or the Committee. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-
2540 if you have any questions regarding this letter.
