 RE:        Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Chair Makepeace O’Neal: 
 
This office received a complaint from Amanda Alix on August 22, 2025, alleging that the 
Shutesbury Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about June 19, and the Board responded 
to the complaint by letter dated July 2.
1  The complaint alleges that the Board posted a notice 
with insufficient specificity for a meeting held on June 17, by failing to list the names of 
individuals to be appointed and / or reappointed to town public bodies. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way 
alleged.  We also find the violation intentional.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the 
original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice and minutes of the Board’s June 
17 meeting.  Finally, we reviewed our determination OML 2024-236, issued in response to a 
prior complaint that the complainant filed with the Board. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a public body shall post 
notic
e of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays 
and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice must be printed in a legible, easily 
understandable format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed.  Id.  The list of topics 
shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific

when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated 
nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2019-70; OML 2015-35; OML 2013-39.2   
 
Here, the notice of the June 17 meeting included the following topic: “Review for Vote, 
FY26 Annual Appointment List.”  The notice did not include the names of any individuals to be 
appointed.  During the meeting, the Board voted to approve the FY26 Annual Appointment List.  
That list contained over 80 names and their respective positions. 
 
We have consistently stated that the names of applicants for appointment or employment 
whic
h are known 48 hours in advance of a meeting should be included on a meeting notice.  See 
OML 2022-217; 2019-24; OML 2017-148.  The Board does not dispute that the appointments 
were reasonably anticipated 48 hours in advance of the June 17 meeting.  Notice for the meeting 
should have included the names of the individuals to be considered by the Board.  As it did not, 
the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it voted to approve the list at the June 17 
meeting.  
 
Finally, we must determine whether this violation was, as the complainant urges, an 
intentional one.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  A n intentional violation is an “act or omission by a 
public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 
29.02.  An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate 
ignorance of the law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates 
the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  In OML 2024-236, issued approximately six months prior to the 
violation at issue in this matter, we found the Board in violation of the same notice requirement 
of the Open Meeting Law.  Like the complaint here, the complaint in OML 2024-236 also 
concerned a failure to include the list of annual appointments in a meeting notice.  We explicitly 
instructed the Board in that determination that “the list of candidates for appointment must be 
included as part of the notice document itself, even if it is several pages long; it is not sufficient 
to reference a separate list that is available elsewhere.”  See OML 2024-236.  As the Board 
disregarded this clear instruction, we find the violation intentional.   
 
For t
he reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting an insufficiently specific notice for the June 17 meeting that failed to identify the 
individual candidates being considered for appointment and / or reappointment.  We order the 
Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements. 
 
Because we find an intentional violation, as explained above, we refer this matter for a 
hearing pursuant to 940 CMR 29.07(3) and recommend that the Board be assessed a fine of $500 
pursuant to G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c) (“Upon the finding of a violation, the attorney general may issue 
an order to . . . impose a civil penalty upon the public body of not more than $1,000 for each 
intentional violation”).  We invite the Board to contact this office to discuss the hearing process 
or a resolution of this matter.
