The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of each meeting “at least 
48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays” and that every 
notice include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies 
must list topics for discussion with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the 
issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to 
include sufficient specificity when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and 
understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35; OML 
2012-71; OML 2011-44.3  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly 
insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2022-154; 
OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting 
to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the public aware of the discussion 
topics.  See OML 2021-22; OML 2020-137; OML 2019-64. 
 
On Friday, September 22, 2023, at 12:04 P.M., the Board posted notice for a meeting to 
be held on Tuesday, September 26, 2023, at 7:00 P.M.  The notice listed 11 main topics.  One 
main topic was “Special Town Meeting Warrant,” under which three specific item descriptions 
were listed that further advised the public of the topics to be discussed, including “Review and 
Discuss Board of Assessor’s article regarding minimum fair cash value requirement for personal 
property accounts”; “Review and discuss articles to Accept Public Ways: Souza Avenue and 
Barros Lane”; and “Select Board to close the October 16, 2023 Special Town Meeting Warrant.”   
 
During the September 26, 2023, meeting, Acting Assistant Town Manager Paige Duncan 
provided the Board with a summary of three new articles to be added to the special town meeting 
warrant.  The Board then discussed the new articles, one of which provided a tax break for small 
businesses and the other two which were for the acceptance of two streets as public ways.  The 
Board then unanimously voted to close the Special Town Meeting Warrant without further 
discussion of two other articles that would be listed on the warrant as those articles had been 
discussed at previous meetings. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Board posted an insufficiently specific notice by not 
ident
ifying the specific town meeting warrant articles it intended to discuss.  Where a public 
body anticipates discussing specific warrant articles, it must specifically list them in its meeting 
notice.  See OML 2021-169; OML 2011-7.  However, where a public body is reviewing all 
articles for a Town Meeting Warrant, it is not required to list all articles on its meeting notice.  
See OML 2012-83.  Here, the Board discussed three of five warrant articles and, although the 
Board did not specifically list on the notice the warrant articles by number, we find that the 
information listed on the notice with respect to each article was sufficient to reasonably advise 
the public of the articles to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); see also OML 
2013-152.  As such, we find that the notice for this meeting was sufficiently specific because it 
informed the public that the Board would be reviewing three special town meeting warrant 
articles and reflected the actual discussions held during the September 26, 2023, meeting.  See 
OML 2021-154; OML 2013-8.
