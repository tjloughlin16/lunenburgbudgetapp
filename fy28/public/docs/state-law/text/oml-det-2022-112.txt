 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Riley: 
This office received a complaint from Elizabeth Cunniff on March 21, 2022, alleging that 
the Groveland Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on December 21, 2021, and you 
responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated January 4, 2022. In her complaint, Ms. Cunniff, 
who is the Groveland Town Clerk, alleges that the Board turned off the local access television 
camera during the final portion of a December 20, 2021, meeting. 
Following our review, we find that Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law. In 
reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the 
complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review. We also reviewed the notice of the 
Board’s December 20, 2021, meeting.  
FACTS 
 We find the facts as follows. On December 16, 2021, at approximately 3:27 p.m., the 
Board posted a notice for a December 20, 2021, meeting.1 The notice specified that the meeting 
would be held at 6:30 p.m. at Town Hall, and listed the street address of the Town Hall.  
 
 
1 The December 20, 2021, meeting was originally posted as a joint meeting with the Groveland Finance Committee. 
However, as the Finance Committee did not have quorum of members in attendance, the Finance Committee did not 
hold a meeting as defined by the Open Meeting Law. A meeting is defined as “a deliberation by a public body with 
respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.” G.L. c. 30A, § 18.

 The Board met as planned. Like many Groveland public meetings, the meeting was 
recorded and livestreamed by the local access television station. Prior to discussing the final 
agenda item, the Board voted 3-1 to shut off the local access television camera. After the camera 
was turned off, the Board’s meeting remained open to the members of the public who attended 
the meeting in-person and no person was removed by the Board from the in-person meeting.   
DISCUSSION 
 The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement 
that meetings of public bodies be open and accessible to the public. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except 
when meeting in executive session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”). 
Generally, access to a meeting must include the opportunity to be physically present, as well as 
to see and hear what is being discussed by the members of the public body. See OML 2020-100; 
2017-135; OML 2016-54.2 During the COVID-19 pandemic, public bodies have been permitted 
to meet entirely remotely, provided that the public body provides adequate, alternative means of 
public access to the deliberations of the public body.3 “Adequate, alternative means” may 
include, without limitation, providing public access through telephone, internet, or satellite 
enabled audio or video conferencing or any other technology that enables the public to clearly 
follow the proceedings of the public body in real time. See OML 2021-135. 
 Ms. Cunniff alleges that the Board denied the public access to its December 20, 2021, 
meeting when the Board directed the local access television operator to stop filming prior to the 
discussion of the last agenda item. Because the Board held its December 20, 2021, meeting in-
person at a location that was physically open and accessible to the public, and the notice only 
specified the in-person location for the meeting, the Board was not required to provide a method 
of alternative remote access to the meeting. Furthermore, the Open Meeting Law does not 
require public bodies to record or transcribe, or otherwise broadcast, their public meetings. G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(e); OML 2013-8; OML 2010-3. We therefore find no violation of the Open Meeting 
Law with respect to this allegation.  
 Finally, although not raised in the complaint, we take this opportunity to remind the 
Board that “[a]fter notifying the chair of the public body, any person may make a video or audio 
recording of an open session of a meeting of a public body, or may transmit the meeting through 
any medium.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20 (f). In other words, any individual–whether a member of the 
public, an employee, or a representative of a local access television station—may record or 
transmit an open session meeting of a public body, and a public body may not discourage or 
prohibit such a recording. See OML 2018-4; OML 2012-24.

CONCLUSION 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law as alleged. We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. 
This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or 
the Board. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any questions.
