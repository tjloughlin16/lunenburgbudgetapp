 RE: Revision of OML 2025-173 
 
Dear Attorney Reilly:  
 
On October 24, 2025, our office issued a determination, OML 2025-173, resolving a 
complaint filed by Lisa Bienvenue against the Chicopee City Council (the “Council”).  In that 
letter, the Attorney General concluded that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting a notice for its March 10, 2025, meeting with one insufficiently specific topic.  Because 
the Council posted a notice containing almost the identical topic that it had previously been 
advised by an order of the Attorney General was insufficiently specific and violated the Open 
Meeting Law, the Attorney General found the Council’s violation in this regard to be intentional 
and referred the matter for a hearing pursuant to 940 CMR 29.07(3).     
 
In order to resolve this matter without need for a hearing, the parties have entered into a 
settlement agreement.  As part of the settlement agreement, the Attorney General has agreed to 
revise determination OML 2025-173 to clarify that the finding of an intentional violation was 
based only on the fact that the Council had been previously advised that the above-mentioned 
conduct violated the Open Meeting Law and not that the Council intended to deceive the public.  
Accordingly, we reissue the determination with this revision.

This office received a complaint from Lisa Bienvenue on June 2, 2025, alleging that the 
Chicopee City Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.
1  
The complaint was originally filed with the Council on March 12, and you responded, on behalf 
of the Council, by letter dated April 16, following an extension of time granted by this office.  
The complaint alleges that the Council posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a meeting 
held on March 10.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting a notice with one insufficiently specific topic; we further find that this was an intentional 
violation of the Law.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Council’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice, minutes and video recording of the Council meeting 
held on March 10, as well as the notice, minutes and video recording of the Ordinance 
Committee meeting held on March 10.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Council duly posted notice for a meeting to be held on 
March 10 at 6:30 P.M.  The notice listed the following four topics: “Approval of Real Estate 
Transactions”; “Chapter 7 Ordinance – Subcommittee Report”; “165 Front Street – Cabotville 
security funding request”; and “Appointment of Karla Bourassa as Chief Human Resource 
Officer.”   
 
The Ordinance Committee, which is a subcommittee of the Council, duly posted notice 
for a meeting to be held on March 10 at 6:00 P.M.  The first topic on the notice was listed as 
follows:  
 
BE IT ORDAINED THAT CHAPTER 7 OF THE ORDINANCES FOR THE 
CITY OF CHICOPEE AS AMENDED BE AND IS HEREBY FURTHER 
AMENDED BY STRIKING OUT SALARIES AS THEY PERTAIN TO THE 
FOLLOWING NAMED ACCOUNTS AND INSERTING IN PLACE THEREOF 
THE FOLLOWING: 
TO BE OPERATIVE FEBRUARY 18, 2025 
OFFICE OF THE MAYOR (GROUP 2) 
a. SPECIAL PROJECTS MANAGER  $52,450.56 (GRADE 5) 
b. COMMUMITY & CONSTITUENT LIAISON $52,450.56 (GRADE 5) 
 
The March 10 meeting of the Ordinance Committee was held as planned.  During the 
meeting, the Ordinance Committee heard from the Mayor who stated that that he took two 
existing positions, Special Projects Manager and Community & Constituent Liaison, and created 
“pay parity” explaining that the duties and salaries between the two positions have now been 
split more evenly.  The Committee unanimously voted to approve the ordinance. 
 
The March 10 Council meeting was held as planned and followed the Ordinance 
Committee meeting.  During the meeting, the Council discussed the need for security of a 
privately owned building located at 165 Front Street.  The Building Commissioner explained that 
the current owner of the property will not continue to pay for security of the abandoned building, 
but the City is obligated to secure the site.  The Building Commissioner informed the Council 
that the City plans to board up the first floor of the building and hire a security company to check 
the building and walk the perimeter of the property once a day for a cost of $25 per day.  After 
discussing whether the security costs can be recouped through liens and the likelihood of a 
potential sale of the property, the Council voted by majority vote to approve the funding 
appropriation.  
 
Next, the Council heard from the Mayor who asked the Council to consider Ordinance 7 
which splits duties and equal pay between two existing positions, Special Projects Manager and 
Community & Constituent Liaison, in the Mayor’s Office.  The Council unanimously voted by 
roll call to approve the Ordinance.  Finally, the Mayor informed the Council that he had hired 
Karla Bourassa as the Chief Human Resources Officer and asked the Council to confirm the 
appointment.  After hearing from Ms. Bourassa and briefly discussing the appointment, the 
Council voted by majority vote to refer the appointment to the Human Resources Committee.  In

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a public body shall post 
notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays 
and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice must be printed in a legible, easily 
understandable format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed.  Id.  The list of topics 
shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific 
when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated 
nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35; OML 2013-39.
3  When reviewing a 
meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a 
meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2022-154; OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, 
we review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently 
specific to make the public aware of the discussion topics.  See OML 2021-22; OML 2020-137; 
OML 2019-64. 
 
The complaint alleges that three of the four topics listed on the March 10 meeting notice 
were insufficiently specific.  Specifically, the complaint alleges that the topics – “Chapter 7 
Ordinance – Subcommittee Report,” “165 Front Street – Cabotville security funding request,” 
and “Appointment of Karla Bourassa as Chief Human Resource Officer” – all had “monies 
involved and the values were not listed.”  A public body need not list each and every detail about 
a topic, nor anticipate on the notice the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion.  
See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134.  However, notice topics must be described 
with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting so that the public can make an informed decision about whether to attend the meeting in 
order to observe an anticipated discussion.  See OML 2023-86; OML 2019-95; OML 2018-22; 
OML 2015-74.   
 
We find that the notice included sufficient specificity with respect to two of the topics - 
“165 Front Street – Cabotville security funding request” and “Appointment of Karla Bourassa as 
Chief Human Resource Officer.”  Here, the notice informed the public that the Council would be 
discussing a funding request for security of property located at 165 Front Street and the 
appointment of a Chief Human Resource Officer.  Where that is in fact what the Council 
discussed, the notice was sufficient to comply with the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2020-43; 
OML 2015-35; OML 2014-58.  The Open Meeting Law does not require the level of detail 
requested in the complaint.  See OML 2021-181. 
 
By contrast, we find, for the reasons explained in OML 2023-24, a determination we 
issued to the Council on March 1, 2023, that the topic “Chapter 7 Ordinance – Subcommittee

Report” listed on the March 10 notice was insufficiently specific.  In OML 2023-24, we found 
that the topic “A favorable Ordinance Committee report: Chapter 7 Ordinance Revisions” was 
insufficiently specific and that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law.  Thus, we again find 
that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law by posting a notice with an insufficiently 
specific topic.  We express our concern that the Council posted a notice containing almost the 
identical topic that we previously advised was insufficiently specific.  Thus, because we 
previously advised the Council that such a topic was insufficiently specific and in violation of 
the Open Meeting Law, we find the violation here to be intentional.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 18; 940 
CMR 29.02; OML 2023-24.  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body or a 
member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting 
Law.  Id.  Moreover, we specifically advised the Council in OML 2023-24 that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  We find this violation to be 
intentional based on the fact that the Council had been previously advised that this conduct 
violated the law and not based on any finding that the Council intended to deceive the public. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Council violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting a notice with insufficient specificity with respect to one of the four topics listed on the 
March 10 meeting notice.  Furthermore, we find this violation to be intentional.  We order the 
Council’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements.  Additionally, we refer 
this matter for a hearing pursuant to 940 CMR 29.07(3) and recommend that the Council be 
assessed a fine of $500.00 pursuant to G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c) (“Upon the finding of a violation, the 
attorney general may issue an order to . . . impose a civil penalty upon the public body of not 
more than $1,000 for each intentional violation”).  We invite the Council to contact this office to 
discuss the hearing process or a resolution of this matter.  
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Council.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
