RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Mr. McInerney: 
 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on August 13, 2024, alleging that 
the Northborough Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about July 16, 2024, and you 
responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated August 13, 2024, following an extension of 
time granted by our office.
1  The complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely approve 
minutes of the meeting held on May 6.2 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by not 
timely approving minutes of the May 6, meeting and that this was an intentional violation of the 
Open Meeting Law.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the

Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review.  We also reviewed the notices and open session minutes of the Board meetings held on 
May 6, May 13, June 3, June 13, and July 15. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The minutes of an open session meeting 
must be reviewed and approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” 
means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless 
the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.
3  
Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  
See OML 2014-15; OML 2012-91.  
 
The Board held meetings on May 6, May 13, May 20, June 3, and June 17.  Because the 
Board met three times within 30 days following its May 6 meeting, it had 30 days, or until June 
5, by which to approve the May 6 minutes.  See 940 CMR 29.11(2); OML 2018-152; OML 
2018-82.  The Board did not approve the minutes of the May 6 meeting until July 15, which was 
71 days thereafter.   
 
The Board explains that it discussed the minutes of the May 6 meeting during its meeting 
held on J
une 17, but did not approve the minutes because a Board member suggested certain 
edits to the minutes.  Therefore, the Board delayed approval of the minutes in order to make such 
edits, and then approved the minutes during its next meeting on July 15.  We have previously not 
found a violation of the Open Meeting Law where a public body made a good faith effort to 
approve the meeting minutes within a reasonable period of time.  See OML 2021-177 (finding no 
violation where public body engaged in a good faith effort to approve meeting minutes after 
finding errors in initial drafts); OML 2018-63 (finding no violation where minutes were ready for 
approval and reviewed by public body but body delayed approval to correct inaccuracies); OML 
2015-190 (finding no violation when the public body had repeatedly made good faith efforts to 
approve minutes through multiple rounds of edits in an effort to reach a consensus among its 
members).  While we credit the Board’s explanation, the Board’s June 17 meeting was already 
its fourth meeting and more than 30 days after the May 6 meeting.  We remind the Board that we 
have consistently explained that neither the Open Meeting Law nor the regulations make any 
distinction between “regular” meetings and other types of meetings for purposes of timely 
approval of minutes.  See OML 2019-115 (no exception for “single topic” meetings or meetings 
not “normally scheduled”); OML 2017-103 (public body should have created complete minutes 
for its “workshop” meetings because topics discussed were matters within the public body’s 
jurisdiction).  Therefore, we do not find good cause for the Board’s delay in its approval of the 
minutes of the May 6 meeting, and we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to approve the minutes in a timely manner.

We must determine whether this violation was, as the complainant urges, an intentional 
one.  S
ee G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body 
or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting 
Law.  Id.  The Board was advised by the Attorney General eight months ago, on January 29, of 
its obligation to review and approve minutes in a timely manner and that timely manner means 
within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later.  See OML 
2024-19.  Moreover, we explained that neither the Open Meeting Law nor the regulations make 
any distinction between “regular” meetings and other types of meetings for purposes of timely 
approval of minutes.  See OML 2019-153; OML 2019-115; OML 2017-103.  Finally, we 
specifically advised the Board that similar future violations could be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.  Because the Board had previously been advised that the failure to 
approve minutes within the next three meetings or 30 days, whichever is longer, violates the 
Open Meeting Law, we find that this violation is intentional.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 18; 940 CMR 
29.02; OML 2013-63.  However, we decline to recommend the imposition of a civil penalty in 
this instance where the minutes at issue were approved before the complaint was filed with the 
Board.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c); OML 2022-184; OML 2018-108; OML 2015-122.  
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board’s failure to timely approve the 
minutes of its May 6 meeting was an intentional violation of the law.  We order the Board’s 
immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements and caution the Board that a future 
finding of an intentional violation of the Open Meeting Law may result in a civil penalty of up to 
$1,000 per violation.  Additionally, we order all members of the Board to attend a webinar 
training on the Open Meeting Law conducted by our office within the next 90 days,
4 and to 
certify to our office that each Board member has completed the webinar training within the next 
95 days. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Board or with 
our office.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
