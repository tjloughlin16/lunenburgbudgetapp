RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint  
 
Dear Attorneys Clemens and Feodoroff:  
 
This office received a complaint from Roxanne Gunther on January 27, 2025, alleging 
that the Monson Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.  The complaint originally was filed with the Board on December 12, 2024, and you 
responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated January 15, 2025.1  The complaint alleges that 
notice of the Board’s December 10 meeting was insufficiently specific.   
 
Foll
owing our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law as alleged 
in the complaint.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Board’s response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  In addition, 
we reviewed the notice and minutes of the December 10 meeting. 
 
FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  On December 6, the Board posted on the municipal website 
notice of a meeting to take place on December 10.  The sixth item under “New Business” was 
“Annual License Renewals – list is on file with the Town Clerk.”  The list was four pages long.  
On December 10, the meeting went forward as scheduled and the Board discussed and voted on 
the license renewals.

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at 
the meeting.”  Id.  For local public bodies, notice must be posted in a manner conspicuously 
visible to the public at all hours in or on the municipal building in which the clerk’s office is 
located.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(c).  A municipality may designate a website as an alternative notice 
posting location for all public bodies within the municipality.  940 CMR 29.03(2)(b); OML 
2024-228.  The Town of Monson did so in January 2018.    
 
The list of topics on a notice must have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider 
a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic 
and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2023-3; OML 
2019-134; OML 2015- 35.2  The Open Meeting Law requires that the notice describe topics with 
sufficient specificity so that the public can make an informed decision whether to attend the 
meeting in order to observe the discussion regarding a topic of interest.  See OML 2023-86; 
OML 2019-95; OML 2018-22.   
 
Where a topic requires a public body’s approval or other specific decision, such as annual 
license renewals or appointment of candidates to public bodies, the specific applicants or 
candidates should be listed on the notice itself; it is not sufficient to reference a separate list that 
is available elsewhere.  See OML 2024-236; OML 2020-20; OML 2019-22.3  Because the notice 
of the December 10 meeting did not include the specific licenses that were discussed at the 
meeting, it was not sufficiently specific.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting an insufficiently specific notice for its December 10 meeting.  We order immediate and 
future compliance with the law’s requirements and we caution that similar future violations may 
be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
