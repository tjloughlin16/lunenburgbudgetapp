 RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Mr. Meeker: 
 
This office received a complaint from Christopher Kelleher on December 18, 2024, 
alleging that the Brookfield Conservation Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Commission 
on or about September 5, 2024, and you responded, on behalf of the Commission, by email dated 
September 30, 2024.  The complaint alleges that the Commission posted an insufficiently 
specific notice with respect to an executive session topic for a meeting held on September 19, 
2024. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Commission did not violate Open Meeting Law in 
the way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Commission’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting 
further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice, open session minutes and video recording of 
the Commission meeting held on September 19, 2024.1  Finally, we communicated with the 
Commission on May 6, 2025. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
noticed and open to the public, unless an executive session is convened.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-

(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that 
a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each 
member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly 
announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.2   
 
The chair of the public body must announce in open session the purpose for the executive 
session, “stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which 
the executive session was called.”  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b)(3); see also District Attorney 
for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of 
the reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification 
given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the 
public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  This level of detail 
about the executive session topic must also be included in the meeting notice.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); OML 2016-72.  
 
One permissible reason to convene in executive session is “to discuss strategy with 
respect to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on 
the bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).  This purpose offers the narrow opportunity to discuss strategy with 
respect to litigation that is pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise 
demonstrably likely; the mere possibility of litigation is not sufficient to invoke Purpose 3.  See 
Doherty v. School Committee of Boston, 386 Mass. 643, 648 (1982); Perryman v. School 
Committee of Boston, 17 Mass. App. Ct. 346, 352 (1983); OML 2012-05.   
 
 The Commission posted notice of a meeting to be held on September 19, 2024.  The 
notice listed 10 topics, one of which was an executive session under Purpose 3 “To discuss 
strategy with respect to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a 
detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so 
declares.”  The September 19, 2024, meeting was held as planned and the Commission first 
convened in open session.  Chair William Meeker then announced that the Commission would 
convene in executive session under Purpose 3 “to discuss strategy with respect to collective 
bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or 
litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares,” and that the Commission would 
reconvene in open session after the conclusion of the executive session.  The Commission 
unanimously voted by roll call to convene in executive session and remained in executive for 
approximately one hour before reconvening in open session and discussing the remaining noticed 
topics.   
 
A public body is not required to provide the specific statutory citation to the executive 
session purpose on which it relies, provided that the public body provides a sufficient description 
of the executive session topic in order to ensure that the topic falls within one of the permissible

executive session purposes.  See OML 2022-46; OML 2021-184; OML 2016-95.  Here, the 
notice posted by the Commission for the September 19, 2024, meeting included the specific 
executive session purpose, that is Purpose 3, and then quoted the full text of Purpose 3.  When 
reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not 
review a meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we 
review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently 
detailed to make the public aware of the discussion topics.  See OML 2015-101; OML 2015-21.  
The Commission explains that the discussions in executive session pertained to ongoing 
litigation, that the Chair believed that stating more would compromise the purpose for executive 
session, and therefore “it was in the best interest to not disclose more information regarding the 
move to executive session.”  When convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 3, a 
public body should identify the litigation matter to be discussed, if doing so will not compromise 
the lawful purpose for secrecy.  See OML 2016-12; OML 2013-97.  While we generally defer to 
a public body’s assessment of whether the inclusion of such information would compromise the 
purpose for the executive session, a public body must be able to demonstrate a reasonable basis 
for such a claim if challenged.  See OML 2015-14. 
 
 We find that the Commission’s notice for the September 19, 2024, executive session 
made clear which purpose the Commission relied on to enter executive session.  After 
discussions with the Commission, we understand the nature of the matters discussed in executive 
session and the Commission’s reason for believing that identifying the matter would have 
comprised the purpose for the executive session.  Therefore, we are satisfied that the 
Commission posted a sufficiently specific notice and that the Commission had a reasonable basis 
for concluding that inclusion of the specific litigation matter on the notice would have 
compromised the purpose for the executive session.  See OML 2020-71; OML 2017-178; OML 
2017-87.  As such, we find that the Commission did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  
Although we do not find a violation here, we note that specifically identifying in the notice and 
the announcement in open session whether the executive session will be to discuss collective 
bargaining or litigation would provide additional transparency and clarity that the discussions in 
executive session fall within a proper purpose.
