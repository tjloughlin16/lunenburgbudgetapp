RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint  
 
Dear Chair Kelly:  
 
This office received a complaint from Christopher Harry on October 16, 2025, alleging 
that the Brimfield Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint originally was filed with the Board on September 16 and you responded 
on behalf of the Board by letter dated October 7.1  The complaint alleges (1) that topics listed on 
the notice for the Board's August 28 meeting were insufficiently specific; and (2) that you 
mischaracterized statements that he made on social media as part of a calculated character 
attack.2 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it 
discussed topics at the August 28 meeting which were not listed with sufficient specificity on the 
notice of the meeting.  We decline to review the remaining allegation of the complaint.  In 
reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the 
complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  In addition, we viewed the notice 
and minutes of the August 28 meeting.  Finally, we communicated with Mr. Harry and with 
Administrative Assistant to the Select Board Chris DeFalco via email. 
 
FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  On August 16, Mr. Harry posted to his Facebook account 
regarding emergency management at the flea market, the “Hitchcock property,” and the Building 
Committee.  On August 25, the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on August 28.  The

notice included several headings in bold, one of which was “Open Public Forum and Response to

The meeting went forward as scheduled on August 28.  After a brief statement by a

DISCUSSION 
 
I. Notice of the August 28 meeting was not sufficiently specific. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at 
the meeting.”  Id.  The list of topics must have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).   
 
We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of 
the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body's 
discussion.  See OML 2023-3; OML 2019-134; OML 2015- 35.3  The Open Meeting Law 
requires that the notice describe topics with sufficient specificity so that the public can make an 
informed decision whether to attend the meeting to observe the discussion regarding a topic of 
interest.  See OML 2023-86; OML 2019-95; OML 2018-22.   
 
Generic notice topics do not satisfy the requirement of specificity.  See OML 2025-184; 
OML Declination 7-19-23 (Fairhaven Historical Commission) (noting that “generic headings are 
insufficient when more specific information is known”); OML 2022-179.  For example, we have 
previously stated that “Chairman’s Report” does not sufficiently describe a topic on a meeting 
notice.  See OML 2012-34.  The chair is required to list with specificity those topics they 
reasonably anticipate will be discussed.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  See also OML 2012-34.  A 
recurring feature at a public body’s meetings, e.g., a “Chairman’s Report,” may be included as a 
heading on the notice but the specific topics of discussion must also be included.  See OML 
2023-199.  See also OML 2022-234 (noting that “if the chair anticipates discussing a topic under 
‘Old Business’ or ‘New Business’ more than 48 hours prior to the meeting, then that specific 
topic must be listed in the meeting notice”); OML 2015-74 (“Best practice is. . . to use ‘Old 
Business’ as a heading below which more specific topics are listed.”).  Boilerplate topics and 
placeholder topics lack sufficient specificity to satisfy the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2023-
150; OML 2021-121; OML 2013-164.

The Board misreads the complaint.  In its response, the Board maintains that the Chair 
enjoyed discretion to speak for longer than the five minutes allotted under the Board’s own Open 
Public Forum Policy.  While this may be true, and the Open Meeting Law does not limit the time 
allotted to members of a public body or members of the public, the Board has not addressed the 
allegation of the complaint that notice was insufficient.  The Board does not assert that the Chair 
learned of the complainant’s Facebook post less than 48 hours prior to the meeting.  Because the 
topic was reasonably anticipated, the notice was insufficiently specific, in violation of the Open 
Meeting Law.  Cf. OML 2024-190 (member’s statement at meeting was not reasonably 
anticipated when it was made in response to letter to the editor published hours prior to meeting). 
 
II. We decline to review the allegation that the complainant’s statements were 
mischaracterized. 
 
The complainant also alleges that Chair Kelly attacked the complainant’s character when 
Chair Kelly mischaracterized the content of the Facebook post.  The Division’s review is limited 
to compliance with the Open Meeting Law.  In general, the Open Meeting Law does not govern 
the content of a public body’s discussions.  See OML 2018-43, n. 3; OML 2020-167.  Although 
we certainly discourage uncivil and unprofessional behavior, an allegation of unprofessional 
conduct or incivility does not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2024 -
88; OML 2020-36; OML 2019-166; OML Declination 6-28-16 (Kingston Board of Selectmen).  
Therefore, we decline to review this allegation. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting an insufficiently specific notice for its August 28 meeting.  We order immediate and 
future compliance with the law’s requirements and we caution that similar future violations may 
be considered evidence of intent to violate the law. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any questions.
