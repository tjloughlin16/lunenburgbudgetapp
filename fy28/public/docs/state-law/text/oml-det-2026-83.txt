 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Schuko: 
 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins (the “Complainant”) on March 4, 
2026, alleging that the New Bedford School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on 
February 2
1 and you responded on behalf of the Committee by letter dated February 19. The 
complaint alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to a 
request for meeting minutes within 10 days. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law in the 
way alleged. In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaint, the 
Committee’s response to the complaint, and the Committee’s meeting minutes for the years 2024 
and 2025.2

The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The Open Meeting 
Law further provides that “minutes of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in 
draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 days.” G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(c). When a public body receives a request for executive session minutes, the body must also 
respond to the request within 10 days. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). If at the time of the request 
the public body has not recently performed a periodic review of the requested minutes, as 
provided by G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1), the body must perform the review and release the non-
exempt minutes, or any portion thereof, not later than the body’s next meeting or 30 days, 
whichever occurs first. Id. If this review is required, the public body must still respond to the 
requester within 10 days of receiving the request, informing the requester that the public body 
will conduct a review to determine whether the minutes may be withheld or must be released. 
See OML 2023-62; OML Declination 10-15-2023 (Hubbardston Select Board).
3 
 
Requests for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law or the Open 
Meeting Law, depending on how the request is made, and the requirements for responding differ. 
A request for minutes made directly to the public body falls under the Open Meeting Law and 
requires a response within 10 calendar days, while a request made to a municipal employee 
would likely trigger the Public Records Law and requires a response within 10 business days. 
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c); G.L. c. 66, § 10; OML 2023-33. The Open Meeting Law’s 10 calendar day 
requirement applies whether the minutes are already publicly available, under review, or only in 
draft form. See OML 2024-212; OML 2025-50; OML 2026-11. However, the Open Meeting 
Law does not specify the form in which minutes must be made available. Therefore, directing a 
requester to a website where the requested minutes are posted and accessible complies with the 
requirement to make the minutes “available.” See OML 2024-212.  
 
Here, on January 5, the Complainant sent an email to Mayor Jonathan Mitchell, as the 
Chair of the Committee, requesting a copy of “all meeting minutes . . ., both open and executive 
session for calendar years 2024 and 2025.” Because the request for meeting minutes was directed 
to the Chair of the Committee, the request fell under the Open Meeting Law and required a 
response within 10 calendar days. The Committee responded 11 calendar days later on January 
16, and included a link to the Committee’s website where the meeting minutes were available.
4 
We therefore find that, while it was proper for the Committee to direct the Complainant to the 
webpage where minutes were posted, neither the Committee nor anyone acting on its behalf 
responded to the request within the 10 calendar days required by the Open Meeting Law. 
Accordingly, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law.  
 
In instances where all appropriate corrective action has already been taken, such as here 
where the Committee provided access to the minutes prior to the complaint being filed, we urge 
complainants not to consume resources by filing complaints or requesting further review where 
the transparency purpose of any such complaint or review has already been addressed. See OML 
2026-12; OML 2024-224; OML 2023-113; OML 2017-191; OML 2012-46. Indeed, the Open

Meeting Law complaint process is designed to allow public bodies to take corrective action and 
address concerns raised in a complaint without this office’s involvement. See G.L. c. 30A, § 
23(b) (“At least 30 days prior to the filing of a complaint with the attorney general, the 
complainant shall file a written complaint with the public body, setting forth the circumstances 
which constitute the alleged violation and giving the body an opportunity to remedy the alleged 
violation.”). Pursuing such complaints takes up scarce state and local resources without 
advancing the Open Meeting Law’s goal of improved governmental transparency. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to respond to a request for minutes within the 10 days required by the Law. We order 
the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Law and caution that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the Law.  
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Committee or 
with our office. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
