DISCUSSION 
I. The Committee was Not Required to Review its Executive Session Minutes in Response 
to the Complainant’s Public Records Request.   
 Most requests for government  documents are governed by the Public Records Law. See 
950 C.M.R. 32.06(2)(e). However, the Open Meeting Law also provides a mechanism for the 
public to request meeting minutes. G.L. c. 30A, § 22. A request for meeting minutes that is 
made under the Open Meeting Law rather than the Public Records Law imposes additional 
obligations on the public body. If a request is made under the Open Meeting Law, “[minutes of 
an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available 
upon request by any person within 10 days.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).8 Executive session minutes, 
however, may be withheld from disclosure to the public, so long as publication may defeat the 
lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f). If a public body 
has determined, prior to the request, that the requested executive session minutes may be 
released, the body must respond to the requester directly, notifying him or her as to whether 
those minutes are available. See OML 2015-53.9 And if, at the time of a request, the public 
body has not conducted a review of the minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure 
is warranted, the body must perform such a review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no 
later than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). In 
such circumstances, the body must still respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying him 
or her that it is conducting this review. See OML 2020-16; OML 2015-53. 
 Here, Ms. Potts sent her reque st for minutes and other documents to both the Committee 
Chair and the Superintendent, specifically citing the Public Records Law. Where, as here, the 
requestor clearly identifies the request as a Public Records request, the public body cannot be 
faulted for treating the request as such. We therefore find that the request for meeting minutes 
was not a request that fell under the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c), (g)(2), and did 
not impose upon the Committee the additional obligation to review its executive session 
minutes for possible public release. Therefore, the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law in this regard.

II. The Committee Failed to Timely Approve Numerous Sets of Executive Session Meeting 
Minutes.  
 
 The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting minutes must be reviewed 
and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). The Open Meeting Law itself does not 
define “timely manner.” However, in 2017 the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law 
regulations provided that “timely manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or 
within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further 
delay.” 940 C.M.R. 29.11 (2).10 Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting be 
approved at the next meeting. See OML 2019-39; OML 2014-15; OML 2012-91. The 
requirement that meeting minutes be approved in a timely manner applies equally to minutes of 
executive sessions. See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2019-115. 
 Here, the Committee failed to approve minutes in a timely manner on numerous 
occasions. Minutes from executive sessions held on February 12, 2018, April 30, 2018, May 14, 
2018, and June 14, 2018, were approved over a year later, on September 19, 2019.11 In addition, 
one set of minutes from an executive session held on April 2, 2018, had not been approved as of 
May 2020, over two years late. As these minutes were not approved within three meetings or 30 
days, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law. The timeliness of the specific

III. The Committee Failed to Periodically Review its Outstanding Executive Session Minutes 
for Release.  
 
Executive session minutes may be withheld from disclosure to the public “as long as 
publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer.” G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 22(1). When the purpose for a valid executive session has been served, the minutes and any 
documents or exhibits used at the session must be disclosed, unless the attorney-client privilege 
or an exemption to the Public Records Law applies. Id. The burden of justifying continued 
nondisclosure of executive session minutes lies with the public body. Foudy v. Amherst-Pelham 
Regional School Committee, 402 Mass. 179, 184 (1988). 
 
A public body or its chair or designee must, at reasonable intervals, review the minutes of 
executive sessions to determine if continued nondisclosure of those minutes is warranted. G.L. c. 
30A, § 22(g)(1). Although “reasonable interval” is not defined by the Law, our office has found 
a quarterly review or a review every six months to be appropriate. See OML 2019-45; OML 
2017-104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180. At the meeting following each periodic review, the 
public body must announce which executive session minutes will be released and which will 
continue to be withheld, and such announcement shall be included in the minutes. G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 22(g)(1); OML 2019-3; OML 2015-94; OML 2013-56. A public body’s obligation to review 
executive session minutes for possible release is ongoing. If a public body reviews executive 
session minutes and determines that the purpose for the executive session remains such that the 
minutes should continue to be withheld from the public, it must continue to review those same 
executive session minutes at reasonable intervals until it determines that the reason for secrecy 
has expired. 
 
Here, when asked when the Committee reviewed executive session minutes since January 
of 2014 to determine if continued nondisclosure was warranted, the Committee could only 
identify two reviews taking place at its meetings on September 18, 2017, and September 19, 
2019.12 Because the Committee went two years or longer between reviews, we find that the 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to review its executive session minutes at 
reasonable intervals to determine whether continued nondisclosure was warranted or whether the 
minutes could be released to the public.

Here, we find that the Committee approved dozens of sets of executive session meeting

 Many of the minutes merely state that a discussion 
took place on a given topic. Even more egregiously, some of the meeting minutes only cite to the 
Open Meeting Law executive session purpose as a stand in for any summary of the discussion 
that took place. The Open Meeting Law requires that meeting minutes include more than a 
statement that a public body held a discussion about a specified topic; the Law requires that the 
minutes summarize the substance of the public body's discussion. See OML 2020-53; OML 
2019-167. We therefore find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
draft and approve accurate minutes for dozens of executive sessions from 2014 through 2018.  
 
In situations where a public body has failed to create sufficiently detailed minutes, we 
generally order the public body to recreate those minutes to the best of its ability. However, as 
some of the Committee’s meeting minutes are many years old, we acknowledge the infeasibility 
of accurately capturing the substance of those meetings after such a substantial amount of time 
has passed. We therefore order the Committee to create sufficiently detailed meeting minutes for 
the following executive sessions to the best of its ability, through whatever means are available, 
including the memories and individual notations of the attendees: 1/8/2018; 1/29/2018;

within ninety (90) days of the Committee’s receipt of this letter. Specifically, the Committee

 The Open Meeting Law was enacted to “eliminate much of the sec recy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” See Ghiglione v. School 
Committee of Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). As mentioned above, a public body may 
enter into executive session to “conduct strategy sessions in preparation for negotiations with 
nonunion personnel or to conduct collective bargaining sessions or contract negotiations with 
nonunion personnel.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2). However, the Law does not authorize a public 
body to approve a contract in executive session. See OML 2011-28 (finding that a school 
committee violated the Open Meeting Law by voting to approve a superintendent's contract 
addendum in executive session). A public body may reach an agreement on the terms of a 
contract in executive session, but then must ratify any terms agreed to immediately upon 
returning to open session. Id. See also OML 2019-51 (“Purpose 2 does not allow a public body 
to execute a final agreement behind closed doors; any vote to execute an agreed-upon contract 
must take place in open session.”).  
 The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by voting on Febru ary 3, 2015, to give 
Superintendent Marsden a 3% retroactive pay increase for the 2014-2015 year in executive 
session. See OML 2012-66. Although negotiation of the Superintendent’s contract was an 
appropriate subject for executive session, once the negotiations concluded, the Committee was 
obligated to return to open session to vote to execute the contract extension. Therefore, we find 
that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law.  
VI. We Decline to Find the Committee’s Violations to be Intentional. However, We Order 
the Committee to Attend Open Meeting Law Training. 
 
 We must determine whether the vi olations were, as the complainant urges, intentional. 
See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c). An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body or a 
member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].” 940 CMR 29.02. An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
Law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open 
Meeting Law. Id. Here, notwithstanding a lengthy pattern of committing the same types of Open 
Meeting Law violations, particularly with regard to meeting minutes, we find no specific 
evidence that the Committee intentionally violated the Law or acted with deliberate ignorance of 
the Law’s requirements. Furthermore, we had not previously advised the Committee of its 
obligations under the Law regarding these particular violations. Therefore, we decline to find an 
intentional violation. 
 However, we are gravely concerne d that the Committee neglected its obligations to create 
and then review and release sufficiently detailed executive session minutes for almost five years. 
Additionally, the Committee’s Open Meeting Law violations span a variety of different 
requirements of the Law. For these reasons, we order all members of the Committee to attend 
comprehensive Open Meeting Law training offered by our office via one of our monthly live 
webinars. 
CONCLUSION 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to timely approve numerous executive session meeting minutes; failing to periodically
