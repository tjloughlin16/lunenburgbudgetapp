Dear Ms. Linares:
This office received two complaints from Kevin Wood, on March 22 and 28, 2019, 
alleging that the Georgetown School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaints were originally filed with the Committee on 
February 13 and 26, 2019, and the Committee responded by letter on March 28, 2019. In his 
complaints, Mr. Wood alleges that the School Committee regularly includes “executive session” 
as a topic on its agendas, even when no executive session is planned. The complaints also allege 
that the Committee improperly enters executive session without providing sufficient detail as to 
the purpose for entering the session and has improperly refused to release executive session 
minutes from an October 25, 2018, meeting. Finally, the complaints state that the Committee 
does not note the date and time of posting on meeting notices.
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
entering executive session during its October 25, 2018, meeting without a permissible purpose. 
However, we decline to review the allegations regarding the insufficiency of the executive 
session notice and procedures for entering executive session on October 25, 2018, because those 
allegations are untimely. Finally, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law, as its website specified the time and date meeting notices were posted.
In reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaints, the Committee’s 
response and the request for further review. We also reviewed Committee meeting notices for 
meetings held on October 25, 2018, and February 26 and March 12, 2019, that were included in 
the complaints and request for further review.1 We also spoke with Mr. Wood by phone on June

FACTS
We find the facts as follows. The Committee posts its meeting notices on the Town’s 
website, which displays a date and time stamp next to the link to the agenda. The notice for the 
Committee’s October 25, 2018, meeting contained an item: “Executive Session Chapter 30A §
21.” At a meeting on October 25, 2018, the Committee voted in open session to enter executive 
session “for the purpose of litigation.” The Chair did not identify the litigation matter to be 
discussed, nor did the Chair state that having the discussion in open session would have a 
detrimental effect on the Committee’s litigating position.
On February 5, 2019, Mr. Wood requested copies of executive session minutes “from 
June 14, 2018 to the present” along with other information related to executive sessions held by 
the Committee. The Chair replied by email on the same day saying she was happy to discuss Mr. 
Wood’s concerns and provide him with the requested materials.
In a letter on behalf of the Committee on March 28, 2019, the Chair explained that the 
Committee would not be releasing the October 25, 2018, executive session minutes because “the 
matter discussed may yet result in litigation.” The letter went on to state that: “the Committee 
typically contains an otherwise unspecified executive session item on each agenda so that it has 
the possibility of entering into executive session should, as the case occurred for the October 25, 
2018 meeting, an issue arise within 48 hours of the meeting that could not have been reasonably 
anticipated.” We understand from the Chair that the Committee continues to review its executive 
session minutes and has decided not to release the October 25, 2018, executive session minutes.
DISCUSSION
I. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by entering executive session during its
October 25, 2018, meeting without a permissible purpose.
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an 
open session, with some exceptions. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). One permissible reason to 
convene in executive session is “to discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining or 
litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating 
position of the public body and the chair so declares.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).3 
This purpose offers the narrow opportunity to discuss strategy with respect to litigation that is 
pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise demonstrably likely; the mere 
possibility of litigation is not sufficient to invoke Purpose 3. See Doherty v. School Committee

Here, we find that the Committee’s discussion does not fit within Purpose 3 for two 
reasons. First, there does not appear to be any “clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise 
demonstrably likely” litigation. In the October 25, 2018, executive session, the Committee 
received an update from the Superintendent about the noticeable buckling in the floor of the Pen 
Brook gym floor. We decline to read Purpose 3 so broadly as to include a public body’s 
discussion of a matter pending before it anytime it faces the possibility of litigation as a result of 
its decision on that matter. See OML 2019-12; OML 2015-10. Second, even if litigation had 
been pending, the Committee did not discuss litigation strategy during its executive session. The 
Committee’s seven-minute conversation actually concerned the process for filing a claim for 
insurance to pay for the needed repairs. Accordingly, we find that the Committee violated the 
Open Meeting Law by convening in executive session under Purpose 3 for this discussion.
II. We decline to review the allegations regarding the sufficiency of the executive session
notice and procedures for entering executive session on October 25, 2018, because those
allegations were untimely.
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public 
body within 30 days of the alleged violation. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b). If the alleged violation would 
not reasonably have been known at the time it occurred, then the complaint must be filed within 
30 days of the date it should reasonably have been discovered. 940 CMR 29.05(3). When an 
alleged violation occurs during an open session, the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable 
at the time it occurs. See OML Declination 7-3-2018 (Truro Select Board); OML 2017-41, Fn. 2. 
Here, any deficiencies in the notice for the October 25, 2018, meeting or in the Chair’s open 
session statement of the purpose for executive session could have been discovered right away. 
Since the first complaint was not filed with the Committee until February 13, 2019, we find these 
two allegations to be untimely and therefore decline to review them.
Although we do not review the specific allegations concerning the notice or the 
procedures for convening in executive session for the October 25, 2018, meeting that are alleged 
in the complaints, we note that the Committee appears to include a recurring general executive 
session agenda item on each meeting notice. For example, the Committee had a generic 
executive session item as a topic on the notice for the March 12, 2019, meeting. If the Committee 
had met in executive session, the notice would have been inadequate. However, since the 
Committee never met in executive session at the March 12, 2019, meeting, we find no violation 
of the Open Meeting Law in that instance. See OML 2017-146; OML 2015-21. We take this 
opportunity to offer guidance about recurring agenda items to the Committee.
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meeting notices contain the date, time, and place 
of the meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed. G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(b). For an executive session topic, the notice must state the purpose for the 
executive session, stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose 
for which the executive session was called. This typically means including which of the ten 
purposes the executive session is called for. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 
2019-31; OML 2017-53; OML 2016-43; OML 2013-82; OML 2012-89; OML 2011-54. “A 
precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary under the open

meeting law because that is the only notification given to the public that the [public body] would 
conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the reason for doing so 
was proper or improper.” See District Attorney for N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. Of Wavland, 455 
Mass. 561,567 (2009).
Here, the Committee’s meeting notices contain a recurring topic entitled: “Executive 
Session Chapter 30A §21.” A general, standing executive session topic is not sufficiently 
specific to satisfy the requirements of the Open Meeting Law, because it does not provide any 
detail about what type of discussion will be had or what executive session purpose the session 
will be conducted under. See OML 2013-164. Therefore, contrary to the Chair’s explanation that 
including an unspecified executive session item on each agenda allows the Committee to enter 
into executive session as the need arises, the Committee’s meeting notices actually accomplish 
the opposite: they will never be sufficiently detailed and therefore do not enable the Committee 
to properly convene in executive session.
Finally, we address the Committee’s concerns about unanticipated matters that may arise 
less than 48 hours before a meeting. A public body may discuss a truly unanticipated topic at a 
meeting, although the Attorney General strongly recommends that public bodies, as a matter of 
best practice, table discussion of topics that might be of significant interest to the public in order 
to include such topics in a meeting notice for a subsequent meeting. See OML 2016-54; OML 
2018-119. Including an insufficiently detailed topic on a meeting notice, however, does not 
increase the Committee’s ability to discuss the topic, and for the reasons stated above, the 
generic item will typically fail to meet the specificity standards of the Open Meeting Law.
III. The Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law by noting the time and date its
notices were posted on the website next to those notices.
A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not 
including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). The date and time that 
the notice is posted must be conspicuously recorded on or with the notice itself. 940 CMR 
29.03(l)(d); OML 2018-53. Here, since the Committee included date and time stamps on the 
website next to each agenda documenting when it was posted, we do not find a violation of the 
Open Meeting Law. We do not require that the notice itself include the information, if it is 
otherwise posted on the website.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by convening in executive session under Purpose 3 at its October 25, 2018, meeting. We order 
the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution the 
Committee that a determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law. We also order the Committee to 
publicly release its October 25, 2018, executive session minutes within 30 days of the receipt of 
this determination.
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any 
questions.
