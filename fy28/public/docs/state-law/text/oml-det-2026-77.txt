This office received a complaint from Amanda Alix on December 2, 2025,
1 alleging that 
the Shutesbury Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about October 7, and you responded 
on behalf of the Board by letter dated October 28. The complaint alleges that the Board failed to 
list an executive session topic with sufficient specificity on the notice of a meeting held on 
September 15. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way 
alleged. In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaint, the 
Board’s response, the complainant’s request for review, notice of the September 15 meeting, and 
the minutes of the September 15 executive session.
2 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts to be as follows. On September 9, the Board posted notice of a meeting 
to be held on September 15. The notice included the following topic to be discussed in executive 
session: “VI. Enter Executive Session for Reason 3. To discuss strategy with respect to collective 
bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or 
litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares. Will not be returning to public

session.” The meeting notice included no further information regarding the executive session. 
The meeting took place as scheduled. The Board voted to enter executive session under “Reason 
3” but did not identify the collective bargaining unit or litigation to be discussed. During the 
executive session, the Board discussed settlement of potential claims involving former Police 
Chief Kristen Burgess. Town Counsel updated the Board regarding settlement discussions. After 
further discussion, the Board voted to approve the settlement. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
 The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). Except when meeting in executive session, “all meetings  
of a public body shall be open to the public.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a). A public body may enter an  
executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 21. One such purpose is “[t]o discuss strategy with respect to collective  
bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or  
litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) 
(“Purpose 3”). With respect to litigation, this purpose offers the narrow opportunity to discuss 
strategy concerning litigation that is pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise 
demonstrably likely; the mere possibility of litigation is not sufficient to invoke Purpose 3. See 
OML 2026-51; OML 2024-144; OML 2021-75. 
 
Executive session topics must be described, both in the meeting notice and in the  
announcement during open session, in as much detail as possible without compromising the  
purpose for which the executive session was called. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); OML 2025-
185; see also Dist. Att’y. for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) 
(“A precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary under the 
open meeting law because that is the only notification given to the public that the [public body] 
would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the reason for 
doing so was proper or improper.”). Thus, when convening in executive session under Purpose 3 
to discuss litigation strategy, both the meeting notice and the announcement before entering the 
executive session must identify the litigation matter to be discussed, if doing so will not 
compromise the executive session purpose. See OML 2025-185; OML 2024-111; see also Bd. of 
Selectmen of Hull v. Healey, 2017 WL 6601467 at *7 (Mass. Super. Ct. December 14, 2017) 
(“The OML does not prevent a public body from withholding from public disclosure the name of 
a litigant if the facts warrant it doing so to preserve the purpose of the executive session in light 
of adverse impacts that would otherwise result from such disclosure.”). The purpose of executive 
session “Purpose 3” is to protect the public body’s litigating position. OML 2025-185. While 
deference is generally given to the public body’s assessment of whether identifying the litigation 
matter to be discussed would compromise the purpose for the executive session, a public body 
must be able to demonstrate a reasonable basis for that claim if challenged. Bd. of Selectmen of 
Hull at *2, 7 (finding no violation where disclosure could result in more litigants coming forward 
and other adverse impacts on the Town); OML 2024-195; OML 2016-12. 
 
 Here, the meeting notice referenced both collective bargaining and litigation as 
justification for entering executive session. The meeting notice did not include any additional

information to inform the public that the Board intended to convene in executive session to 
discuss litigation, rather than to discuss collective bargaining. Even if we assume, arguendo, that 
the Board was justified in not identifying the specific litigation matter to be discussed, the notice 
still did not precisely identify the reason for convening in executive session. See OML 2025-43, 
n.7; see also Dist. Att’y for the N. Dist., 455 Mass. At 567. Thus, the meeting notice was at least 
insufficient in this way. 
 
In its response, the Board maintains, without elaboration, that “the name of the party was 
not included as it was related to a personnel matter, and the Board did not want to violate that 
employee’s right to privacy.” Assuming that the executive session discussion concerned 
litigation that was pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise demonstrably 
likely, the notice should have identified that litigation unless doing so would compromise the 
purpose of the executive session. A blanket policy of not identifying threatened litigation matters 
does not pass muster. See OML 2025-185. While there are certain circumstances where a public 
body may, out of concern for the privacy of the complainant, withhold the complainant’s 
identity, the public body should still identify the litigation in some way. See id.; OML 2024-168 
(clarifying that with respect to complaints before the Massachusetts Commission Against 
Discrimination, a public body may, out of concern for the privacy of the complainant, withhold 
the complainant’s identity while still identifying the type of litigation matter to be discussed). It 
is our understanding that the matter here did not relate to a complaint before the Massachusetts 
Commission Against Discrimination or a similar body. Furthermore, the board does not argue 
that violation of an employee’s right to privacy might compromise its litigating position, 
jeopardizing the purpose for the executive session. To the extent that the Board had concerns 
about privacy specific to this topic, the notice still could have identified the litigation while 
withholding the name of the individual involved in the litigation, if appropriate. See OML 2025-
185; OML 2024-168; OML 2016-152.3 Thus, we find that the Board failed to describe the 
executive session topic in as much detail as possible when it failed to identify the litigation 
matter to be discussed, and failed to demonstrate a reasonable basis for withholding 
identification of the litigation matter. See Dist. Att’y. for the N. Dist., 455 Mass. at 567; see also 
OML 2025-185; OML 2024-111. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it described an executive session topic on the notice of the September 15 meeting with 
insufficient specificity. We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the Open 
Meeting Law, and we caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent 
to violate the law.
