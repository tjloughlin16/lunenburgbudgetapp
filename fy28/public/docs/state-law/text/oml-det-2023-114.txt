 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Riley: 
 
This office received a complaint from Tarsha Veiga on April 19, 2023, alleging that the 
Freetown Planning Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.1  
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about March 30, and you responded to 
the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated April 19.  The complaint alleges that the 
Board i) posted an insufficiently specific notice for a meeting held on March 21 and ii) failed to 
post the documents that corresponded with a topic that was listed on the March 21 meeting 
notice.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed with our office requesting further review, 
and the notice and minutes of the Board meeting held on March 21.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice 48 hours in advance of a 
meeting and include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the 
chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public 
bodies are required to list topics in a meeting notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably 
advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We 
generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could 
read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML

2018-7; OML 2015-35.2  A public body need not list each and every detail about a topic in order 
to comply with the Law’s requirements.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134.   
 
Here, the Board duly posted notice of a meeting to be held on March 21.  The notice 
included six topics, one of which was “Continued Site Plan Review and Special Permit – 52 
County Road.”  The notice included the name of the owner/applicant and representative, and the 
“scope of work” at the property, which was “Razing kiosk to construct a 1300 sq ft convenience 
store to be operated in association with the existing gas station.”  During the meeting, the Board 
heard from the owner of 52 Country Road and the engineer in charge of the project.  The Board 
asked about parking, traffic, number of employees and hours of operation, and then allowed the 
public to participate.  The Board then considered waivers submitted by the engineer the day 
before the meeting requesting that certain information need not be submitted with the site plan 
for 52 Country Road.  The Board unanimously approved the site plan and granted the requested 
waivers. 
 
The Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to list all tangential topics related 
to a discussion nor does the law require a public body to anticipate the course of deliberation or 
the outcome of a discussion, only that it identify the discussion topic with sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2015-73; OML 2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38.  
We are satisfied that the topic as listed for the site plan at 52 County Road was sufficient to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); see also OML 2013-152.  Moreover, we find that the discussions about the waivers for the 
site plan naturally flowed from the Board’s consideration of the posted agenda topic and 
therefore the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law by not separately listing this as a topic 
on the notice.  See OML 2020-99.  Nonetheless, even if the waivers did not naturally flow from 
the Board’s consideration of the posted topic, the chair could not have reasonably anticipated the 
discussion about the waivers for the site plan 48 hours prior to the Board’s March 21 meeting 
because the request had only been submitted the day before the meeting.  See OML 2017-2; 
OML 2013-87.  A public body may discuss a topic not listed on a meeting notice if the topic was 
not anticipated by the chair 48 hours in advance of the meeting.  See OML 2017-2; OML 2013-
87.    
 
With respect to the allegation raised in the complaint that the Board failed to post the site 
plan review, special permit and waiver requests related to 52 Country Road, the Board was not 
required to include attachments pertaining to topics listed on the notice.  The Open Meeting Law 
does not require that a public body attach supporting documents to a meeting notice or that it 
publicly post a “meeting packet” that contains such documents.  See OML 2022-24; OML 2022-
19; OML 2021-181.  To the contrary, the meeting notice itself must contain all information that 
the Open Meeting Law requires; posting attachments does not cure an otherwise defective notice.  
See OML 2021-138; OML 2021-57; OML 2019-22.  Moreover, there is no requirement in the 
Open Meeting Law that the public body affirmatively provide copies of documents to persons in 
attendance at a meeting.  But see G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c) (minutes of an open session, if they exist 
and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person

within 10 days); G.L. c. 66, § 10 (Public Records Law requires Board to permit inspection or 
provide copy of any public record, such as documents used during a meeting, within 10 business 
days of request).  We encourage public bodies, whenever feasible, to provide meeting attendees 
with copies of all documents it intends to discuss.  Particularly where viewing such documents 
would be helpful to following the conversation during the meeting, providing copies of 
documents that the Board intends to discuss promotes transparency and public access to 
government deliberations.  See OML 2016-94. 
 
Finally, the complaint suggests that the Board did not allow public participation during 
the March 21 meeting.  The Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body allow public 
participation in its meetings, but rather provides that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a 
public body without permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be 
silent.”  G.L. c. 30A § 20(f); see also OML 2021-27; OML 2020-161; OML 2017-189; OML 
2014-23; OML 2012-78.  We do, however, encourage public bodies to allow for as much public 
participation as time and circumstances permit.  See OML 2015-12; OML 2014-129; OML 
2012-59.  Here, the Board allowed public comment after discussing the site plan and heard from 
the complainant.  However, the Board did not allow another opportunity for public comment 
after it discussed the waivers for the 52 Country Road site plan.  Because it is within the Chair’s 
discretion to limit public participation during a meeting, we find no violation of the Open 
Meeting Law when the Chair did not allow for further public participation during the March 21 
meeting.  See OML 2020-114; OML 2017-189; OML 2014-23; OML 2012-78.  Moreover, it is  
not a violation of the Open Meeting Law for a public body to vote on a topic without first taking 
comments from the public.  See OML 2019-2. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
