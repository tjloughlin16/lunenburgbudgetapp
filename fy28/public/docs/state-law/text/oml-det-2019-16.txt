FACTS
We find the facts as follows. In February 2018, the Town of Hampden filed suit 
against the Hampden-Wilbraham Regional School District (the “District”) concerning, in part, 
the closure of a middle school in the District. On July 27, 2018, the parties entered into a 
settlement agreement and the court dismissed the case several days later. Pursuant to the 
agreement, if either town’s residents rejected the proposal at Town Meeting, the parties had 
the option of moving the court to vacate the judgment of dismissal within 30 days. On 
October 15, 2018, the Town of Wilbraham held its Town Meeting and rejected the proposed 
amendment to the regional school agreement. On October 29, 2018, the Town of Hampden 
held its Town Meeting and also rejected the agreement. On November 26, 2018, counsel 
hired by the Town specifically for this lawsuit filed a Motion to Vacate the settlement 
agreement, which was allowed and placed the lawsuit back on the Superior Court’s docket.
Following the initial dismissal of the suit, the Board convened several executive 
sessions to discuss the matter, including on October 22, October 29, and November 5, 2018. 
The Board’s October 22, 2018 meeting notice listed the topic as, “6:00 pm EXECUTIVE 
SESSION: Attorney Ed Pikula re: School Issue.” The October 29, 2018 meeting notice listed 
the topic as, “6:00 PM EXECUTIVE SESSION: Attorney Ed Pikula re: School Discussion, 
for the purpose of discussing litigation with the HWRSD School Committee as an open 
meeting may have a detrimental effect on the public body.” The Board’s November 5, 2018 
meeting notice listed the topic as, “6:00 pm Executive Session: Attorney Ed Pikula.” In light 
of the fact that the Town of Wilbraham had already rejected the agreement at its Town 
Meeting, the Board met in executive session on October 22 and October 29 to discuss its 
options in the event that the Town of Hampden also rejected the agreement at Town Meeting.3
DISCUSSION
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of each meeting and 
include in that notice a “listings of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed at the meeting.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Public bodies must list topics for discussion 
with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). Executive session topics must be described, both in the 
meeting notice and in an announcement during open session, in as much detail as possible 
without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called. See District 
Attorney for N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm, of Wayland. 455 Mass. 561, 567 (“[a] precise statement 
of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary under the open meeting law 
because that is the only notification given to the public that the school committee would 
conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the reason for doing

generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public 
could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.
See OML 2015-35.
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an 
open session, with some exceptions. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). One permissible reason to 
convene in executive session is “to discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining or 
litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating 
position of the public body and the chair so declares.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”). 
This purpose offers the narrow opportunity to discuss strategy with respect to litigation that is 
pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise demonstrably likely; the mere 
possibility of litigation is not sufficient to invoke Purpose 3. See Doherty v. School 
Committee of Boston. 386 Mass. 643, 648 (1982); Ferryman v. School Committee of Boston. 
17 Mass. App. Ct. 346, 352 (1983); OML 2012-05.4 When convening in executive session 
pursuant to Purpose 3, a public body should identify the litigation matter to be discussed, if 
doing so will not compromise the lawful purpose for secrecy. See OML 2016-12. While we 
generally defer to a public body’s assessment of whether the inclusion of such information 
would compromise the purpose for the executive session, a public body must be able to 
demonstrate a reasonable basis for such a claim if challenged. See OML 2015-14.
I. The Board Failed to Provide Sufficient Detail on its October 22 and 
November 5. 2018 Meeting Notices.
Mr. Davenport alleges that the executive session topics on the Board’s October 22 and 
November 5, 2018 meeting notices lacked sufficient detail. The Board contends that, by 
identifying on its notices the name of the special counsel who was hired specifically for the 
lawsuit with the school, it had intended to indicate to the public that the anticipated discussion 
concerned the lawsuit. The Board’s position assumes that the public already has some level 
of knowledge about the lawsuit, however. Public bodies should not assume that the public 
has a particular familiarity with its business, even for topics that are repeatedly discussed over 
a period of time. See e.g., OML 2017-197 (our office discourages the use of acronyms that 
are not widely understood by members of the public). Although we generally defer to a 
public body’s decision about whether to withhold detail from a meeting notice because such 
detail may compromise the purpose for the executive session, we disagree that naming the 
specific litigation matter here would have compromised the purpose for the executive session, 
because the case was pending in court and had been the subject of public discussion, 
including a press release the Board issued on October 23, 2018. See OML 2015-117; OML 
2012-118. We therefore find that the meeting notices lacked the requisite detail about the 
anticipated executive session topic because they should have included the case name and/or 
parties to the case, as the Board had properly done for its October 29, 2018 meeting notice. 
See OML 2013-97. Accordingly, the Board violated the Open Meeting Law.

We commend the Board for pledging to include more information on its meeting 
notices going forward. As a result of this violation, however, the Board may not rely on the 
Open Meeting Law to continue to withhold the executive sessions minutes of its October 22 
and November 5, 2018 meetings from public disclosure. The Board may, however, continue 
to withhold the minutes, in whole or in part, if an exemption to the Public Records Law or the 
attorney-client privilege applies. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).
II. The Board Did Not Violate the Open Meeting Law by Convening in
Executive Session on October 22 and October 29. 2018.
The complaints also allege that the Board’s October 22 and October 29, 2018 
executive sessions discussions were improper because the lawsuit had been dismissed and 
therefore there was no pending matter to discuss behind closed doors. The Board responds 
that the dismissal was not final because the settlement agreement was provisional and subject 
to an agreement to vacate the dismissal if the Town declined to approve the settlement at 
Town Meeting, which is precisely what occurred. Due to the provisional nature of the 
settlement agreement, we agree that the Board still had a litigating position to protect prior to 
the 30-day deadline set forth in the agreement.
Purpose 3 is not limited to discussions of litigation strategy for cases pending in a 
court of law, but extends to strategy discussions for administrative proceedings or post­
judgment discussions about whether to file an appeal of a decision. See OML 2017-9; OML 
2012-116. Although the settlement agreement here had been executed and the judgment of 
dismissal entered by the court, resolution of the lawsuit would not be finalized until residents 
either approved or rejected it at Town Meeting. Accordingly, we conclude that the litigation 
remained threatened at the time of the executive sessions, which were held immediately prior 
to Town Meeting. Even if certain aspects of the Board’s litigating position had been publicly 
disclosed at that time, as the complainants contend, the Board was still permitted to discuss its 
strategy in executive session if holding an open meeting could have had a detrimental effect 
on its litigating position. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3); OML 2012-116; see also OML 2017-9 
(discussion of a Board’s litigative options and whether to appeal a court decision were 
appropriately-held under Purpose 3). Accordingly, we find that the Board’s October 22 and 
October 29, 2018 executive sessions were properly held under Purpose 3.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to include sufficient detail on its October 22 and November 5, 2018 meeting 
notices. We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, 
and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate 
the law. Additionally, we order the Board to publicly release its October 22 and November 5, 
2018 executive session meeting minutes in accordance with the above guidance.
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or
