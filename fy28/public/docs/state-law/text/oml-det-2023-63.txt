This office received a complaint from Betty King on August 8, 2022, alleging that the 
Asian American and Pacific Islanders Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Commission 
on or about July 5, 2022, and you
1 responded, on behalf of the Commission, by letter dated 
August 2, 2022.2  The complaint alleges that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely post notice of a meeting to be held on June 6, 2022, and by posting an 
insufficiently specific notice with respect to an executive session topic for the June 6, 2022, 
meeting. 
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter.  We resolve this 
matter

original complaint, the Commission’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed with our 
office requesting further review, and the notices and minutes of Commission meetings held on 
June 6, 2022, and July 19, 2022.  Following our review, we find that the notice for the June 6,

On June 28, 2022, the complainant, who is a member of the Commission, checked the 
cale
ndar page on the Commission’s website for the month of June and did not see a notice posted 
for the June 6, 2022, meeting.  The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a 
public body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding 
Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  For state public bodies, notice 
shall be posted on a website and a copy of each notice must be sent to the Secretary of the 
Commonwealth’s Regulations Division.  See 940 CMR 29.03(6).  The Commission states that 
notice for the June 6, 2022, meeting was timely posted to the website.  In fact, on May 31, 2022, 
the Executive Director of the Commission forwarded a copy of the notice to the Regulations 
Division within the Secretary of the Commonwealth’s Office via email.  Although a public body 
is required to post notice 48 hours prior to a meeting, the Open Meeting Law does not require 
that the notice remain posted to the website after the meeting has occurred.  
 
We note that a notice is currently posted on the Commission’s calendar page for a June 6, 
2022, meet
ing.  Furthermore, the complainant asserts that no notice for the June 6, 2022, meeting 
was posted when she checked on June 28, 2022, but the complainant does not assert that she had 
checked and found no notice in the days prior to the June 6, 2022, meeting.  Therefore, we find 
no evidence that the Commission failed to timely post notice for its June 6, 2022, meeting.  We 
do, however, find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to record the 
date and time that the notice was posted.  The Open Meeting Law requires that “the date and 
time that the notice is posted shall be conspicuously recorded thereon or therewith.”  940 CMR 
29.03(1)(d); see OML 2011-45.3  The date and time of posting may be listed either on the 
meeting notice itself or on the website next to the link to the meeting notice.  See OML 2019-
102; OML 2018-53.  Recording the date and time enables the public to observe that public 
bodies are complying with the Open Meeting Law’s notice requirements and further provides 
this office with evidence of compliance with the law.  See OML 2013-44.  As evidenced here, 
the failure to record the date and time of posting impeded our and the public’s ability to 
determine whether the notice was timely posted and underscores the importance of this 
requirement.   
  
We also find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law where the June 6, 
2022, meet
ing notice lacked sufficient specificity with respect to the executive session topic.  
The Open Meeting Law requires that notices include a listing of topics that the chair reasonably 
anticipates will be discussed.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The list of topics shall have “sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 
CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable 
member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public 
body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35; OML 2013-39.  To convene in executive session, a 
public body must identify in the notice the executive session purpose it intends to invoke to 
justify the executive session and provide as much detail as possible to inform the public of the

nature of the executive session discussion without compromising the confidentiality of the 
exec
utive session.  See OML 2016-21; see also District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. 
of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for convening in 
executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification given the public that a 
[public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the 
reason for doing so was proper or improper”).   
 
Here, the notice of the Commission meeting held on June 6, 2022, simply listed 
“Exe
cutive Session” and did not identify or describe the executive session purpose nor the topic 
to be discussed.  While a public body is not required to provide the specific statutory citation of 
the executive session it plans to convene under, the public body must at least provide a sufficient 
description of the executive session purpose.  See OML 2016-95.  We find that the executive 
session topic listed on the notice for the June 6, 2022, meeting did not offer the public any 
understanding of the anticipated executive session discussion.  See OML 2018-80; OML 2014-
43. 
 
Although not specifically raised in the complaint, we note that it does not appear that the 
disc
ussions in executive session were proper.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, 
session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has 
first convened in an open session, that a majority of members of the body have voted to go into 
executive session, that the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and entered into the 
minutes, and the chair has publicly announced whether the open session will reconvene at the 
conclusion of the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.  The 
Commission convened in open session on June 6, 2022, “Commissioner Megha Prasad 
motion[ed] to enter executive session,” and the Commission unanimously voted by roll call to 
convene in executive session.  However, the minutes of the June 6, 2022, executive session 
meeting, which have been publicly released, state that “Executive Director Forbes review[ed] the 
proposal for how to use the remaining FY22 funding.”  “Commissioners discuss[ed] proposed 
bonuses for current staff” and then unanimously voted to “approve the proposed budget.”  A 
public body may convene in executive session to “conduct strategy sessions in preparation for 
negotiations with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective bargaining sessions or contract 
negotiations with nonunion personnel.”  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a)(2) (“Purpose 2”).  While a public 
body may discuss the contract of specific non-union personnel or strategy in preparation for such 
contract negotiations in executive session, general budgetary discussions are not appropriate 
under executive session Purpose 2.  See OML 2015-63; OML 2014-30; OML 2013-91.  We 
remind the Commission of its obligations under the law. 
 
For t
he reasons stated above, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law by providing insufficient specificity in the notice for the June 6, 2022, meeting with respect 
to the executive session topic to be discussed and by failing to record the date and time that the 
notice was posted.  We order immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and 
caution that future similar violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
