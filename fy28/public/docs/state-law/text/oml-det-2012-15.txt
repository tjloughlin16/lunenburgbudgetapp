FACTS 
According to our conversation with Mr. O'Keefe I, the Committee was created during the May 
23, 2011 Town Meeting. The Committee was assigned the task of determining whether the former court 
house is a viable location for a new police station, and assessing what would be necessary to turn the 
court house into a police station. In addition, the Committee was asked to find an alternative location if 
the court house was not a viable option. On September 2, 2011, the Committee consisted of seven 
members. The members included Mr. O'Keefe; Mr. Zbikowski; Martin Rose; Guy Corbosierio; David 
Walsh; David Johnson; and Robert Guenther. 
On September 2, 2011, Mr. Zbikowski sent a letter to Mr. O'Keefe, expressing his concerns about 
matters under consideration by the Committee at that time. Mr. Zbikowski also copied the other five 
Committee members on this letter. In the letter, Mr. Zbikowski expressed his displeasure with 
discussions that took place at a previous Committee meeting. The letter also questioned the legality of 
purchasing the court house and discussed other options that may be viable for a new police station. It 
appears that no member responded to Mr. Zbikowski's letter. In a phone conversation with our office, 
Mr. O'Keefe indicated that Mr. Zbikowski's letter was included in the Committee's September 29, 2011 
minutes as a document used by the Committee. The Committee's October 7, 2011 response to the 
complaint indicated that "[c]opies have already been distributed to the local media" 2  and that "[t]hese 
actions should satisfy that the public is now aware of the contents of the letter and proper action was 
taken by the public body." 
DISCUSSION 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted "to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding deliberations 
and decisions on which public policy is based." Ghiglione v. School Committee of Southbridge, 376 
Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Open Meeting Law therefore requires that "[e]xcept as provided in [G.L. c. 
30A, § 21(a)], all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public." G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a). Meeting 
is defined, in part, as "a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body's 
jurisdiction." G.L. c. 30A, § 18. Deliberation is defined, in relevant part, as "an oral or written 
communication through any medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public 
body on any public business within its jurisdiction." Id. 
Mr. Zbikowski's September 2, 2011 letter constituted deliberation outside of an open meeting, in 
violation of the Open Meeting Law. The letter was sent to a quorum of the Committee's members and 
discussed matters within the Committee's jurisdiction — namely the viability of the court house as a police 
station and other options that the Committee should explore. In previous determinations, our office has 
found that any written communication sent by an individual member of a public body to a quorum of the 
public body concerning matters within that body's jurisdiction is a deliberation, even where no other 
member replied. See OML 2011-35; OML 2011-37; and OML 2012-8. Because there was no response 
from any Committee member, however, we do not find that the Committee as a whole violated the Open 
Meeting Law. 
In his response to our office, Mr. Zbikowski stated, "The chairman of a committee that I serve

the Open Meeting Law. When I reread the definition of deliberation, I realized that I may have 
inadvertently done so." Mr. Zbikowski further stated that, at the next meeting, he submitted the letter he 
wrote to make it part of the record and acknowledged the potential violation. The Committee's
