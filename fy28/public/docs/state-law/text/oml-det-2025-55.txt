RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Castro:  
 
This office received a complaint from Jacquelyn Wehtje on November 22, 2024, alleging 
that the Fitchburg Agricultural Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Commission on 
November 5, 2024, and you responded, on behalf of the Commission, by letter dated November 
26, 2024.  The complaint alleges that the Commission continues to withhold minutes of an 
executive session held on April 4, 2023, past the time that publication would defeat the lawful 
purpose for the executive session. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Commission did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law in the way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Commission’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting 
further review.  In addition, we received the notice and open and executive session minutes of 
the Commission meeting held on April 4, 2023. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberation and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The law requires that all meetings of a public body be 
conducted in an open session, with some exceptions.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  A public 
body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the 
Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that a majority of 
members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each member is 
===PAGE 2===
recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly announced whether 
the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.1   
 
One permissible reason to convene in executive session is “to consider the purchase, 
exchange, lease or value of real property if the chair declares that an open meeting may have a 
detrimental effect on the negotiating position of the public body.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6) 
(“Purpose 6”).  Purpose 6, narrowly construed, permits a public body to meet in executive 
session to protect its negotiating position when seeking to purchase, exchange, or lease real 
estate, or to discuss the value of real estate where it has a negotiating position relative to that 
property.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6); Allen v. Committee of Selectmen of Belmont, 58 Mass. 
App. Ct. 715, 719-721 (2003); OML 2019-143; OML 2016-50.  Purpose 6 is intended to 
preserve confidentiality in negotiating the value of the property to be purchased, exchanged or 
leased to avoid putting the public body at a disadvantage in its negotiations for the property.  See 
District Atty. for the Plymouth Dist. v. Selectmen of Middleborough, 395 Mass. 629, 631 (1985); 
OML 2016-50.  
 
The Board has not publicly released the minutes of the April 4, 2023, executive session; 
therefore, we do not recount their content in detail here.  However, based on our in camera 
review of the minutes, during the executive session meeting held on April 4, 2023, the 
Commission discussed the potential purchase of farmland owned by a private entity and thus the 
discussion was appropriate for executive session under Purpose 6.  See OML 2023-81; 2022-
119.  On October 15, 2024, the complainant emailed the Commission requesting the executive 
session minutes of the April 4, 2023, meeting.  On October 24, 2024, Commission Chair Evan 
Halstead responded and explained that the Commission would continue to withhold the minutes 
as the purpose for the executive session was “still active.”   
 
The complaint alleges that the executive session purpose underlying the April 4, 2023, 
meeting has expired because the land “deal has fallen through” and therefore the minutes should 
be released.  The Commission contends that “the underlying real estate transaction has yet to be 
resolved” and release of “the minutes would defeat the lawful purpose of the executive session.”  
Executive session minutes may be withheld from disclosure to the public in their entirety “as 
long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer.”  G.L. 
c. 30A, § 22(f).  When the purpose for a valid executive session has been served, the minutes and 
any documents or exhibits used at the session must be disclosed, unless the attorney-client 
privilege or an exemption to the Public Records Law applies.  Id.  The burden of justifying 
continued nondisclosure of executive session minutes lies with the public body.  Foudy v. 
Amherst-Pelham Regional School Committee, 402 Mass. 179, 184 (1988).   
 
Our role is not to supplant the Commission’s judgment that disclosure of the minutes 
would defeat the lawful purpose of the executive sessions but rather to determine whether the 
Commission has a reasonable basis for continuing to withhold the executive session minutes.  
We find that the Commission has made a sufficient showing to justify its continued 
nondisclosure of the minutes used during the April 4, 2023, executive session.  Nonetheless, we

CONCLUSION 
 
We find that the Commission did not violate the Open Meeting Law by withholding 
executive session minutes.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to 
be resolved.  This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with 
our office or the Commission.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you 
have any questions regarding this letter.
