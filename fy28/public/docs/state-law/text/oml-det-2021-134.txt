This office received a complaint from Jeremy Brown on April 5, 2021, alleging that the 
Somerville School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on February 25 and the 
Committee’s Chair responded on behalf of the Committee by letter dated March 12.
1 In his 
complaint, Mr. Brown alleges that the Committee 1) failed to timely approve meeting minutes of 
all meetings after July 27, 2020, and 2) failed to respond to a request for meeting minutes within 
10 calendar days.

We resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
and find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law in both respects. In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Committee’s response to the complaint, 
and the complainant’s request for further review. We also reviewed the Committee’s website and 
the meeting minutes of the Committee’s February 22 and March 8 meetings.  
1. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely approve meeting 
minutes. 
 The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting minutes must be reviewed 
and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). “Timely manner” means “within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can 
show good cause for further delay.” See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.4 Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting. See OML 2018-152; 
OML 2012-91. 
 Here, Mr. Brown alleges that the Committee stopped approving meeting minutes after the 
approval of the July 27, 2020, meeting minutes until he filed his complaint on February 25. For 
example, the Committee did not approve meeting minutes for the August 31, 2020, September 
14, 2020, and September 21, 2020, meetings until March 8, 2021, despite having met at least ten 
times in the meantime. The Committee concedes that the Committee’s staff fell behind on 
drafting meeting minutes due to staffing changes and the increased frequency of Committee 
meetings during the fall of 2020. We understand the demands on a public body with limited 
staff; however, the public body is still obligated to timely approve minutes. See OML 2017-115. 
The responsibility for complying with the Open Meeting Law rests with public bodies and 
members of such bodies, not with individual public employees, such as administrative staff. 
See OML 2018-105. If staff resources are not available to create minutes for a public body to 
approve, then public body members must take on this responsibility to ensure compliance with 
the Law. See OML 2018-111. Accordingly, we find that the Committee violated the Open 
Meeting Law by failing to timely approve numerous sets of meeting minutes in a timely manner. 
2. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to a request for 
meeting minutes within 10 calendar days.  
 The Open Meeting Law requires that “[m]inutes of an open session, if they exist and 
whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 
days.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). Under the Open Meeting Law, a public body has 10 calendar days 
from the date a request is received to provide a response. 5 If minutes do not yet exist at the time

of a request, the public body is still required to respond to the request within 10 calendar days 
with an explanation of whether the minutes do or do not exist in either approved or draft form.  
 Here, Mr. Brown made his request for meeting minutes directly to all Committee 
members in a February 9 email, which was overlooked until Mr. Brown sent a follow-up email 
on February 19. On February 22, the Committee’s Chair sent Mr. Brown a letter with a 
preliminary response, indicating that all of the meeting minutes were not available at that time as 
many had not been drafted or approved. As the Committee did not send a response to Mr. Brown 
until 13 calendar days after he sent his initial request, we find that the Committee violated the 
Open Meeting Law.  
 Finally, we take this opportunity to note that a public body is only required to produce 
any meeting minutes, whether in draft or approved form, which exist at the time of a request. 
Handwritten notes, depending on the condition they are in, may be considered a draft of the 
minutes that need to be produced, and “materials used in the preparation of minutes” are public 
records. See OML 2018-117; OML 2013-131; OML 2013-64; OML 2013-31. However, a public 
body is not required to produce meeting minutes in response to a request that do not already 
exist. See OML 2020-151. 
CONCLUSION 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to timely approve meeting minutes and failing to respond to a request for meeting 
minutes within 10 calendar days. We order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance 
with the Open Meeting Law and caution the Committee that a determination by our office of a 
similar violation in the future may be considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting 
Law. As of August 18, the Committee’s legal counsel informs us that the Committee has created, 
approved and sent copies of all of the requested meeting minutes to Mr. Brown. Therefore, we 
order no further relief.  
 We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any 
questions.
