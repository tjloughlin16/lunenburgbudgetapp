 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Driscoll: 
 
This office received a complaint from Christine McCue Potts on January 16, 2025, 
alleging that the Medfield Housing Authority Board of Commissioners (the “Board”) violated 
the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the 
Board on November 21, 2024, and you responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated 
December 12, 2024.  The complaint alleges that the Board i) posted a notice with insufficient 
specificity for a meeting held on November 12, 2024; ii) created insufficient minutes of meetings 
held on October 26, 2023, and October 15, 2024, by failing to include a list of documents used 
during the meetings; and iii) failed to timely review the October 26, 2023, executive session 
meeting minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure was warranted.    
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated Open Meeting Law in the ways 
alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s 
response to the complaint, the complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the 
notice and minutes of the Board meetings held on October 26, 2023, October 15, 2024, and 
November 12, 2024. 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  During a meeting held on October 26, 2023, the Board first 
convened in open session, then moved to convene in executive session to discuss the terms of the

“Tilden Village MDA,” and finally unanimously voted by roll call to convene in executive

On November 7, 2024, at 3:40 P.M., the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on

The November 12, 2024, meeting was held as planned.  During the meeting, the Board 
unanimously approved the executive session minutes of the Board meeting held on October 26, 
2023, as well as the minutes of the Board meeting held on October 15, 2024.   
 
DISCUSSION 
 
I. The November 12, 2024, Meeting Notice Lacked Sufficient Specificity by 
Failing to Identify the Specific Minutes to be Approved.   
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the  
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of  
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a public 
body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding 
Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice must be printed in a 
legible, easily understandable format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as 
well as a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed.  Id.  The list of 
topics shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be 
sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand 
the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.2

The complaint alleges that the November 12, 2024, meeting notice was insufficient where 
it referred to minutes to be reviewed as “October 2023 Special Meeting.”  We decline to find that 
the notice was insufficient for identifying the minutes as from a “special” meeting without 
specifically referring to an executive session, but we do find a violation where the November 12, 
2024, notice did not identify the specific date of the meeting minutes to be approved.  We have 
explained in previous determinations that notices should generally identify the specific meeting 
minutes to be reviewed and approved.  See OML 2020-34; OML 2017-92; OML 2013-199.  The 
Board does not contend that it was unaware of the exact minutes to be approved during its 
November 12, 2024, meeting.  Compare OML 2015-25 (when the chair does not anticipate 
which minutes will be ready for review until the meeting, a generic topic may be sufficient).  As 
such, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to list on its notice the 
specific meeting minutes to be approved.   
 
II. The Board Created Minutes Which Failed to Include a List of Documents 
Used During its Meetings of October 26, 2023, and October 15, 2024.  
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies “create and maintain accurate minutes 
of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members 
present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other 
exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including 
the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); 940 CMR 29.11(1).  Although the Law does not 
define what it means for a document to be “used” during a meeting, we have said that a 
document is “used,” and must be listed in the minutes, when the document is physically present, 
verbally identified, and its contents discussed by the public body during a meeting.  See OML 
2024-217; OML 2017-43; OML 2014-12.  The failure to separately list documents used at a 
meeting constitutes a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2019-46.   
 
Here, the Board acknowledges, and we find, that the Board failed to include within the 
minutes of its meetings of October 26, 2023, and October 15, 2024, a list of documents used 
during those meetings.  That omission constituted a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  We 
order the Board to revise its minutes to include a list of the documents used during the October 
26, 2023, and October 15, 2024, meetings if it is not already done so.  
 
We must determine whether this violation was, as the complainant urges, an intentional 
one.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body 
or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting 
Law.  Id.  As of November 12, 2024, the date that the minutes were reviewed and approved , the 
Board had not yet been advised by the Attorney General of its obligation to list in the minutes the 
documents used during meetings.  We acknowledge that, on January 28, 2025, the Attorney 
General found that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by approving minutes which failed 
to include lists of documents used during meetings.  See OML 2025-6.  However, we do not rely 
on the January 2025 determination to find an intention violation because the Board had not yet 
been advised by the Attorney General of its obligation to list in the minutes the documents used

find that the violation here was intentional. 
 
III. The Board Violated the Open Meeting Law by Failing to Periodically 
Review Executive Session Minutes. 
 
The Open Meeting Law also states that “[m]inutes of all open sessions shall be created 
and approved in a timely manner,” while requiring that minutes of executive sessions be 
“review[ed]” “at reasonable intervals” to determine whether continued nondisclosure is 
warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c); G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); see also OML 2024-158; OML 2024-
29; Swansea Board of Selectmen v. Attorney General, Civil Action No. 2173CV00906 (Bristol 
Sup. Ct. December 27, 2023).  In other words, the Open Meeting Law does not require that a 
public body approve executive session minutes in a timely manner, but rather that the executive 
session minutes be “create[d] and maintain[ed]” and “review[ed]” “at reasonable intervals” to 
determine whether continued nondisclosure is warranted, and to announce that determination at 
the next meeting following the review.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(a), (g)(1).3  Although “reasonable 
intervals” is not defined by the law, our office has consistently found a quarterly review or a 
review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2022-233; OML 2019-45; OML 2017-
104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180.  The review of minutes may be conducted by the public 
body, or its chair or designee.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1).   
 
In addition, the Open Meeting Law requires a public body to respond to a request for 
executive session minutes within 10 days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  If the public body or a 
designee has determined, prior to the request, that certain executive session minutes may be 
released, it should respond to the requester directly, notifying him or her of the availability of 
those minutes.  See OML 2013-105; OML 2013-99.  And if, at the time of a request, the public 
body has not conducted a review of the minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure is 
warranted, the body must perform such a review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no later 
than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  In such 
circumstances, the body must still respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her 
that it is conducting this review.  See OML 2013-99.   
 
On October 25, 2024, the complainant requested from the Board the executive session 
minutes of the meeting held on October 26, 2023.  On November 4, 2024, within 10 days, the 
Board responded to the request explaining that minutes had not yet been reviewed to determine

whether continued non-disclosure is warranted.  During its next meeting on November 12, 2024, 
the Board reviewed and approved for release the October 26, 2023, executive session minutes.   
 
Although the Board timely reviewed the October 26, 2023, executive session minutes in 
response to the request as the Board reviewed the minutes at its next meeting, the Board 
acknowledged that “no procedure is in place for periodic review of Executive Session minutes.”  
As stated above, a public body is required to review, at reasonable intervals, executive session 
minutes to determine if the Open Meeting Law allows for continued non-disclosure, or if the 
lawful purpose for the executive session has been served such that the minutes must be released, 
and to announce that determination at the next meeting following its review.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(g)(1).  Here, the Board did not review the October 26, 2023, executive session minutes at all 
until it received a request for those minutes on October 25, 2024, a year later.  Therefore, we find 
that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to review the minutes at reasonable 
intervals.  We commend the Board for its commitment “to adopt a procedure under which it will 
review and approve executive session minutes twice annually” and order no further remedial 
action where the Board has now reviewed and released the requested executive session minutes.  
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting the November 12, 2024, meeting notice with insufficient specificity, by creating minutes 
which failed to include a list of documents used during the meetings, and by failing to 
periodically review executive session minutes.  We order the Board’s immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements and caution the Board that a future finding of an 
intentional violation of the Open Meeting Law may result in a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per 
violation.  In addition, we order the Board, within 30 days of the date of this letter, to revise the 
minutes of the October 26, 2023, and October 15, 2024, meetings to include a list of the 
documents used during those two meetings if it is not already done so.
