RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Corbo: 
This office received six complaints from Robert Belbin, dated April 29, 2013. The 
complaints allege that the Carver Zoning Board of Appeals (the "Board") violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. Mr. Belbin originally filed four of the complaints with the 
Board on March 14, 2013; one on March 29, 2013; and one on March 31, 2013. The complaints 
allege that, with regard to the Board's March 14, 2013 meeting, the Board (1) failed to provide 
sufficient detail in the notice regarding certain topics discussed at the meeting; (2) failed to 
announce that the meeting was being recorded; (3) prevented the complainant from participating 
in the meeting; and (4) approved minutes during that meeting that did not include a sufficiently 
detailed summary of the discussion that occurred. The Board responded to the complaints by 
letter dated April 23, 2013, following an extension granted by our office. 
In reaching a determination, we reviewed the four complaints filed with the Board on 
March 14, 2013; the complaint filed with the Board on March 29, 2013; the complaint filed with 
the Board on March 31, 2013; the Board's April 23, 2013 response; the April 29, 2013 
complaints filed with our office; and the notices for and minutes from the Board's December 13, 
2012; January 10, 2013; February 14, 2013; and March 14, 2013 meetings. Additionally, we 
reviewed an April 19, 2013 letter from Board Chair Stephen Gray and an April 19, 2013 letter 
from Board member James Nauen; a Carver Police Department incident report filed on March 
28, 2013; a transcript of Mr. Nauen's call to the police, released on March 19, 2013; Carver 
Police Officer David Harriman's narrative report; and a video of the Board's March 14, 2013 
meeting, provided by the complainant. Finally, we spoke with Board Attorney Gregg Corbo1 by 
telephone on August 2, 2013.

Following our review, we find that (1) the notice for the Board's March 14, 2013 meeting 
was sufficiently specific; (2) the complainant did not have the right to participate in the Board's 
March 14, 2013 meeting; and (3) the Board's December 12, 2012; January 10, 2013; and 
February 14, 2013 meeting minutes were sufficiently detailed. However, we find that the Chair 
violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to inform the attendees that the March 14, 2013 
meeting was being recorded. The violation was de minimis, though, as the complainant drew 
substantial attention to the fact that he was recording, and those in attendance were aware that he 
was doing so. 
FACTS 
On March 11, 2013, the Board posted notice for its March 14, 2013 meeting with the 
Town Clerk. The notice included the following information: 
Case No. 12-956 Petitioner: Andy Cimbron of 45 Provincetown View Road, Plymouth, 
Massachusetts regarding property located at 70 West Street, Carver, Massachusetts. The 
applicant seeks a Variance and/or Special Permit under Article II, Section 2320. The 
applicant is seeking to raze the existing single family dwelling and rebuild a new 36'x34' 
+\- single family dwelling with a 12'x20' garage attached at said address. The property is 
zoned Residential/Agricultural District; in accordance to Assessors Map 72, Lot 5-A. 
Before the Board commenced its March 14, 2013 meeting, the complainant entered the 
meeting room at the Carver Town Hall with his video camera recording. He announced to the 
Board members present that he had started recording. A Board member asked the complainant 
to wait for the Chair to arrive before recording, and the complainant replied that he would not. 
The complainant and the Board members argued about whether the complainant had a right to 
record before the meeting commenced, and Board member James Nauen left the room to call the 
police. About three minutes later, both the Chair Stephen Gray and Carver Police Officer David 
Harriman arrived. By that time, the applicant and his engineer had arrived, and they were the 
only other attendees present. The complainant announced to the room that he was video 
recording, and Officer Harriman requested that the complainant step outside with him into the 
hallway. Two additional police officers then arrived to speak with the complainant. When the 
complainant returned to the meeting room with Officer Harriman about three minutes later, the 
Chair announced that he did not want the complainant removed and no longer needed police 
presence at the meeting. The Chair did not announce to the public that the complainant was 
video recording the meeting. 
The Board proceeded with its meeting and discussed the permit application listed in the 
notice. During that discussion, a Board member commented about the potential applicability of 
Article II, section 2254 of Town of Carver Zoning Bylaws. The Board had discussed this bylaw 
at previous meetings, however the Board had not previously referred to the bylaw by its article 
and section numbers. The complainant attempted to address the Board, and was told by the 
Chair to wait until the Board had finished its discussion, at which point he would be allowed to 
comment. When the Board completed its discussion, the Chair recognized the complainant. The 
complainant noted that the bylaw was not included in the meeting notice, and stated that 
therefore the notice was insufficiently detailed.

The Board then voted unanimously to approve the minutes from its December 13, 2012; 
January 10, 2013; and February 14, 2013 meetings. The minutes from the January 10, 2013 
meeting indicate that during that meeting, the Board recessed for 10 minutes to review Ch. 40A 
of the Massachusetts General Laws for clarification. The Chair explains in his April 19, 2013 
letter to this Office that he wished to review the law at that time, but did not want to require the 
others in attendance to sit idly by. He writes that there was no communication between or 
among the Board members during the recess. 
DISCUSSION 
1. The Notice for the Board's March 14, 2013 Meeting Provided Sufficient Detail about 
the Topics to be Discussed. 
The complainant alleges that the notice for the Board's March 14, 2013 meeting failed to 
provide specific information about the relief requested by the permit applicant, and failed to 
include a bylaw that the Board discussed at the meeting. Public bodies must list anticipated 
topics in a meeting notice with "sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the 
issues to be discussed at the meeting." G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(1 )(b). We 
generally consider a topic to include sufficient specificity when a reasonable member of the 
public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body's discussion. 
See OML 2013-55; OML 2011-7.2 
Here, the Board's notice for its March 14, 2013 meeting included the name of the 
applicant, his home address, the address of the property on the application, the type of permit 
requested, the article and section of the zoning bylaws under which the permit was requested, a 
description of the property, what the applicant planned to do with the property, and the current 
zoning status of the property, including its location on the Assessors Map. A reasonable member 
of the public could have read this topic and understood what the nature of the public body's 
discussion would be. The Board was not required to include in its notice, and indeed may not 
have been able to anticipate, every aspect of the discussion that occurred. We therefore find that 
the posted topic was sufficiently specific. 
2. The Board did not Violate the Open Meeting Law by Restricting the Complainant's 
Remarks. 
The complainant alleges that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when the Chair 
denied his request to address the Board. The Open Meeting Law does not require that public 
bodies allow public participation. OML 2013-64. The Law states that "[n]o person shall address 
a meeting of a public body without permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of 
the chair, be silent." G.L. c. 30A, § 20(f). Thus, the Board Chair had discretion to decide who 
could speak at the meeting and when they could do so, and did not violate the Law by asking the 
complainant to hold his remarks until the Board finished its discussion. See id.; OML 2013-64, 
OML 2012-23.

3. The Board did not Violate the Open Meeting Law when Police Asked the 
Complainant to Leave the Meeting Room. 
The complainant further alleges that the Board violated the Law when police asked him 
to leave the meeting room for three minutes. The Open Meeting Law states that "[n]o person 
shall disrupt the proceedings of a meeting of a public body. If, after clear warning from the 
chair, a person continues to disrupt the proceedings, the chair may order the person to withdraw 
from the meeting and if the person does not withdraw, the chair may authorize a constable or 
other officer to remove the person from the meeting." G.L. c. 30A, § 20(1). The Board's 
assessment that the complainant was being disruptive was not unreasonable, thus it was not a 
violation for the police to ask the complainant to leave the meeting room. 
4. The Board's Minutes from its December 12, 2012: Januarv 10, 2013; and February 
14. 2013 Meetings are Sufficiently Detailed. 
Public bodies must "create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including 
executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a 
summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the 
meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all 
votes." G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The complainant alleges that the Board's minutes were 
insufficiently detailed for two reasons. First, the complainant alleges that the Board deliberated 
while on recess during its January 10, 2013 meeting, and that those deliberations should have 
been included in the minutes. Second, the complainant alleges that during the Board's March 14, 
2013 meeting, the Chair said that the Board had discussed Bylaw 2254 in previous meetings, and 
because the Board's minutes from its December 12, 2012; January 10, 2013; and February 14, 
2013 meetings do not mention Bylaw 2254, those minutes are insufficiently detailed. 
Regarding the complainant's first allegation, the Board's minutes for its January 10, 2013 
meeting state, "There was a discussion re: meeting Ch 40A requirements. The Board suspended 
for 10 minutes in order to review Ch. 40A for clarification." We do not believe that there was 
communication between or among a quorum of the Board's members during this recess. Rather, 
the members recessed so that the Chair could review the statute. Therefore, the summary in the 
minutes was accurate. Regarding the complainant's second allegation, the Board's minutes from 
its December 12, 2012; January 10, 2013; and February 14, 2013 meetings include a detailed 
record of the proceedings, including discussions about the content of Bylaw 2254, but the 
minutes do not include the bylaw's article and section numbers because the Board did not refer 
to the Bylaw in that way. Therefore, we find that the minutes from the Board's December 12, 
2012; January 10, 2013; and February 14, 2013 meetings provide sufficient detail. 
5. The Chair Violated the Open Meeting Law by Failing to Inform Attendees that the 
Meeting was Being Recorded, however the Violation was De Minimis because the 
Complainant Announced to the Room that he was Recording. 
The Open Meeting Law states that, "[ajfter notifying the chair of the public body, any 
person may make a video or audio recording of an open session of a meeting of a public body . . . 
At the beginning of the meeting the chair shall inform other attendees of any such recordings."

G.L. c. 30A, § 20(e). The Chair did not make a statement at the start of the March 14, 2013 
meeting that the complainant was recording. However, the complainant announced that he was 
recording before the meeting commenced, and again when the Chair and the other attendees 
arrived. While the complainant's recording was open and obvious, the Law nevertheless 
requires that the chair of a public body state at the beginning of the meeting whether anyone is 
recording. OML 2013-116. This clear rule eliminates the need for chairs to determine in each 
individual instance whether a recording is obvious, or whether the statement made by the person 
who is recording was sufficiently clear or audible to attendees. Id. We therefore find that the 
Chair violated the Open Meeting Law, but find that the violation was de minimis, as all attendees 
were aware of the recording, and order no further relief. We remind the Chair of his obligation 
to inform attendees when an individual is recording a meeting. 
We find that (1) the notice for the Board's March 14, 2013 meeting was sufficiently 
specific; (2) the Chair had discretion to limit the complainant's participation during the Board's 
March 14, 2013 meeting; and (3) the Board's December 12, 2012; January 10, 2013; and 
February 14, 2013 meeting minutes were sufficiently detailed. The Chair violated the Open 
Meeting Law by failing to inform the attendees that the meeting was being recorded, however 
the violation was de minimis because the complainant drew substantial attention to the fact that 
he was recording, such that everyone in attendance was aware that he was doing so. We 
therefore order no further relief.

determination does not address any other complaints which may be pending with our office or 
the Board. If you have any questions regarding this determination, please contact our office at
