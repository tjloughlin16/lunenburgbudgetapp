RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney D’Ortenzio: 
 
This office received a complaint from Maureen Longobardi on October 15, 2025, 
alleging that the Middlefield Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on September 15, 2025, and 
you responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated October 13, 2025, following an extension 
of time granted by this office.  Specifically, the complaint alleges that the Board posted notices 
with insufficient specificity for meetings held on July 16, July 30, August 13, and August 27, 
2025.
1 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by posting 
an insufficiently specific notice for the meeting held on August 27, 2025.  We decline to review 
the allegations that the notice for meetings held on July 16, July 30, and August 13, 2025, were 
insufficiently specific, as the complaint was untimely as to those allegations.  In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the 
complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the notices and minutes of the 
Board meetings held on July 16, July 30, August 13, and August 27, 2025.

We find that the allegation regarding notice of the August 27, 2025, meeting to be timely

The complaint alleges that two of the topics listed on the August 27, 2025, meeting 
notice, “Accounting Issues” and “Discuss Potential Items for Special Town Meeting,” were 
insufficiently specific.  The Open Meeting Law requires that the notice describe topics with 
sufficient specificity so that the public can make an informed decision whether to attend the 
meeting in order to observe the discussion regarding a topic of interest.  See OML 2023-86; 
OML 2019-95; OML 2018-22.  It is not enough that a topic is accurate and meaningful to 
members of the public body and those who regularly follow the public body’s business; topics 
should be listed with sufficient clarity to reasonably advise the broader public, including those 
unfamiliar with the regular matters before the body, of the matters to be discussed.  See OML 
2023-79; OML 2023-67; OML 2013-55.  We find both topics to be insufficient as they failed to 
provide the public with a sufficient understanding of the anticipated discussions.  See OML 
2024-32; OML 2020-164; OML 2018-60. 
 
The Board suggests that the topic “Discuss Potential Items for Special Town Meeting” 
was sufficiently specific because the intent of the Board was to discuss all potential town 
meeting warrant articles.  We have explained that when a public body anticipates reviewing all 
town meeting warrant articles or an entire budget, it is not required to list all the warrant articles 
or all budgetary items on its meeting notice.  See OML 2017-185; OML 2012-83.  However, 
when a public body anticipates discussing particular Town Meeting warrant articles or budgetary 
items, it must specifically list them in its meeting notice.  See OML 2024-27; OML 2013-8; 
OML 2011-7.  During the meeting, the Board discussed potential articles to include in the special 
town meeting warrant and therefore we agree with the Board that it was not required to list 
specific warrant articles on the notice.  However, we find the notice to be insufficient here 
because the topic listed the discussion as one involving “Potential Items” and a reasonable 
member of the public could have understood the term “items” as relating to any number of topics 
involving a Special Town Meeting.  See, e.g., OML 2025-182 (finding topic “Highway 
Superintendent to discuss truck concerns” insufficient where notice did not describe the concerns 
or the trucks); OML 2022-212 (finding topic “Informational Items” in no way sufficient to 
inform a reasonable member of the public that the Committee would receive and discuss a 
presentation regarding school district rankings); OML 2021-116 (finding topic “Summer work 
plan – next meetings July 21 and August 18” insufficient where topic could naturally be 
understood as referring to little more than the scheduling of the Board’s next two meetings but 
discussions actually pertained to possible zoning amendments and warrant articles).  Had the 
notice listed the topic as a discussion of “Potential Warrant Articles for Special Town Meeting” 
or “Potential Articles for Special Town Meeting” or even “Potential Warrant for Special Town 
Meeting,” the notice would have been sufficiently specific.  See OML 2024-110 (finding topic 
“STM Warrant” sufficiently specific where public body anticipated reviewing the full warrant); 
OML 2024-74 (finding topic “Open and Close Special Town Meeting warrant” sufficiently 
specific as it was reasonably foreseeable that Board might discuss Special Town Meeting 
warrant, including the proposed articles for the warrant); OML 2017-185 (finding topic

Although not raised in the complaint, we note our concern that the Board did not provide 
a clear start time for its August 27, 2025, meeting.  Here, the notice listed the time of the meeting 
as “Open immediately after the Joint Selectboard/Finance Committee Meeting.”  We remind the 
Board that the Open Meeting Law requires that a public body post notice for a meeting listing 
the “date, time and place of such meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Members of the public should 
not have to guess when a meeting will start or attend the entire joint meeting to avoid the risk of 
missing a portion of the Board’s meeting.  The Board must post notice for its meetings with a 
precise start time.  See OML 2013-61.  When noticing consecutive meetings of two public 
bodies, the notices may state that the second meeting will start at a precise time, or later if the 
prior meeting runs late.  However, a public body’s meeting cannot start earlier than the posted 
start time.  Thus, for example, the Board could have noticed its start time for “7:15 P.M. or 
immediately following the Joint Selectboard/Finance Committee’s meeting if that meeting runs 
past 7:15 P.M.”   
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting a notice with insufficient specificity.  We order the Board’s immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law. 
  
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the
