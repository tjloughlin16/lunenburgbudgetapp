This office received a complaint from Robert Coyne on behalf of Manchester Matters 
Group on September 19, 2022, alleging that the Manchester-by-the-Sea Select Board (the 
“Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally 
filed with the Board on July 20, 2022, and you responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated 
August 5, 2022.1 The complaint alleges that the Board acted outside its authority or contrary to 
local by-law, or contrary to prior representations or prior practice, when the Board placed the 
question of whether to join the North Shore Regional 9-1-1 Center on the annual town election 
ballot rather than on the Town Meeting warrant.  The complaint also alleges that the question 
placed on the ballot was confusing, and that thereafter the Board disregarded the majority vote 
regarding the Regional 9-1-1 Center.  Finally, the complaint alleges that the Board posted an 
insufficient notice for its June 21 meeting at which the Board voted to join the Regional 9-1-1 
Center, thus depriving the public of the opportunity to debate and vote on the issue.  As a 
remedy, the complainant requests that the question whether to join the Regional 9-1-1 Center be 
placed on the Town Meeting warrant for a binding vote. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged in the complaint.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Board’s response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  We also 
reviewed notices for the Board’s June 16, June 21, and August 1 meetings, and reviewed video

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  At some point prior to July 2021, the Select Board began a 
review of the Town’s in-house 9-1-1 dispatch operation and the possibility of joining the North 
Shore Regional 9-1-1 Center.  The Board held a public forum on July 22, 2021, at which it 
presented the pros and cons of joining the Regional 9-1-1 Center; an additional public forum was 
held in September 2021.  In October 2021 the Board voted to conduct a feasibility study 
regarding joining the Regional 9-1-1 Center; the feasibility study was completed in February 
2022 and additional public forums were held in April and May 2022.  
  
The Select Board discussed placing a nonbinding resolution regarding whether to join the 
Regional 9-1-1 Center before the voters at the Annual Spring Town Meeting, but instead opted to 
place a nonbinding advisory question on the May 17, 2022, town election ballot in order to reach 
more voters.  Fifty-seven percent of voters favored keeping 9-1-1 dispatch services in-house 
rather than joining the Regional 9-1-1 Center.   
 
The Board posted notice for a meeting to take place on June 21, 2022.  The notice 
included eight topics for discussion, including “4. Dispatch update.”  During the June 21 
meeting, the Board’s Chair explained that the Board had toured the Regional 9-1-1 Center and 
was prepared to make a decision regarding 9-1-1 dispatch services.  The Chair explained the 
Board’s responsibility to act in the best interest of the Town, even when doing so may not be 
popular; the Chair also stated that a switch to the Regional 9-1-1 Center was recommended 
unanimously by the Town’s Finance Committee.  The Board’s discussion on June 21 regarding 
whether to join the Regional 9-1-1 Center lasted nearly one-and-a-half hours and included 
extensive public comment.  Ultimately, the Board voted 4-1 by roll call to join the Regional 9-1-
1 Center. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
As a preliminary matter, we decline to review all allegations in the complaint pertaining 
to the Board’s authority to place a nonbinding advisory question on the election ballot rather than 
submit the question to Town Meeting and to vote to join the Regional 9-1-1 Center 
notwithstanding the results of that ballot question.  We also decline to review all allegations 
pertaining to the ballot process as well as allegations pertaining to the Board’s compliance with 
local by-laws and practices.  The Division of Open Government’s statutory authority concerns 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  These allegations, even if true,

We do, however, review the complaint’s allegation that the notice for the Board’s June 21 
meeting “stated #4 Dispatch Update. However, the [Board] voted 4-1 [to] sign a contract with 
Northshore 911 Dispatch in Middleton ... The Agenda for this meeting stated Dispatch Update, 
as a result much of the Public was deprived from commenting on this important Public Safety 
Issue.”  We understand from the complaint and subsequent correspondence that the complaint is 
concerned with the failure to specify on the meeting notice that the Board would vote regarding 
the 9-1-1 dispatch matter, and alleges that members of the public did not have an opportunity to 
attend and comment prior to the vote. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  To that end, a public body must post notice of every 
meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(b).  Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates 
will be discussed at the meeting.”  Id.  The listing of topics shall have sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  
We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public 
could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See 
OML 2018-7; OML 2015-35.  A public body need not list each and every detail about a topic in 
order to comply with the Law's requirements.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-
134. 
 
Although it is good practice for a meeting notice to specify that a vote will be taken when 
a vote
 is indeed anticipated in advance, the Open Meeting Law does not require this.  See OML 
2015-66.  It is reasonably foreseeable that a public body may take action and hold a vote 
following discussion of a topic listed on a meeting notice; the Open Meeting Law does not 
require that the notice separately indicate the possibility of a vote.  See OML 2021-153; OML 
2019-36; OML 2015-66; OML 2012-75.  Furthermore, the Open Meeting Law does not require 
that a public body offer an opportunity for public comment on matters on the meeting’s agenda, 
nor does the Open Meeting Law require that a meeting notice specify that there will be an 
opportunity for public comment if one is in fact anticipated.  See G.L. c. 30A § 20(g); OML 
2017-189; OML 2014-31; OML 2014-23.  It is reasonably inferable that a public body may 
allow the public to comment on items listed on its posted meeting notice, even if public comment 
is not specifically listed on the meeting notice.  See OML 2022-62; OML 2014-31.  Therefore, 
the Board was not required to specify on the notice for the June 21 meeting that it would vote 
whether to join the Regional 9-1-1 Center, and the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law 
by not specifying in advance that a vote would be taken.

had been the subject of great discussion within the Town for several months and the complaint 
does not allege that members of the public did not understand the topic that the Board anticipated 
discussing, only that they did not realize the Board would take action on the matter.  Therefore, 
we do not find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in this instance, but recommend 
that the Board list more descriptive and detailed topics on its meeting notices in the future.
