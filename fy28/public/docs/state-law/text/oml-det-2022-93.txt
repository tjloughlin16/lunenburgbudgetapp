This office received a complaint from Doreen Taylor on January 6, 2022, and a second 
complaint on January 24, 2022, alleging that the Seekonk School Committee (the “Committee”) 
violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The first complaint was originally filed 
with the Committee on or about December 22, 2021, the second complaint was originally filed 
with the Committee on or about January 11, 2022, and you responded to both complaints, on 
behalf of the Committee, by separate letters dated January 6, 2022, and January 19, 2022, 
respectively.  Both complaints allege that the Committee met in executive session for an 
improper purpose on December 22, 2021, and January 5, 2022, to discuss prior Open Meeting 
Law complaints. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law 
in the ways alleged.  We resolve these complaints by informal action in accordance with 940 
CMR 29.07(1) after reviewing the original complaints, the Committee’s responses to the 
complaints, the complaints filed with our office requesting further review, and the notices and 
audio recordings of the Committee meetings held on December 22, 2021, and January 5, 2022.1   
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberation and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The law requires that all meetings of a public body be

conducted in an open session, with some exceptions.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  A public 
body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the 
Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that a majority of 
members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each member is 
recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly announced whether 
the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.2  Before entering the executive session, the chair must state 
the purpose for the executive session, stating all subjects that may be revealed without 
compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 
567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary 
... because that is the only notification given the public that a [public body] would conduct 
business in private, and the only way the public would know if the reason for doing so was 
proper or improper”).   
 
Here, the Committee posted notices for meetings to be held on December 22, 2021, and 
January 5, 2022.  Each notice included an Executive Session pursuant to Purpose 1 “to discuss an 
Open Meeting Law complaint . . . filed by Doreen Taylor.”  On both December 22, 2021, and 
January 5, 2022, the Committee convened in open session, announced the purpose for the 
executive session, approved a vote by roll call to enter into executive session, and announced 
that it would not resume in open session following its executive session.   
 
The complaints allege that the Committee improperly met in executive session to discuss 
two Open Meeting Law complaints filed by the complainant.  Our office has stated in past 
determinations that public bodies may meet in executive session under Purpose 1 to review Open 
Meeting Law complaints against the body.  See OML 2015-105; OML 2013-82; OML 2012-119; 
OML 2011-6.  Executive session Purpose 1 allows a public body to discuss “the reputation, 
character, physical condition or mental health, rather than professional competence, of an 
individual, or to discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or charges brought against, 
a public officer, employee, staff member or individual.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1).  We have 
determined that an Open Meeting Law complaint is by its nature a “complaint[]... brought 
against[] a public officer,” namely, the members of the public body.  See OML 2012-119.  
Accordingly, we find that the Committee was permitted to conduct its review in executive 
session during both the December 22, 2021, and January 5, 2022, meetings, and therefore did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law.  
 
The complainant is also concerned that the notice identified her as the individual who 
filed the Open Meeting Law complaints, but the Committee failed to specifically notify her that 
she would be discussed during the December and January meetings.  We note that a meeting 
notice should generally identify the person who filed the Open Meeting Law complaints, or 
include other identifying information about the complaints.  See OML 2013-82.  If a public body 
enters executive session under Purpose 1, the public body must afford the subject of the 
complaint certain rights, including written notice of the executive session and the right to be 
present during deliberations involving the individual.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a)(1).  Here, Ms.

Taylor was not the subject of the Open Meeting Law complaints that the Committee reviewed in 
executive session, although she was named as the complainant.  Rather, the subject of the 
complaints were the individual Committee members against whom the Open Meeting Law 
complaints had been filed.  Therefore, because Ms. Taylor was not the subject of the complaints 
that the Committee was reviewing, she was not entitled to be present during the executive 
sessions, and the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law by not giving her individual 
notice of the meetings.  See OML 2017-39; OML 2013-52. 
 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.  We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved. 
This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or
