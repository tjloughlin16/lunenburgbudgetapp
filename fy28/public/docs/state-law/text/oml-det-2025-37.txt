This office received a complaint from George Harris on February 12, 2025, alleging that 
the Wayland Zoning Board of Appeals (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on December 4, 2024, and 
you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated January 16, 2025, 
following an extension of time granted by this office.  The complaint alleges that the Board 
failed to timely approve minutes of the meeting held on August 13, 2024.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by not 
timely approving the August 13, 2024, meeting minutes; we further find that this was an 
intentional violation of the Open Meeting Law.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the 
original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review.  We also reviewed the notices, minutes and video recordings of the 
Board meetings held on November 12 and December 10, 2024.
1   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting,

must be reviewed and approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” 
means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless 
the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.

Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  
See OML 2018-67; OML 2017-133.  
 
The Board held meetings on August 13, September 10, October 8, November 12, and 
Dec
ember 10, 2024.  The Board was required to approve the minutes of its August 13, 2024, 
meeting within 30 days or within the next three public body meetings, i.e. by November 12, 
2024, as that was the later of the two time periods.  See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-111.  
During the November 12, 2024, meeting, the Board reviewed the minutes of the August 13, 
2024, meeting, and Chair Thomas White made a motion to approve the minutes, which was 
seconded by Board Member Adam Hirsch.  Board Member Justine Paul noted that her name was 
incorrect in the minutes and Chair White then revised his motion to approve the minutes with 
corrections.  After a brief discussion, the Board decided to “bypass” the topic without voting, and 
to revise the minutes before approving them.  The Board expressly acknowledged that doing so 
would mean they were in violation of the Open Meeting Law for failing to timely approve 
minutes.  During the December 10, 2024, meeting, the Board reviewed the minutes of the August 
13, 2024, meeting noting that the minutes still incorrectly recorded Ms. Paul’s name.  Chair 
White made a motion to approve the minutes with the understanding that staff would correct the 
member’s name, which motion was seconded by Board Member Shaunt Sarian.  The Board 
unanimously approved the minutes by roll call vote.   
 
The Board explains that it delayed approval of the August 13, 2024, minutes in order to 
make
 corrections, and then approved the minutes during its next meeting on December 10, 2024.  
We have previously found that the Open Meeting Law was not violated where a public body 
failed to approve meeting minutes due to a significant obstacle, and the public body made a good 
faith effort to approve the meeting minutes within a reasonable period of time.  See, e.g. OML 
2021-177 (finding no violation where public body engaged in a good faith effort to approve 
meeting minutes after finding errors in initial drafts); OML 2018-63 (finding no violation where 
minutes were ready for approval and reviewed by public body but body delayed approval where 
draft minutes did not accurately memorialize and reflect tenor of conversation); OML 2015-190 
(finding no violation when the public body had repeatedly made good faith efforts to approve 
minutes through multiple rounds of edits in an effort to reach a consensus among its members).  
We find the instant circumstances meaningfully distinguishable because here, the Board made no 
effort to correct the errors in the August 13, 2024, minutes.  Rather, during the December 10, 
2024, meeting, the Board noted that the minutes still incorrectly recorded Ms. Paul’s name, as 
well as the chair’s last name, and then approved the minutes with the understanding that the 
corrections would be made, action which it could have taken during the November 12, 2024, 
meeting.  As such, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to approve 
the August 13, 2024, meeting minutes in a timely manner.

We must determine whether this violation was, as the complainant urges, an intentional 
one.  Se
e G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body 
or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting 
Law.  Id.  As of the November 12, 2024, meeting when the minutes were due for approval, the 
Board had been advised by the Attorney General twice, in 2019 and 2022, of its obligation to 
review and approve minutes in a timely manner and that timely manner means within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later.  See OML 2022-120; OML 
2019-21.  Moreover, we specifically advised the Board that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  Because the Board had previously been advised 
that, in the absence of good cause for delay, the failure to approve minutes within the next three 
meetings or 30 days, whichever is longer, violates the Open Meeting Law, and the Board itself 
acknowledged at the November 12, 2024, meeting that failure to approve the August meeting 
minutes would violate the Open Meeting Law, we find that this violation is intentional.  See G.L. 
c. 30A, § 18; 940 CMR 29.02; OML 2013-63.   
 
On December 3, 2024, the Attorney General again found that the Board violated the 
Open M
eeting Law by failing to timely approve meeting minutes.  We similarly found that 
violation was intentional in light of the 2019 and 2022 warnings, and we recommended, and the 
Board has since paid, a fine of $200.00.  See 2024-227; see also G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c) (“Upon the 
finding of a violation, the attorney general may issue an order to . . . impose a civil penalty upon 
the public body of not more than $1,000 for each intentional violation”).  We do not rely on the 
December 2024 determination in reaching the current findings as the August 13, 2024, meeting 
minutes were already past due at the time that determination was issued, and we decline to 
recommend another fine in light of the timing of the prior order and the approval of the minutes 
very shortly after.  Nonetheless, we note that the Board has now been advised four times of its 
obligation to approve meeting minutes in a timely manner, and we emphasize that future 
violations may result in a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation.   
 
For t
he reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely approve the minutes of the August 13, 2024, meeting.  Furthermore, we find this 
violation to be intentional.  We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the 
law’s requirements and caution the Board that another future finding of an intentional violation 
of the Open Meeting Law may result in a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation.
