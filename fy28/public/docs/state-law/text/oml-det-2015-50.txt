RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Dupere: 
This office received a complaint from Ms. Paula Quinn, dated November 22, 2014, 
alleging that the Pioneer Valley Chinese Immersion Charter School Board of Trustees (the 
"Board") violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally 
filed with the Board on July 25, 2014, and the Board responded to the original complaint by 
letter dated October 10, 2014. In her complaint, Ms. Quinn alleges that the Board (i) improperly 
voted to approve changes to an employment contract during an executive session held pursuant 
to G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2); (ii) failed to provide sufficient notice for a meeting; and (iii) failed to 
make meeting minutes available upon request. 
We appreciate the patience and cooperation of the parties while we considered this 
matter. Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
improperly voting to approve changes to an employment contract in executive session on June 
19, 2014. We do not find that this violation was intentional, however, as the Board was acting 
on the advice of counsel when it committed the violation. Further, we find that, following a 
request, the Board failed to provide open session meeting minutes for its June 25, 2014, meeting 
within the time frame required by law. However, we find that the Board provided sufficient 
notice for that subsequent meeting. 
In reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaint; the Board's response; 
and the request for further review filed with our office. We also reviewed the minutes of 
meetings of the Board held on June 19, 2014 and June 25, 2014. Finally, we reviewed a written 
statement by Attorney Fernand Dupere, dated April 14, 2015.'

FACTS 
We find the facts as follows. The Board is a nine-member public body that meets at 
regular intervals to manage the affairs of the Pioneer Valley Chinese Immersion Charter School 
("PVC1CS").2 The Board scheduled a meeting for June 19, 2014. The agenda for that meeting 
listed a planned executive session to "conduct strategy sessions in preparation for negotiations 
with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective bargaining sessions or contract negotiations 
with nonunion personnel." On the morning of June 19, 2014, the parent of a PVCICS student 
sent an e-mail to the Board. The e-mail, quoting from the Attorney General's Open Meeting 
Law website, reminded the Board that it may not vote to execute contracts during certain 
executive sessions. Board Chair Mindy Chang forwarded the e-mail to Attorney Dupere, 
inquiring whether the statements contained in the e-mail were accurate. Because Attorney 
Dupere was otherwise occupied, he did not immediately respond. Hard copies of the e-mail sent 
by the PVCICS parent were made available to the Board during the open session portion of the 
Board's June 19, 2014 meeting. 
At the end of the Board's June 19, 2014 meeting, the Board entered executive session. 
Once in executive session, Chair Chang placed a phone call to Attorney Dupere. During this 
phone call. Attorney Dupere stated that it was permissible for the Board to conduct the vote in 
executive session. Ultimately, two votes were taken during the executive session: (i) to approve 
an employment contract for the PVCICS Principal; and (ii) to give written notice to the 
Executive Director that a roll-over clause was being removed from his contract. On June 20, 
2014, Chair Chang again e-mailed Attorney Dupere, asking him to double-check the advice he 
had given during the telephone call the previous day. After conducting additional research. 
Attorney Dupere e-mailed Chair Chang on June 23, 2014, stating that the Board should schedule 
a further meeting to conduct the votes at issue again in open session. 
On June 23, 2014, the Board posted notice for a meeting on June 25, 2014 to repeat the 
discussion held during the June 19, 2014 meeting. Regarding the Principal's contract, the 
minutes of the June 25, 2014 meeting show that the Board Chair summarized the provisions in 
the contract and that a vote took place with the motion passing 7-1. Regarding the Executive 
Director's contract, the minutes show that the Board Chair summarized the roll-over clause in 
the contract. A discussion took place during which comparisons were made to other 
employment contracts at comparable institutions. Various Board members voiced their opinions 
and concerns. Ultimately, the motion to approve the Executive Director's contract passed with a 
6-2 vote. 
On July 11, 2014, Ms. Quinn requested a copy of the minutes from the June 25, 2014 
meeting. On July 12, 2014, the PVCICS Executive Director sent an e-mail stating that the 
minutes were not yet available, and that the Board wanted to approve them before releasing 
them. Ms. Quinn was not provided with a copy of the June 25, 2014 minutes until October 10, 
2014, when the Board responded to her Open Meeting Law complaint.

DISCUSSION 
I. The Board Violated the Open Meeting Law by Voting to Authorize the 
Principal's Contract in Executive Session. 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted "to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based." Ghiglione v. School Comm. of 
Southbridge. 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). However, the Open Meeting Law permits public bodies 
to enter executive session and conduct deliberations outside of the public view for ten specific 
purposes. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a). One appropriate purpose for executive session is "[t]o conduct 
strategy sessions in preparation for negotiations with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective 
bargaining sessions or contract negotiations with nonunion personnel." G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2) 
("Purpose 2"). Purpose 2 allows public bodies to meet in executive session to discuss strategy 
and to engage in direct negotiations, but it does not allow the public body to take a final vote to 
execute a contract behind closed doors. See G.L. c. 3OA, § 21(a)(2); OML 2013-195; OML 
2011-56; OML 2011-44; OML 2011-28.4 Thus, the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
voting to execute the Principal's contract during its June 19, 2014 executive session. The 
complaint urges that we find this violation to be intentional. See OML 2014-41 (holding that a 
public body intentionally violated the Open Meeting Law when it had been informed of the law's 
requirements and nevertheless acted in violation thereof). Where a public body acts in good faith 
compliance with the advice of the body's legal counsel, such conduct will not be considered an 
intentional violation of the law. 940 CMR 29.02 (defining "Intentional Violation"). Based on 
Attorney Dupere's written statement, we find that the Board relied on the advice of counsel when 
conducting the vote during the June 19, 2014 executive session. Accordingly, we do not find an 
intentional violation of the Open Meeting Law. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(g); 940 CMR 29.02; 
OML 2013-97. 
If a vote is improperly taken in executive session, a public body may remedy a violation 
of the Open Meeting Law by taking an independent, deliberative action, but not by merely 
ratifying the secret decision in a ceremonial and perfunctory way. See Pearson v. Board of 
Selectmen of Longmeadow, 49 Mass. App. Ct. 119, 125 (2000). The minutes for the June 25, 
2014 meeting show that the Board engaged in a public, independent deliberative action. We 
therefore find that the Board's reconsideration of the issues discussed during the June 19, 2014 
meeting during its open June 25, 2014 meeting was appropriate remedial action. See OML 
2011-14. 
II. The Board Gave Sufficient Notice of its June 25. 2014 Meeting. 
The Open Meeting Law provides that, "a public body shall post notice of every meeting 
at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays." G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(b). Here, notice was posted on June 23, 2014 (a Monday) for a meeting on June 25, 
2014 (a Wednesday). Because the notice was posted at least 48 hours in advance of the meeting, 
the requirement of the Open Meeting Law were satisfied. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). The 
complaint suggests that PVCICS bylaws place a more stringent five-calendar-day requirement 
for the posting of special meetings. While this may be the case, our review concerns only

III. The Board Failed to Provide Meeting Minutes within the Time Frame Required 
by Law. 
"The minutes of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, 
shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 days." G.L. c. 30A, § 22(b) 
(emphasis added). Here, Ms. Quinn requested the minutes of the Board's June 25, 2014 open 
session meeting on July 11, 2014. The Board, therefore, should have provided the minutes to her 
no later than July 21, 2014. The Board's statement that the minutes would be released once they 
were approved is in conflict with the plain text of the Open Meeting Law, which requires open 
session minutes to be released whether or not they have been approved. See G.L. c. 3OA, § 
22(c). By failing to release them within the time required by law, the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law. We remind the Board of the importance of complying with this requirement. 
As a final matter, we note that the Board failed to respond to Ms. Quinn's Open Meeting 
Law complaint within the time required by law. Unless an extension of time has been requested, 
a public body must, within 14 business days of receipt of an Open Meeting Law complaint, send 
a copy of the complaint to the Attorney General's Office and notify the Attorney General's 
Office of any remedial action taken. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5), (6). Here, the 
original complaint was filed with the Board on July 25, 2014. Therefore, the Board should have 
responded no later than August 14, 2014. Because the Board did not request an extension and 
responded by letter dated October 14, 2014, we find that the Board failed to comply with the 
Open Meeting Law's requirements.5 See OML 2013-94. 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
improperly voting to approve changes to an employment contract in executive session. We also 
find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to provide meeting minutes within 
the time frame required by law. We order the Board's immediate and future compliance with the 
Open Meeting Law and caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.
