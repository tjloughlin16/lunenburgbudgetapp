 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints 
Dear Attorney D’Ortenzio: 
This office received a complaint from Stephen Barr on June 15, 2023, alleging that the 
Sandwich Board of Health (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.1  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about May 9, and you 
responded to the complaints, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated June 14.  The complaint 
alleges that following the Board’s adjournment of its April 10 meeting, the Board members 
remained in the meeting room talking for approximately eight minutes and two Board 
members continued talking as they walked to their cars.
      
 
We resolve this complaint by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 
29.07(2)(a), and find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  In reaching a 
determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and 
the complaint filed with our office requesting further review.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a public body be properly noticed 
and open to members of the public for the duration of the meeting, unless an executive session 
is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21; see also OML 2014-118; OML 2012-46.  The 
law defines a “meeting” as “a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within 
the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.   
 
The Board duly posted notice for and held a meeting at 7:00 P.M. on April 10 at the 
Sand Hill School, which is a two-room schoolhouse located in Sandwich.  The complaint

alleges that the Board adjourned its meeting at 8:17 P.M., at which time the one member of 
the public who was in attendance at the meeting left the meeting location.  The complaint 
further alleges that the three Board members were not seen leaving the building until 8:25 
P.M., and that two Board members continued speaking as they walked to their cars.  The 
Board acknowledges that its members briefly remained in the meeting room after adjourning 
but asserts that they were rearranging and re-setting the meeting room to its schoolhouse use 
in the absence of custodial staff, and in the meanwhile engaged in informal conversations and 
pleasantries about their families and hobbies.  The Board expressly denies that its members 
discussed matters within the Board’s jurisdiction during that time.  The complainant, who was 
not present to hear any conversations that took place during this brief period of time, asserts in 
his request for review of the complaint that “[t]here is absolutely no proof” that the Board’s 
description of its conversations is true and that it should be believed false unless each Board 
member signs a sworn statement under penalty of perjury regarding the nature of the 
conversation.   
 
Absent sufficient evidence to the contrary, we credit a public body’s account of the 
facts.  See OML 2017-93; OML 2016-141; see also LaPointe v. License Board of Worcester, 
389 Mass. 454 (1983) (“There is every presumption in favor of the honesty and sufficiency of 
the motives actuating public officers in actions ostensibly taken for the general welfare.”).  
We find no evidence that the Board discussed Board business during the approximately eight 
minutes that its members remained in the meeting room and walked to their cars following 
adjournment of the April 10 meeting.  See OML 2021-34; OML 2019-52.  Accordingly, we 
find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or 
the Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
