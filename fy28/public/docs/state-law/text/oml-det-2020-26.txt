RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint
Dear Ms. Keegan:
This office received a complaint from Marian Baker on January 3, 2020, alleging that the 
Uxbridge Historic District Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.1 2 The complaint was originally filed with the Commission on October 31, 
and the Commission responded by letters dated November 20 and November 22} In the 
complaint, Ms. Baker asserts that she received a letter from the Commission as an Historic 
Property Owner that the Commission “never voted on.”3
The Commission contends that the complaint is untimely. The Open Meeting Law states 
that complaints must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation. G.L. 
c. 30A, § 23(b). If the alleged violation could not have been known at the time it occurred, then 
the complaint must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the date on which the alleged 
violation could reasonably have been discovered. 940 CMR 29.05(4). The Commission states 
that the letter to Historic Property Owners was sent on or about September 17. The complainant 
claims she received the letter “in late September.” We are unable to come to a conclusion of 
exactly when the letter was received so as to put the complainant on notice of an alleged 
violation. We, therefore, treat this complaint as timely and review its merits.
After reviewing the original complaint, the Commission’s responses to the complaint, the 
letter to Historic Property Owners described in the complaint, and the notices and meeting

minutes of the Commission’s June 17 and August 13 meetings, as well as video recordings of 
those meetings, we resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 940 CMR 
29.07(2)(a) and find that the Commission did not violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged.
The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a pubic body be properly noticed and 
open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
20(a)-(b), 21. A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public body with 
respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.” G.L. c. 30A, § 18. Any communication 
among a quorum of a public body on matters that are pending or will come before it and within 
the public body’s jurisdiction is considered deliberation. See OML 2015-3; OML 2014-108; 
OML 2013-136; Boelter v. Board of Selectmen of Wayland. 479 Mass. 233, 243 (2018). The 
Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to vote on a topic that it has discussed during 
an open meeting. Here, the Commission discussed the letter during at least two open session 
meetings, during its June 17 meeting for over twelve minutes and then again during its August 
13 meeting. Despite the complainant’s assertion, the Commission voted to approve the letter 
during its June 17 meeting and then voted on additional information to be included with the letter 
during its August 13 meeting. Where the Commission duly posted notices for both the June 17 
and August 13 meetings, the notices included the letter as a topic of discussion, and the letter 
was actually discussed and voted on in open session during those meetings, we find that the 
Commission did not violate the Open Meeting Law. We note that the complainant was present 
at the August 13 meeting.
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Commission. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you have any 
questions.
