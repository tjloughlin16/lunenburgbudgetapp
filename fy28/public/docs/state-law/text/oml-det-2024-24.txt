RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints 
Dear Ms. Gibbons: 
This office received six complaints from George Harris between June 28, 2023, and 
October 4, 2023, alleging that the Wayland School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the 
Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaints were filed with the Committee 
between May 24, 2023, and August 11, 2023, and you responded to the complaints, on behalf of 
the Committee.  The complaints allege that the Committee failed to timely respond to a request 
for open and executive session minutes; failed to timely respond to an Open Meeting Law 
complaint; and failed to periodically review executive session minutes to determine whether 
there is a continued purpose for nondisclosure. 
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed these matters.  Following 
our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to 
an Open Meeting Law complaint within the timeframe required by the law.  We find that the 
Committee did not violate the law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaints, the Committee’s response to the complaints, and the 
complaints filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed notices and 
minutes of Committee meetings held on May 1, 2023, May 10, 2023, May 24, 2023, June 21, 
2023, July 19, 2023, and August 23, 2023.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On May 4, 2023, at 11:36 A.M., the complainant sent an 
email to the Committee requesting “to inspect all minutes of executive sessions not previously 
released in their entirety.”  At 12:55 P.M. that same day, Vice Chair Ellen Grieco1 responded to 
the request advising the complainant that the Committee has “released es minutes up through the 
end of 2022” and she would ask the Committee’s Administrative Assistant to “gather any 
released 2023 es minutes and send them to you.”  At 1:20 P.M., the complainant responded to 
Ms. Grieco and clarified that his request should include “all executive session minutes containing 
redactions.”   
 
On May 7, 2023, at 8:10 A.M., Ms. Grieco sent an email advising the complainant that 
“all executive session minutes through 2022, both those fully released and those released with 
redactions” are posted on the school district’s website, https://www.wayland.k12.ma.us/ 
school_committee.  Later that day at 10:14 A.M., the complainant responded that he was 
requesting “the unredacted minutes of (1) executive sessions whose minutes have not been 
released and (2) executive sessions whose minutes have been released with redactions.”  Ms. 
Grieco replied at 11:19 A.M. that “someone will get back to you within the specified timeframe.” 
 
During a meeting held on May 10, 2023, Ms. Grieco announced that minutes of executive 
sessions held between May 2021 and November 2022 had been reviewed during an executive 
session meeting held on May 1, 2023, and that the Committee had approved fifteen sets of 
minutes to be released, some with redactions.  The Committee identified the dates of the fifteen 
sets of minutes that had been approved for release.  The Committee later convened in executive 
session and reviewed nine sets of minutes of executive sessions held between January 4, 2023, 
and March 29, 2023, for possible release. 
 
On May 24, 2023, at 12:38 P.M., Committee Chair Erin Gibbons advised the complainant 
via email that “the Committee reviewed all unreleased executive session minutes from 2023” 
during its May 10, 2023, meeting and determined that nine sets of minutes of executive sessions 
held between January 4, 2023, and March 29, 2023, could be released with redactions, and that 
such minutes would be posted to the school district’s website by May 31, 2023.  During the 
Committee meeting held at 6 P.M. that night, the Committee specifically identified in open 
session the nine sets of minutes that had been approved for release. 
 
On May 30, 2023, the complainant sent an email to Chair Gibbons requesting “minutes of 
the most recent meeting at which the Committee announced or reported it had conducted a 
periodic review of all withheld executive session minutes” and “all released executive session 
minutes containing redactions” from July 1, 2010, to the present.  The next day at 11:06 A.M., 
Chair Gibbons advised the complainant that she would have “to look into this” and “need some 
time to get the answers.”  The complainant responded on June 6, 2023, stating that he “would 
appreciate hearing from you by June 16.”

On May 4, 2023, the complainant requested “to inspect all minutes of executive sessions 
not previously released in their entirety,” including minutes containing redactions.  Ms. Grieco  
responded that same day advising the complainant that the Committee had released executive 
session minutes through the end of 2022 and that such minutes, both those fully released and 
those released with redactions were posted on the school district’s website and provided the 
specific website address where the minutes could be found. 
 
At the end of May and then in July, the complainant sent an email to the Committee 
requesting access to “minutes of the most recent meeting” at which the Committee announced it 
had conducted a periodic review of executive session minutes.  Chair Gibbons responded to the 
requests and advised the complainant that all open session minutes were posted to the school 
district’s website, provided the specific website address where the minutes could be found, and 
suggested that he was “welcome to review those minutes to locate the ones you are seeking.” 
 
The Open Meeting Law, unlike the Public Records Law, does not specify in what format 
min
utes must be made available.  Rather, the Open Meeting Law simply states that meeting 
minutes must “be made available upon request.”  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c), but see G.L. c. 66, § 
6A(d) (records access officer shall, to the extent feasible, provide the public record in the 
requestor’s preferred format).  Therefore, directing a requester to a website where the requested 
minutes are posted and accessible complies with the requirement to make the minutes 
“available.”  As such, because both Chair Gibbons and Ms. Grieco directed the complainant to 
the school district’s website where minutes were posted, we find that the Committee’s responses 
to the complainant’s requests for open and executive session minutes complied with the Open 
Meeting Law.  We also find that the Committee responded to the requests in a timely manner 
where Ms. Grieco responded to the early May request within three days, while Chair Gibbons 
responded to the July request within four days, and initially responded to the late May request 
within seven days and then followed up with the complainant ten days thereafter on June 16, 
2023.  Where the Committee’s initial response to the late May complaint was that it needed 
“some time” and the complainant responded stating he “would appreciate hearing from [the 
Committee] by June 16,” we find that the Committee timely responded to the request when it

Responding to an Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
The complaint dated June 22, 2023, alleges that the Committee failed to timely
  respond 
to the Open Meeting Law complaint filed on May 24, 2023.  The Open Meeting Law requires 
that within 14 days of receipt of a complaint, the public body review the complaint’s allegations; 
take remedial action, if appropriate; and send to the Attorney General a copy of the complaint 
and a description of any remedial action taken.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  A 
public body must meet to review the complaint and formulate a response, or meet to delegate 
that authority, and respond to the complaint within 14 business days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 
OML 2022-43; OML 2017-69; OML 2012-90.   
 
Here,
 the Committee acknowledges that the complaint was “lost in the shuffle” and 
therefore it did not respond until over one month after the complaint was filed.  Because the 
Committee failed to respond to an Open Meeting Law complaint within 14 business days, we 
find that it violated the Open Meeting Law.    
 
Periodic Review of Executive Session Minutes 
 
The remaining complaints allege that the Committee does not conduct a periodic review 
of all executive minutes.4  Public bodies must create and maintain accurate minutes of all 
meetings.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Executive session minutes may be withheld from disclosure to 
the public “as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no 
longer.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).  When the purpose for a valid executive session has been served, 
the minutes and any documents or exhibits used at the session must be disclosed unless the 
attorney-client privilege or an exemption to the public records law applies to withhold them, in 
whole or in part, from disclosure.  See id.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body or its chair or designee must, at 
reason
able intervals, review the minutes of executive sessions to determine if continued 
nondisclosure of those minutes is warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1).  Although “reasonable 
interval” is not defined by the law, our office has found a quarterly review or a review every six 
months to be appropriate.  See OML 2019-45; OML 2017-104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180. 
At the meeting following each periodic review, the public body must announce which executive 
session minutes will be released and which will continue to be withheld, and such announcement 
shall be included in the minutes.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); OML 2019-3; OML 2015-94; OML 
2013-56.  A public body’s obligation to review executive session minutes for possible release is 
ongoing.  If a public body reviews executive session minutes and determines that the purpose for 
the executive session remains such that the minutes should continue to be withheld from the 
public, it must continue to review those same executive session minutes at reasonable intervals 
until it determines that the reason for secrecy has expired.  See OML 2022-155; OML 2022-46; 
OML 2019-133.

We find here that the Committee regularly reviewed executive session minutes that had 
not bee
n previously released to determine if continued nondisclosure of those minutes was 
warranted.  We further find that the Committee announced in open session the executive session 
minutes to be released and the minutes to be withheld.  We agree that the Committee did not 
specifically identify, during its June 21, 2023, meeting, the dates of executive session minutes 
that were reviewed for the years 2010, 2011, and 2012.  However, we understand that the 
Committee reviewed all executive session minutes of meetings held in these three years that had 
not been fully released and therefore find the announcement to be sufficient.  See, e.g., OML 
2023-207 (finding that a notice sufficiently identified executive session minutes to be approved 
where topic was listed as accept minutes “from 9/13/2021 to 12/12/2022” and Board reviewed all 
executive session minutes of meetings held between those two dates).  As such, we find that the 
Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law in this regard.  
 
With respect to the allegations that the Committee does not continue to conduct periodic 
revi
ews of executive session minutes that were previously reviewed but not released, either in 
whole or in part, our office will not conduct broad audits of public bodies based on generalized 
allegations.  See OML 2022-43; OML 2013-180; OML 2012-106.  As such, we decline to 
formally review precisely when the Committee conducted a periodic review of all withheld 
executive session minutes over a ten-year period.  Nonetheless, we note that if executive session 
minutes have been released with redactions pursuant to the attorney-client privilege or one or 
more of the exemptions under the Public Records Law, in accordance with G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f), 
then the Committee is not obligated to continue to periodically review those minutes.  However, 
if executive session minutes were released but redacted because the purpose for those specific 
executive session discussions remained ongoing, then the Committee would be required to 
continue to periodically review those minutes.

We remind the Committee of its obligations under the law and commend 
the Committee 
for ensuring future compliance with the law by putting a process in place to ensure that redacted 
executive session minutes are periodically reviewed and properly announced during the next open 
session meeting.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
We find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to an 
Open Meeting Law complaint within 14 business days.  We order immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
  
 
5 We acknowledge there may be circumstances where the purpose for the executive session will remain ongoing in 
perpetuity, such as when executive sessions are convened to comply with laws requiring confidentiality of certain

possible release.
