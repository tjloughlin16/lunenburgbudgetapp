RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint
Dear Attorney Goldberg:
This office received a complaint from Owen Murphy on September 11, 2019, alleging 
that the Weston Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§18- 
25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on June 14, 2019, and you responded on 
behalf of the Board by letter dated July 10, 2019. In his complaint, Mr. Murphy alleges that the 
Board did not approve minutes for its February 13, 2018, executive session in a timely manner, 
that a significant topic of discussion was removed from those minutes before they were approved 
and that then-Selectman Doug Gillespie deliberated outside of a public meeting via an email 
requesting the removal of that topic from the minutes.1
Following our review, we find that: 1) the Board did not approve minutes for its February 
13, 2018, executive session in a timely manner; 2) the Board’s February 13, 2018, executive 
session minutes, as approved, were inaccurate; and 3) then-Selectman Gillespie deliberated 
outside of a public meeting via an October 9, 2018, email. In addition, we find the Board’s 
approval of inaccurate executive session minutes to be intentional.
In reaching a determination, we reviewed the complaint, the Board’s response and the 
request for further review. This review included both the draft and final versions of the February 
13, 2018, executive session minutes. We also reviewed dozens of emails of Board members 
about Chapter 40B development projects that were obtained by the Complainant via a public 
records request. Finally, we reviewed a Purchase Agreement for the sale of the property known 
as 751 Boston Post Road in Weston, effective February 21, 2019.

FACTS
We find the facts as follows. The Board is a three-member public body; thus two 
members constitute a quorum. The Board’s discussions that are the subject of this complaint 
involve an application for a comprehensive permit for an affordable housing development under 
General Laws Chapter 40B. In Weston, the Select Board has sought to reach the law’s 10% 
affordable housing threshold for several years, at which point Weston can invoke a “safe harbor” 
and its Zoning Board of Appeals may refuse to allow further 40B developments. The Board 
considered the possibility that a property at 133 Boston Post Road could be developed as a major 
40B project by Boston Properties as early as November 3, 2016.
On February 13, 2018, the Board met in executive session citing to Purpose 6 to discuss 
“the purchase, exchange, lease or value of an interest in real property related to Boston 
Properties at 133 Boston Post Road.” The Board’s notice for its October 9, 2018, meeting, posted 
on October 4, 2018, included the approval of minutes for its February 13, 2018, meeting. 
According to emails published online on May 16, 2019, in response to a public records request 
from another individual, the Assistant to the Town Manager emailed the Board a meeting packet 
on October 5, 2018, that included the draft minutes of the February 13, 2018 meeting. A draft of 
the February 13, 2018, executive session minutes was later posted online on June 7, 2019, in 
response to a public records request by Mr. Murphy.2 These draft minutes, in addition to a 
summary of the discussion of the property at 133 Boston Post Road, included a paragraph 
regarding a property at 751 Boston Post Road, which was owned at the time by then-Selectman 
Doug Gillespie (“Selectman Gillespie”) and his family. The paragraph from the draft minutes 
that the Assistant to the Town Manager circulated on October 5, 2018, read:
The Board discussed the possibility of a developer building a friendly 40B project 
at 751 Boston Post Road and how that might offset the magnitude of the 133 Boston 
Post Road project while still achieving safe haven. It was noted that the developer 
of Highland Meadows was interested in developing 751 Boston Post Road as a 
friendly 40B.
In an email three days later, on October 8, 2018, Selectman Gillespie replied only to the 
Assistant to the Town Manager, “The February 2018 Executive Session minutes included are in 
error! There was no 40B discussion at [sic] 751 BPR at this meeting. The developer didn’t reach 
out until May 2018.1 would have recused myself anyway.”
The Assistant to the Town Manager replied on October 9, 2018, “Let me look into that. 
Thanks for pointing it out.” Selectman Gillespie replied fifteen minutes later, and copied on his 
email Board Chair Boshart, “Please just take any reference to 751 out of the minutes. Any 
mention by me was not for public consumption because it creates a conflict of interest for me. 
Bring revised copies to the meeting, or remove it from consent agenda[.]”
The draft minutes were then altered to remove any reference to 751 Boston Post Road, 
and the revised version was approved unanimously by the Board at the October 9, 2018, meeting.

DISCUSSION
I. The Board Did Not Approve Minutes for its February 13, 2018. Executive Session in a
Timely Manner.
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting minutes must be reviewed 
and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). The Open Meeting Law itself does not 
define “timely manner.” Flowever, the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations 
provide that “timely manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 
days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.” 940 
C.M.R. 29.11 (2). Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at 
the next meeting. See OML 2019-39; OML 2014-15; OML 2012-91.3
Here, despite meeting more than a dozen times in the intervening months, the Board 
failed to approve minutes for its February 13, 2018, executive session until October 9, 2018.4 In 
its response, the Board asserts that the delayed creation of minutes was due to a lack of personnel 
and unusual burdens on staff time. The Board also commits to developing a staff protocol to 
ensure timely creation of minutes. Although we acknowledge that many local public bodies 
delegate responsibility for drafting minutes to staff, and we understand the demands on a Board 
with limited staff, the Board is still obligated to timely approve minutes. If staff resources are not 
available to create minutes for the Board to approve, then Board members must take on this 
responsibility to ensure compliance with the law. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2019-133;
OML 2017-27; OML 2013-166.
II. The Board’s February 13. 2018, Executive Session Minutes, as Approved, were
Inaccurate.
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a) (emphasis added). When reviewing 
minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the

accuracy requirement. See OML 2019-136; OML 2013-64. By substantial compliance, we mean 
that the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who 
did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what 
occurred. Id.
Selectman Gillespie stated in his second email that the discussion of the property at 751 
Boston Post Road created a conflict of interest for himself and he asked for references to that 
property to be removed because the topic “was not for public consumption.” Furthermore, we 
have no reason to believe that the Assistant Town Manager would have added the paragraph 
about 751 Boston Post Road to the draft minutes if that property had not actually been discussed. 
We therefore find that the minutes of the February 13, 2018, executive session, as approved, 
were inaccurate because they failed to summarize a topic that was discussed.
Finally, we must determine whether the violation was, as the complainant urges, 
intentional. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c). An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public 
body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].” 940 CMR 29.02. 
An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of 
the law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open 
Meeting Law. Id.
Here, all members of the Board received the draft February 13, 2018, executive session 
minutes on October 5, 2018, from the Assistant to the Town Manager, and then a few days later, 
voted to approve the meeting minutes, with the paragraph about the purported conflict of interest 
removed. The Board’s approval of the inaccurate minutes served to help Selectman Gillespie 
conceal his perceived conflict of interest from the public. Chair Boshart was specifically alerted 
by Selectman Gillespie’s email as to why the topic was being removed. Even if the Board 
members did not take this action with the specific intent to violate the Open Meeting Law, at the 
very least they acted with deliberate ignorance of the law’s requirements. The Legislature 
designed the Open Meeting Law “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the deliberations 
and decisions on which public policy is based.” See Dist. Attorney for N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm, of 
Wayland. 455 Mass. 561. 563 (2009); Ghiglione v. School Comm, of Southbridge. 376 Mass. 70, 
72 (1978). Accurate executive session minutes are essential to the transparency aims of the Open 
Meeting Law, since the public is unable to witness deliberations in executive session. See OML 
2019-133; OML 2012-43. Therefore, we find that the Board intentionally violated the Open 
Meeting Law by approving inaccurate minutes to conceal information from the public.
III. Then-Selectman Gillespie Deliberated Outside a Public Meeting via an October 9. 2018,
Email.
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be properly noticed 
and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
20(a)-(b), 21. In relevant part, a “‘meeting’ is defined as a deliberation by a public body with 
respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.” G.L. c. 30A, § 18. The law defines 
deliberation as:
[A]n oral or written communication through any medium, including electronic
mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within
its jurisdiction; provided, however, that “deliberation” shall not include the

distribution of a meeting agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other 
procedural meeting or the distribution of reports or documents that may be 
discussed at a meeting, provided that no opinion of a member is expressed.
G.L. c. 30A, § 18.
Although it is clear that certain administrative tasks are excluded from the definition of 
“deliberation,” that exclusion includes a strong caveat: such administrative communications are 
permissible “provided that no opinion of a member is expressed.” G.L. c. 30A, § 18; OML 2013- 
5. For example, the distribution of minutes ahead of a public meeting is permissible as long as 
the minutes simply memorialize the deliberation that took place at a preceding open meeting.
See OML 2013-127. We construe the administrative exclusion from the definition of deliberation 
narrowly and find that the inclusion of a public body member’s opinion within an otherwise 
administrative communication, will remove the communication from the protection of the 
administrative exclusion. See OML 2019-78. In addition, the expression of an opinion by one 
public body member on matters within the body’s jurisdiction to a quorum of a public body is 
considered a deliberation, even if no other public body member responds. See OML 2016-104; 
OML 2015-33; OML 2012-73.
Here, when Selectman Gillespie emailed the Assistant to the Town Manager on October 
9, 2018, and copied Board Chair Boshart, his communication reached a quorum of the Board.
In its entirety, the email stated:
Please just take any reference to 751 out of the minutes. Any mention by me was 
not for public consumption because it creates a conflict of interest for me. Bring 
revised copies to the meeting, or remove it from consent agenda
The Board argues that Selectman Gillespie’s email fell within the exemption to the 
definition of deliberation because his email merely “provided notice to the Chair that Selectman 
Gillespie might be requesting removal of an agenda-item for the meeting that night.” We 
disagree. Although the email may have served to inform the Chair that Selectman Gillespie 
might wish to remove the draft minutes from the agenda, which may have fit within the 
exclusion for administrative tasks, the email also expressed Selectman Gillespie’s opinion that 
the minutes were inaccurate and his desire that the substance of the minutes be changed. This 
email met the Open Meeting Law standard for deliberation because the email was “between or 
among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction.” We therefore 
find that selectman Gillespie’s email copying Chair Boshart constituted deliberation outside of a 
public meeting, in violation of the Open Meeting Law.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that: 1) the Board did not approve minutes for its 
February 13, 2018, executive session in a timely manner; 2) the Board’s February 13, 2018 
executive session minutes, as approved, were inaccurate; and 3) then-Selectman Gillespie 
deliberated outside a public meeting via an October 9, 2018 email.

Additionally, we find the Board’s approval of inaccurate executive session minutes to be 
intentional. We order immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and caution 
that similar violations in the future may result in the imposition of a civil penalty. G.L. c. 30A, § 
23(c)(4); 940 CMR 29.07(3)(d). In addition, we order each member of the Board to attend a 
comprehensive Open Meeting Law training, either by participating in one of the Division of 
Open Government’s monthly webinar trainings or by attending a training presented by the 
Board’s legal counsel. Each member of the Board shall certify to our office that they have done 
so within sixty (60) days of receipt of this letter.
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. Please be 
advised that this letter does not resolve any other complaints that may be pending with this office 
or with the Board. We invite the Board to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 to discuss training 
options.
