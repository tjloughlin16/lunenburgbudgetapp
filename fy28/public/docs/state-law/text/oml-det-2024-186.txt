This office received a complaint from Allen Waller on April 1, 2024, alleging that the 
Marblehead Board of Assessors (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about February 23, 2024.  The 
Board did not respond to the complaint.1  The complaint alleges that the Board failed to create 
sufficient minutes of meetings held on June 21, 2023; October 11, 2023; November 13, 2023; 
December 11, 2023; and December 18, 2023. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by creating 
insufficient minutes for the meetings held on October 11, 2023, December 11, 2023, and 
December 18, 2023.  We find that the Board did not violate the law with the respect to the 
remaining two sets of meeting minutes – June 21, 2023, and November 13, 2023.  In reaching 
this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review, and the notices and open session minutes of the Board meetings held 
on June 21, 2023; October 11, 2023; November 13, 2023; December 11, 2023; and December 
18, 2023.

The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.
2  By substantial compliance, we mean that the 
minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not 
attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See 
OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a 
transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion 
presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55.   
 
Specifically, the complaint alleges that the minutes of the meetings held on June 21, 
2023; October 11, 2023; November 13, 2023; December 11, 2023; and December 18, 2023, lack 
sufficient detail of the discussions that occurred during each meeting and do not include a list of 
documents and exhibits used during each meeting.   
 
The meeting held on June 21, 2023, lasted only 10 minutes and the Board simply signed 
an expe
nditure warrant and the “commitment for FY24 preliminary billing for Real Estate and 
Personal Property” and thereafter adjourned.  Since this meeting was brief and the minutes 
capture the substance of the discussion and actions of the Board, we find that the minutes of this 
meeting are sufficient and that a member of the public could read the minutes and have an 
understanding of what occurred during the meeting.  See OML 2023-35; OML 2021-151. 
 
During the meeting held on November 13, 2023, the Board first met in open session and 
then convened in executive session to discuss a litigation matter.  The Open Meeting Law 
permits a public body to enter executive session provided that it has first convened in open 
session, that a majority of the members of the body have voted to go into executive session, and 
that “the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes.”  See G.L. c. 
30A, § 2l (b); OML 2014-94.  The minutes reflect that the Chair asked for a motion to convene 
in executive session, announced that the Board planned to convene in executive session to 
discuss a litigation matter, identified the specific litigation matter, noted that discussion in open 
session would have a detrimental effect on the Board’s litigating position, and that the Board 
would not reconvene in open session.  The Board did not discuss any topics during the open 
session portion of this meeting but simply conducted the procedural requirements for convening 
in executive session.  Therefore, we find that the minutes contain sufficient information where 
they record the procedural requirements for entering executive session and thus provide a 
member of the public with a clear understanding of what occurred during the meeting.  However, 
we note that the minutes reflect the vote to convene in executive session as a “unanimous 
decision by the Board.”  The Open Meeting Law expressly requires not only that a roll call vote 
be conducted before entering executive session, but also that the roll call vote be “entered into 
the minutes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(2).  These requirements are intended to create a clear record

of what occurred and to reflect the significance of holding a discussion behind closed doors.  See 
OML 2015-131; OML 2014-94; OML 2012-10.  Even unanimous votes need to be recorded by 
roll call in the minutes.  See OML 2019-32; OML 2015-131; OML 2013-195.  A roll call vote is 
“the act or an instance of calling off a list of names.” Webster’s Third New International 
Dictionary of the English Language, 683 (unabridged ed. 1961).  It is unclear here whether the 
Board failed to vote by roll call to enter executive session, or voted by roll call but failed to 
properly record the vote in the minutes, and we therefore remind the Board of its obligations 
under the law.   
 
 The remaining three sets of meeting minutes - October 11, 2023, December 11, 2023, and 
December 18, 2023 - on the other hand, do not contain sufficient specificity.  The Board met for 
approximately one hour during each of these three meetings, but each set of minutes merely 
contains one short phrase for each topic stating that the Board discussed that particular topic 
without including any summary of what those discussions were.  The Open Meeting Law 
requires that meeting minutes include more than a statement that a public body held a discussion 
about a specified topic; the law requires that the minutes summarize the discussions that were 
held.  See OML 2019-167; OML 2018-8.  As such, we find that the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law where the minutes of the meetings held on October 11, 2023, December 11, 2023, 
and December 18, 2023, do not adequately capture the substance of the discussions that occurred 
so that a member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have 
an understanding of what occurred.  See OML 2022-70; OML 2020-90.   We order the Board to 
create minutes for these three meetings using any means available to it, including the notes and 
recollections of the attendees.  See OML 2020-53 (noting that, although “time ha[d] passed since 
[the] meeting occurred, the Board should add the requisite level of detail to the minutes to the 
best of its ability”); OML 2020-69 (ordering a school committee to create and approve minutes 
for meetings of a subcommittee held over a three-year period).  We acknowledge the potential 
infeasibility of accurately capturing the substance of the discussions that occurred almost one 
year ago, and we remind the Board that any minutes that are created must accurately capture 
what was actually discussed during the meetings.  See OML 2020-101; OML 2020-62. 
 
The complainant urges us to find that the Board’s violation was inte
 ntional.  See G.L. c. 
30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body or a member 
thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An intentional 
violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the law’s 
requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting Law. 
Id.  The Board was not previously warned that its actions ran afoul of the Open Meeting Law, 
and there is no evidence that the Board acted with deliberate ignorance.  Therefore, we decline to 
find an intentional violation.  See OML 2024-66. 
 
With respect to the allegation that the fiv e sets of minutes do not include a list of 
documents, we acknowledge that the five sets of minutes do not contain a list of documents or 
exhibits used during the meeting but we are unable to conclude that documents were, in fact, 
“used” during the meetings.  The Open Meeting Law does not define what it means for a 
document to be used at a meeting but, at a minimum, it is clear that where a document is 
physically present, verbally identified, and the contents are discussed by the members of a public 
body during an open meeting, it has been “used” for purposes of the Open Meeting Law.  See

OML 2014-12; OML 2012-42.  There is no indication that the Board used any documents prior 
to conve
ning in executive session during the November 13, 2023, meeting.  It is unclear from the 
minutes of the other four meetings whether documents were used during those meetings.  Video 
recordings of the meetings are not available and the complaint does not identify any particular 
documents that were used during the meetings but not listed in the minutes.  Therefore, we are 
unable to determine whether documents were “used” during the four meetings that should have 
been listed in the minutes.  Accordingly, we are constrained to find that the Board did not violate 
the Open Meeting Law with respect to this allegation.  See OML 2018-50.  We remind the Board 
of its obligations under the law, and we further clarify that it is insufficient to simply reference 
documents throughout the minutes or link to the documents; the minutes must contain a “a list of 
documents and other exhibits used at the meeting.”  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2024-148; 
OML 2019-29; OML 2018-70. 
 
We take this opportunity to advise the Board of the Attorney General’s recommendations 
as t
o best practices for drafting meeting minutes.  In particular, when summarizing discussions, 
we encourage public bodies to include dissenting or minority opinions whenever possible.  
Where significant debate on a matter occurs and it is clear that one or more members of the body 
and/or the public disagree with the decision ultimately taken by the public body, public bodies 
should note the opposing viewpoints in the meeting minutes.  Although it is not necessary to 
record the comments of every speaker, and we acknowledge the difficulty of doing so where 
there are many speakers whose remarks may even overlap, if a particular individual speaks at 
some length or is the only one to offer an argument for or against a proposal, that person and his 
or her comments should be identified in the minutes.  See OML 2014-108; OML 2013-47; OML 
2012-29.   
 
 Finally, although not clearly raised in the complaint, we remind the Board that the Open 
Mee
ting Law requires that the minutes of an open session meeting must be reviewed and 
approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” means “within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can 
show good cause for further delay.”  See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.  Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2014-15; 
OML 2012-91.  The Open Meeting Law does not govern the method for approving minutes.  As 
such, public bodies may choose the method for approving their minutes, including approval by 
the public body chair or another designee alone, by majority vote of the public body, or by 
consent of the body (approved unless there is an objection).  See OML 2023-204.  When a 
quorum of a public body approves minutes, however, it must do so during a noticed meeting, and 
the notice must include the specific meeting minutes to be approved.

For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing
 to create sufficient minutes of the meetings held on October 11, 2023, December 11, 
2023, and December 18, 2023.  We order the Board to revise, to the best of its abilities, these 
three sets of minutes and to certify to this office within sixty (60) days of the date of this letter 
that it has done so.  See 940 C.M.R. 29.07(4).  We also order the Board’s immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
 
We now cons
ider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
