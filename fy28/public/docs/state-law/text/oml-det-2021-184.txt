 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Harrington: 
 
This office received a complaint from Brian Cruise on October 22, 2021, alleging that the 
Carlisle Recreation Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Commission on September 21, 2021, 
and you responded, on behalf of the Commission, by letter dated October 8, 2021.  The 
complaint alleges that the notices for the Commission meetings held on July 14, 2021, August 
11, 2021, and September 20, 2021, lacked sufficient detail with respect to the executive session 
topics and that the Commission entered executive session on those dates for an improper purpose 
by discussing a monetary donation for creation of a pickleball facility.  
 
We resolve this matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Commission’s response to the complaint, the 
complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the notices and minutes of 
Commission meetings held on July 14, 2021, August 11, 2021, and September 20, 2021.  In 
addition, we communicated with you on November 3, 2021 and November 22, 2021.  Following 
our review, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law in the ways alleged 
with respect to the July 14, 2021, meeting only. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberation and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Commission of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session

is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  Public bodies may enter a closed, executive 
session for any of ten enumerated purposes, provided that the chair of the public body first 
announces in open session the purpose for the executive session, “stating all subjects that may be 
revealed without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called.”  See 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), 21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of 
Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for convening in 
executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification given the public that a 
[public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the 
reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  This level of detail about the executive session 
topic must also be included in the meeting notice.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); OML 2016-72.1  
 
The complaint alleges that the notices for Commission meetings held on July 14, 2021, 
August 11, 2021, and September 20, 2021, lacked sufficient detail because they did not 
adequately describe the topic to be discussed in executive session.  Here, the notices for all three 
meetings listed the following topic: “Executive Session – Donor Negotiations.”  With respect to 
the meetings held on August 11, 2021, and September 20, 2021, the Board did not convene in 
executive session on either date.  Therefore, because the Commission did not convene in 
executive session, we find no violation with respect to the sufficiency of the executive session 
topic listed on the notices.  When a public body does not actually deliberate on a noticed topic, 
the public body cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with respect to that topic, even if it would 
have been insufficiently detailed had the public body proceeded with its deliberation.2  See OML 
2019-61; OML 2015-63; OML 2014-81.  
 
With respect to the meeting held on July 14, 2021, the Commission convened in 
executive session to consider the value of real property, purportedly pursuant to Purpose 6.  See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6) (public body may consider the purchase, exchange, lease or value of real 
property if the chair declares that an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the 
negotiating position of the public body).  While a public body is not required to provide the 
specific statutory citation of the executive session it plans to convene under, the public body 
must at least provide a sufficient description of the executive session purpose in order to ensure 
that the topic falls within one of the permissible executive session purposes.  See OML 2016-95.  
Moreover, when noticing an executive session pursuant to Purpose 6, a public body must identify 
the property to be discussed if doing so would not compromise the purpose for which the 
executive session was called.  See OML 2013-97; OML 2012-52; OML 2010-2.  Here, the notice 
for the July 14, 2021, meeting did not reference executive session Purpose 6, did not indicate that 
the Commission would discuss real property, and did not include any information that identified 
the specific piece of property to be discussed.  As such, we find that the notice was insufficiently 
detailed with respect to the executive session topic.  See OML 2014-36.

Moreover, we find that the Commission’s discussions in executive session were not 
appropriate under Purpose 6.  Purpose 6, narrowly construed, permits a public body to meet in 
executive session to protect its negotiating position when seeking to purchase, exchange, or lease 
real estate, or to discuss the value of real estate where it has a negotiating position relative to that 
property.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6); Allen v. Board of Selectmen of Belmont, 58 Mass. App. 
Ct. 715, 719-721 (2003); OML 2019-143; OML 2016-50.  Here, the Board’s discussions in 
executive session pertained to the Town’s receipt of a monetary donation for the creation of a 
pickleball facility, the potential location of such a facility, the benefits of such a facility, and the 
creation of a design committee which would oversee fees, scheduling and rules for the facility.  
General discussions regarding real property or the use of real property do not fall within 
executive session Purpose 6.  See OML 2019-108.  We find that the Commission violated the 
Open Meeting Law by entering into executive session for an improper purpose on July 14, 2021. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to include sufficient information on its July 14, 2021, notice with respect to the 
executive session topic and convening in executive session for an improper purpose.  We order 
the Commission’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and we caution 
that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  Because 
the Commission has publicly released the minutes of the executive session meeting held on July 
14, 2021, we order no further remedial action. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Commission.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
