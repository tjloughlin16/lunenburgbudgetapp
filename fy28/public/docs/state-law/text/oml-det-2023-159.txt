 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Salas: 
 
This office received a complaint from John Cherry on August 14, 2023, alleging that the 
Boston Zoning Board of Appeals (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.1  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on July 14, and you responded, on 
behalf of the Board, by letter dated August 4.  In his complaint, Mr. Cherry alleges that the 
Board did not allow him to speak during a meeting held on July 11.  
 
We resolve this matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review, and the notice and video recording of the meeting held 
on July 11.
2  We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged. 
 
The Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body allow public participation in 
its meetings, but rather provides that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body 
without permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”  G.L. 
c. 30A § 20(f); see also OML 2021-27; OML 2020-161; OML 2017-189; OML 2014-23; OML 
2012-78.3  We do, however, encourage public bodies to allow for as much public participation as 
time and circumstances permit.  See OML 2015-12; OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59.

The Board met on July 11 and held numerous public hearings, one of which pertained to 
1, 2 and 3 Fourth Street Place which are three single-family homes attached to each other.  The 
hearing concerned the homeowners’ appeal of the denial of the Building Commissioner to build 
a third floor addition and new roof to their properties.  During the hearing, the Board heard from 
an attorney representing all three homeowners, the Boston Mayor’s Office of Neighborhood 
Services, representatives of two Boston City Councilors, a neighborhood association, and an 
abutter.  Mr. Cherry is also an abutter to the property and submitted written opposition to the 
project but also wished to speak during the July 11 meeting.  At the beginning of the hearing, the 
Board recognized Mr. Cherry to speak believing that he was the attorney for the property 
owners.  After realizing that he was not the attorney, Mr. Cherry was not allowed to speak at that 
time nor was he recognized later during the hearing and was thus unable to speak during the 
meeting. 
  
The Open Meeting Law permits the Chair of the Board to establish parameters for public 
participation.  See OML 2023-41; OML 2013-2; OML 2012-23.  Therefore, because it is within 
the Chair’s discretion to limit public participation during a meeting, the Chair did not violate the 
Open Meeting Law by not recognizing Mr. Cherry from addressing the Board during the July 11 
meeting.  See OML 2020-114; OML 2017-189; OML 2014-23; OML 2012-78.  We note that the 
Division of Open Government is charged specifically with reviewing complaints to determine 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25, thus we offer no opinion on 
whether any actions taken by the Board could be a violation of any other law or regulation 
pertaining to public hearings.  See OML 2014-31; OML Declination 10-30-20 (Billerica Zoning 
Board of Appeals); OML Declination 6-25-18 (Rowley Planning Commission). 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions.
