 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Chair Barclay: 
This office received a complaint from Gary Winitzer on March 18, 2021, alleging that the 
Royalston Building Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on February 14.1 On March 31, 
the Town Clerk forwarded to us your response on behalf of the Committee, which you had 
emailed to the Town Clerk on February 19. In his complaint, Mr. Winitzer alleges that the 
Committee 1) changed the time of a January 21 meeting without amending the meeting notice to 
advise the public of the change, and 2) only offered a long-distance phone conference line for the 
public to access the Committee’s January 21.  
We resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
and find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law in both respects. In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Committee’s response to the complaint, 
and the complainant’s request for further review. We also reviewed the meeting notice and 
meeting minutes of the Committee’s January 21 meeting.  
1. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to post a meeting notice with 
an accurate start time for the January 21 meeting. 
 The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Law provides that notice of every meeting must be 
posted “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal

holidays,” and that every notice shall include “the date, time and place of such meeting, and a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” G.L. c. 
30A, § 20(b). Public bodies must list the topics for discussion with “sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).   
 Here, the Committee concedes that the start time of the January 21 meeting, which was 
scheduled to take place at 10:00 a.m., was rescheduled to 1:00 p.m. to accommodate one of the 
Committee’s members. Due to “inadvertent” error, the Committee did not correct the meeting 
notice to reflect the new time. Mr. Winitzer called in to the meeting at 10:00 a.m. and was not 
able to join the later meeting as no notice had been provided to the public of the new time. The 
Open Meeting Law requires that a public body post notice of every meeting in its official notice 
posting location containing an accurate and clear start time. See OML 2016-97; OML 2013-161; 
OML 2012-59. As the Committee’s meeting notice contained a start time that was incorrect by a 
difference of three hours, we find that the Committee failed to post accurate notice of the January 
21 meeting at least 48 hours in advance, in violation of the Open Meeting Law.  
2. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to provide “adequate, 
alternative” means of access to the Committee’s January 21 remote meeting.  
 Meetings of a public body must be open to members of the public, unless an executive 
session is convened. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21. Under Governor Baker’s March 12, 2020, 
Executive Order suspending certain provisions of the Open Meeting Law, public bodies are 
permitted to meet entirely remotely, provided that the public body provides adequate, alternative 
means of public access to the deliberations of the public body.2 “Adequate, alternative means” 
may include, without limitation, providing public access through telephone, internet, or satellite 
enabled audio or video conferencing or any other technology that enables the public to clearly 
follow the proceedings of the public body in real time.3 Additionally, “[a] public body must offer 
its selected alternative means of access to its proceedings without subscription, toll, or similar 
charge to the public.” Id. 
 Here, the Committee offered remote public access to its January 21 meeting only through 
a conference line with a 701 area code, which is neither a local phone number, nor is it toll-free. 
The use of the long-distance telephone number would result in long-distance toll charges for 
members of the public whose phone service did not include unlimited long-distance calling. 
Thus, the Committee failed to provide “adequate, alternative” means of access “without 
subscription, toll, or similar charge to the public.” See OML 2020-111. Therefore, we find that 
the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law.

CONCLUSION 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to post a meeting notice with an accurate start time for the January 21 meeting and by 
failing to provide adequate alternative means of access to the January 21 remote meeting. We 
order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and 
caution the Committee that a determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may 
be considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law. 
 Additionally, to remedy the lack of notice to the public, we order the Committee, within 
30 days of receipt of this determination, to ratify the votes to approve the November 25, 2020, 
and December 17, 2020, meeting minutes at a properly posted future meeting.  
 We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any 
questions.
