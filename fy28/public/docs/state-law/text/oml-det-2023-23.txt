 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Austin: 
 
This office received a complaint from Mary Wawro on July 3, 2023, alleging that the 
Nantucket Select Board (the “Board”) and Nantucket Conservation Commission (the 
“Commission”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was 
originally filed with the Board and Commission on or about March 30, 2023, and you responded 
to the complaint, on behalf of the Board and Commission, by letter dated May 15, 2023, 
following an extension of time granted by this office.  The complaint alleges that the Board and 
Commission posted an insufficiently specific notice for a joint meeting held on March 21, 2023; 
failed to post documents that corresponded with the topic listed on the March 21, 2023, meeting 
notice; and portions of the March 21, 2023, meeting were inaccessible.  
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter.  Following our 
review, we find that the Board and Commission did not violate the Open Meeting Law in the 
ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the response 
to the complaint, the complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the notice and 
minutes of the joint meeting held on March 21, 2023, as well as a video recording of that 
meeting.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Board is a five-member public body; thus, three 
members constitute a quorum.  The Commission is a seven-member public body; thus, four 
members constitute a quorum.  
 
The Board duly posted notice of a meeting to be held on March 21, 2023, at 5:00 P.M.  
The notice included the following topic: Joint Meeting with Conservation Commission to 
Discuss Sconset Bluff Erosion Control Measures.  The notice listed the location of the meeting 
as PSF Training Room at 4 Fairgrounds Road in Nantucket and via Zoom.  The notice provided 
information necessary to access the virtual meeting, including a link to the Zoom meeting, the 
Webinar ID number, and pass code required to log into the meeting.   
 
The Board and the Commission met jointly on March 21, 2023, and all public body 
members were present at the PSF Training Room, the physical meeting location; the meeting 
was also broadcast via Zoom.  The meeting was conducted by a facilitator from Arcadis, a 
design, engineering and management consulting company, who asked the Board and 
Commission to review three different Sconset Bluff erosion control scenarios.  The three 
scenarios were: 1) Leave geotubes in place and fund privately; 2) Leave geotubes in place for a 
defined period of time; and 3) Remove geotubes and allow nature to take its course.  In 2014, the 
Town approved the installation of a 900-foot geotube along Sconset Bluff to protect it from 
coastal erosion. 
 
The three erosion control scenarios were taped to the wall in three different locations in 
the PSF Training Room.  The Board and Commission broke out into three groups with each 
group containing less than a quorum of Board and Commission members.  For approximately 45 
minutes, each group walked around the room, spent about 15 minutes at each scenario, discussed 
the pros and cons of each scenario, and each Board and Commission member wrote his or her 
thoughts on post-it notes and placed the notes on each scenario.  Board and Commission 
members then returned to their seats and discussed the steps previously taken to control beach 
erosion along Sconset Bluff, ways to move forward, which public body has the authority to make 
a decision on erosion control measures along Sconset Bluff, and the possible use of private funds 
to support erosion control measures. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice 48 hours in advance of a 
meeting and include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the 
chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public 
bodies are required to list topics in a meeting notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably 
advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We 
generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could 
read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML

2018-7; OML 2015-35.2  A public body need not list each and every detail about a topic in order 
to comply with the Law’s requirements.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134.   
 
The complaint alleges that the notice for the joint meeting held on March 21, 2023, was 
insufficient where it did not indicate that the discussions would include scenario planning led by 
a facilitator.  While we acknowledge that the notice did not specifically indicate that the Board 
and Commission would discuss three different scenarios, the Open Meeting Law does not require 
a public body to list all tangential topics related to a discussion nor does the law require a public 
body to anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only that it identify 
the discussion topic with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2015-73; OML 
2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38.  We are satisfied that the topic as listed on the notice 
here was sufficient to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting, 
that is, ways to control the beach erosion along Sconset Bluff, and are further satisfied that the 
discussions involving the three scenarios that occurred during the meeting actually pertained to 
erosion control measures along Sconset Bluff.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); see also OML 2023-
114; OML 2013-152.   
 
In addition, the Board and Commission were not required to post attachments pertaining 
to the topic listed on the notice.  The Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body 
attach supporting documents to a meeting notice or that it publicly post a “meeting packet” that 
contains such documents.  See OML 2022-24; OML 2022-19; OML 2021-181.  To the contrary, 
the meeting notice itself must contain all information that the Open Meeting Law requires; 
posting attachments does not cure an otherwise defective notice.  See OML 2021-138; OML 
2021-57; OML 2019-22.  Moreover, there is no requirement in the Open Meeting Law that the 
public body affirmatively provide copies of documents to persons in attendance at a meeting.  
But see G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c) (minutes of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or 
in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 days); G.L. c. 66, § 
10 (Public Records Law requires Board to permit inspection or provide copy of any public 
record, such as documents used during a meeting, within 10 business days of request).  We 
encourage public bodies, whenever feasible, to provide meeting attendees with copies of all 
documents it intends to discuss.  Particularly where viewing such documents would be helpful to 
following the conversation during the meeting, providing copies of documents that the Board 
intends to discuss promotes transparency and public access to government deliberations.  See 
OML 2016-94. 
 
The complaint also alleges that portions of the joint meeting held on March 21, 2023, 
were inaccessible.  Specifically, the complaint alleges that the discussions that occurred between 
the Board and Commission members when they broke out into separate groups to discuss each of 
the three scenarios were not audible to those in attendance.  The Open Meeting Law was enacted 
“to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding deliberation and decisions on which public policy 
is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Council of Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law 
requires that meetings of a public body be noticed and open to the public, unless an executive 
session is convened.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  Access to a meeting must include the

opportunity to be physically present, as well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the 
members of the public body.  See OML 2017-135; OML 2015-38; OML 2014-3; OML 2013-
189.  For purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a 
deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 18.  The Law defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any 
medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public 
business within its jurisdiction.”  Id.  A “quorum” is a simple majority of the members of a 
public body.  Id.    
 
We acknowledge that when the Board and Commission broke into groups to discuss each 
of the three scenario plans during the March 21, 2023, meeting, certain of the discussions among 
those groups were difficult to follow or inaudible.  However, we do not find a violation of the 
Open Meeting Law here because none of the three groups contained a quorum of Board or 
Commission members.  The Board or Commission engages in deliberation only when a quorum 
of each communicates on public business within the Board’s or Commission’s jurisdiction.  The 
discussions among the three separate groups during the breakout sessions occurred with less than 
a quorum of Board or Commission members present.  Because less than a quorum of members 
participated in that portion of the discussions, the Board and Commission did not deliberate and 
the small-group discussions did not constitute a “meeting” for the purposes of the Open Meeting 
Law.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 18; OML 2016-99; OML 2013-106.   
 
With respect to the concern raised in the complaint that the joint meeting was not 
broadcast via YouTube, we do not find a violation.  Here, the joint meeting held on March 21, 
2021, allowed individuals to attend the meeting in-person at a location that was physically open 
and accessible, as well as attend remotely via Zoom, which qualifies as an adequate alternative 
means for the public to attend remote meetings.  See OML 2021-10; OML 2020-111; Chapter 20 
of the Acts of 2021, § 20(b).  As the Board and Commission are not required to provide more 
than one means of adequate, alternative access, we find that the Board and Commission did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law where it provided both in-person access and access through the 
web-based Zoom platform, without also providing access via YouTube.  See OML 2022-124; 
OML 2021-91. 
 
Finally, although not specifically raised in the complaint, we note our concern with the 
use of post-it notes to convey Board and Commission members’ thoughts on the three erosion 
control scenarios.  Any communication among a quorum of a public body on matters that are 
pending or will come before it and within the public body’s jurisdiction is considered 
deliberation.  See OML 2015-3; OML 2014-108; OML 2013-136; Boelter v. Board of Selectmen 
of Wayland, 479 Mass. 233, 243 (2018).  Moreover, the expression of an opinion of one public 
body member on matters within the body’s jurisdiction to a quorum of a public body is a 
deliberation, even if no other public body member responds.  See OML 2016-104; OML 2015-
33.  Although each of the three breakout groups contained less than a quorum of Board or 
Commission members, the opinions shared via the post-it notes likely reached a quorum of 
Board or Commission members in a serial manner as the groups rotated between the three 
scenarios.  A public body may not engage in a serial communication whereby a quorum 
communicates in a non-contemporaneous manner outside of a meeting on a particular subject 
matter within the public body’s jurisdiction.  See OML 2015-3; OML 2012-84; OML 2011-27; 
McCrea v. Flaherty, 71 Mass. App. Ct. 637 (2008) (holding that private serial communications

violate the spirit of the Open Meeting Law and may not be used to circumvent the intent of the 
Law).  And the sharing of these thoughts and opinions, even if not attributed to specific 
members, is considered deliberation as the opinions pertained to a matter of public business 
within both the Board’s and Commission’s jurisdiction, that is, erosion control measures along 
Sconset Bluff.  See Boelter, 479 Mass. 233, 243 (2018) (“the effect of the circulation of the 
individual and composite evaluations was that all five board members were aware of the 
opinions of four of the members in advance of the open meeting; thus, the circulation, in effect, 
constituted a deliberation, or a meeting, to which the public did not have access”); see also OML 
2022-200.  We remind the Board and Commission of their obligations under the Open Meeting 
Law. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board and Commission did not violate the 
Open Meeting Law as alleged.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination 
to be resolved.  This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending 
with our office or the Board or the Commission.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 
963-2540 if you have any questions regarding this letter.
