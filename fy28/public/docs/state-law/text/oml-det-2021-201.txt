 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Goldberg: 
This office received a complaint from Michael Harrity on June 21, 2021, alleging that the 
Weston Planning Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. 
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on May 17, and you responded on behalf of 
the Board by letter dated June 7.1 In his complaint, Mr. Harrity alleges that, at a May 12 meeting, 
the Board discussed a Town meeting warrant article that was not included in the meeting notice’s 
list of warrant articles to be discussed.2  
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law. In 
reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the 
complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review, including copies of emails sent by 
the Board’s Chair around the time of the meeting. We also reviewed the notice, minutes and 
video recording of the May 12 meeting. We emailed with Mr. Harrity on several occasions from

2 Mr. Harrity also alleges that the Board did not allow proponents of article 28 to speak at the May 12 meeting; did 
not have authority to vote on article 28; and took topics out of order at the May 12 meeting. We note that, even if

Meeting Law does not require that a public body allow public  participation during its meetings, but rather provides 
that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body without permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at 
the request of the chair, be silent.” G.L. c. 30A § 20(f). The Open Meeting Law, therefore, permits the chair of a 
public body to decide whether to allow for public participation, and if so, to set limitations on participation. See 
OML 2017-189; OML 2014-23; OML 2012-23. We do, however, encourage public bodies to allow for as much 
public participation as time and circumstances permit. See OML 2015-12; OML 2014-129. The Law also does not 
prohibit a public body from removing, postponing, or declining to discuss a topic listed on a meeting notice or 
taking topics in a different order than listed on the notice. See OML 2014-35.

September through December of 2021. Finally, we questioned the Board regarding what the 
Chair anticipated in advance of the May 12 meeting. The Board submitted two supplemental 
response letters, dated November 18 and December 17, in response to this questioning. 
 Mr. Harrity alleges that the Board’s meeting notice only listed Town Meeting warrant 
articles 30, 31, 32 and 33 for discussion at the May 12 meeting, but the Board also discussed 
article 28, which was not listed. When questioned, the Board’s Chair informed us that she was 
not able to recall whether she anticipated 48 hours in advance that the Board would specifically 
discuss article 28 at the May 12 meeting. However, the Board asserts, based on the fact that the 
Board specially listed the other four articles, that article 28 would have been listed if the Chair 
anticipated it would also be discussed at this particular meeting.  
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at least 
48 hours prior to such meeting” and that every notice include “the date, time and place of such 
meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the 
meeting.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). A public body may discuss a truly unanticipated topic at a 
meeting, even if it was not listed on the meeting notice, although the Attorney General strongly 
recommends that public bodies postpone discussion and action on unanticipated topics that are 
controversial or may be of particular interest to the public if the topic can wait until a subsequent 
meeting. See OML 2020-172; OML 2019-131; OML 2018-119.3 
  The Open Meeting Law requires that meeting notices include a listing of topics that the 
chair reasonably anticipates 48 hours in advance will be discussed at the meeting. See OML 
2021-121. We have previously found that when a topic was directly brought to a Chair’s 
attention more than 48 hours prior to the meeting, and was something that clearly required action 
or response in short order, it was “reasonably” anticipated that the topic would be discussed at 
the next meeting. See OML 2021-146.  
 On Friday, May 7, at 10:30 a.m. one of the co-chairs of the Weston Historical 
Commission emailed members of several different town public bodies, including the Board’s 
Chair, expressing concern about what she believed was the ambiguous wording of article 28, 
which sought to require a private landowner to maintain a historic stone wall on private property. 
The Historical Commission co-chair expressed her opinion that article 28 needed to be fully 
vetted by the Historical Commission and the Board. The notice for the Board’s May 12 meeting 
was posted on Friday, May 7 at 3:41 p.m. At 8:06 p.m. the same day, which was still more than 
48 hours prior to the Board’s May 12 meeting, not including the weekend, the Chair responded 
to the email regarding article 28, stating that article 28 should be discussed before Town 
Meeting, which was scheduled to take place the following Saturday, May 15. On May 12, the 
Chair again wrote to the group, saying that the Board would discuss article 28 at that evening’s 
Board meeting.  
 Based on this sequence of events, we find that as of Friday evening, May 7, the Chair was 
aware of the need to discuss article 28 during the May 12 meeting, and article 28 was a 
“reasonably anticipated” topic for discussion. Even though the notice for the May 12 meeting 
had already been posted by that time, as it was still more than 48 hours prior to the May 12

meeting, not including weekends, the meeting notice should have been amended on Monday, 
May 10, to add article 28 as a topic for discussion. See OML 2021-142. Therefore, we find that 
the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it discussed article 28 during the May 12 
meeting, as the topic was reasonably anticipated yet not included on the Board’s meeting notice.  
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
discussing an anticipated topic which was not included on the meeting notice. We order the 
Board’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution the Board 
that a determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be considered evidence 
of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law. As warrant article 28 failed to pass at Weston’s May 
15 Town Meeting, we order no further relief.  
 We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any questions.
