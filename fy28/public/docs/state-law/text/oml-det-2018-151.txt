RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints
Dear Attorney Mead:
This office received four related complaints from Allen Waller alleging that the 
Marblehead Board of Health (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18- 
25. The first and second complaints were filed with the Board on December 11, 2017, and the 
Board responded to both by letter dated December 13, 2017. This first complaint was then filed 
with our office on January 16, 2018, and the second complaint was filed with our office on 
January 26, 2018. The third and fourth complaints were filed with the Board on December 15, 
2017, and the Board responded to both by letter on January7 11,2018. This third complaint was 
filed with our office on February 16, 2018, and the fourth complaint was filed with our office on 
March 9, 2018. In his complaints, Mr. Waller alleges that the Board violated the Open Meeting 
Law by: (i) failing to follow proper procedure before convening in executive session; (ii) 
convening in executive session without a proper statutory purpose; (iii) failing to include an 
anticipated topic on a meeting notice; and (iv) creating insufficiently detailed meeting minutes.
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed these complaints. Following 
our review, we find that the Board created insufficiently detailed minutes of the November 14, 
2017 meeting. We find that certain other allegations involving meeting minutes are untimely. 
Finally, we find that the Board did not violate the law in the other ways alleged. In reaching a 
determination, we reviewed the original complaints, the Board’s responses, and the requests for 
further review filed with our office. We reviewed, in camera, the minutes of executive sessions 
held by the Board on September 14, 2016; March 8, 2017; and August 17, 2017. We also 
reviewed supplementary e-mails from the Board’s legal counsel dated March 9 and 22, 2018.
FACTS
We find the facts as follows. The Board held a meeting on February 2.3, 2016. The 
minutes of this meeting state that the Board voted to approve several “change orders,” but the 
minutes do not provide additional detail about what these orders entail. The minutes of this 
meeting were approved on March 8, 2016.

The Board held meetings on September 14, 2016; March 8, 2017; and August 16, 2017. 
During these three meetings, the Board entered executive session. The original open session 
minutes of these meetings did not reflect statements from the chair as to why the executive 
sessions were held or the Board’s vote prior to entering executive session. The minutes of these 
executive sessions have not yet been released, and we therefore do not recount their contents in 
detail. We note, generally, that the Board discussed potential litigation involving a dispute with 
an engineer and a contractor hired to do work on a landfill in town.
On October 21, 2017, the complainant sent a letter to the Board requesting that a 
discussion be added to an upcoming meeting agenda concerning the closure of a transfer station. 
On November 6, 2017, the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on November 14, 2017. 
This notice did not list the requested discussion concerning the transfer station. The Board held 
the November 14, 2017 meeting as planned. The complainant was present during this meeting 
and requested to address the Board. The complainant describes his interaction with the Board as 
“45 minutes of debate as to the status of the landfill closure.” The Board describes this 
interaction as “45 minutes of repeated attempts by the Board and [its] counsel to explain to Mr. 
Waller why the Board would not be discussing his correspondence in open session.” The 
November 14, 2017 meeting minutes summarized the interaction as follows: “Allen Waller... 
came before the Board asking about the landfill closure and transfer station project.”
DISCUSSION
I. The September 14. 2016, March 8. 2017. and August 16. 2017 Minutes Lack
Required Detail. However, the Board Followed Proper Procedure Prior to
Entering Executive Session.
A public body may enter executive session for any of ten enumerated purposes. See G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21 (a). Prior to convening the executive session, certain procedural steps must take 
place. The chair must state all subjects that may be discussed during the executive session, 
without compromising the lawful purpose for secrecy; and a majority of the public body 
members must vote to enter executive session, with that voted reflected by roll call in the 
meeting minutes. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b).
Here, the complaints allege that the Board failed to take a vote prior to entering executive 
session on September 14, 2016, March 8, 2017, and August 16, 2017. Further, the complaints 
allege that the chair did not state all subjects to be discussed in each executive session. The 
Board’s response states that the Board took the required vote prior to the executive session. 
Absent sufficient evidence to the contrary, we credit a public body’s version of the facts. See 
OML 2017-169.1 We therefore credit the Board’s version of the facts and find that the required 
vote was taken. However, such procedural steps must be included in the minutes of each 
meeting. Because the minutes at issue did not include this required information, we find that 
they were not sufficiently detailed to satisfy the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2017-180. The 
Board stated to our office that the minutes have now been revised to include this missing detail. 
We commend the Board for taking these remedial steps.

II. The Board Convened in Executive Session for a Proper Statutory Purpose on the
Three Dates at Issue.
One appropriate purpose for an executive session is “[t]o discuss strategy with respect to 
collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the 
bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.” G.L. c. 30A, § 
21(a)(3) (Purpose 3). To convene an executive session under Purpose 3, a public body must 
have a bargaining or litigating position to protect. See OML 2012-116.
Here, the complaints allege that the Board entered executive session on September 14, 
2016, March 8, 2017, and August 16, 2017 without a proper statutory purpose. Specifically, the 
complaints contend that the Board’s reliance on Purpose 3 was improper because no litigation 
was ongoing at the time. We have stated that public bodies may enter executive session under 
Purpose 3 to discuss the merits of bringing affirmative litigation. See OML 2017-176; OML 
2015-121. Our review of the executive session minutes shows that the Board discussed several 
situations that could have led to litigation. Indeed, the Board’s legal counsel informed our office 
that, after the present complaints were filed, litigation commenced between the town and a 
contractor.2 Because the Board’s discussions of litigation strategy were appropriate for 
executive session, we find no violation with respect to this allegation.3
III. The Board’s Did Not Violate the Open Meeting Law by Failing to Include the 
Complainant’s Letter on the November 14, 2017 Meeting Notice. However, the
Minutes of that Meeting Lack Sufficient Detail.
Public bodies must post notice of all meetings, including “a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Topics must be 
listed with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting. 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).
The complaints allege that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
include the complainant’s October 21, 2017 letter on the notice of the November 14, 2017 
meeting. The Board’s response makes clear that, despite having received the complainant’s 
letter, the chair did not plan on discussing it during the November 14, 2017 meeting. The Open 
Meeting Law does not require a public body to discuss any particular topic. See OML 2015-73. 
Because the chair did not anticipate discussing the complainant’s letter, it was not required to 
appear on the meeting notice, and we find no violation with respect to this allegation.
However, we find the Board’s November 14, 2017 meeting minutes do not meet the 
requirements of the Open Meeting Law. The complaints contend that the distillation of a 45- 
minute discussion into one sentence violated the Open Meeting Law. We agree. The 
complainant contends that the meeting discussion involved “repeated questioning” concerning a

landfill closure. The Board, in turn, contends that 45 minutes was spent explaining to the 
complainant why it would not address these topics. In any event, both parties agree that the 
discussion at issue continued for approximately 45 minutes. Using a single sentence to 
summarize a significant portion of the meeting does not comply with the Open Meeting Law’s 
requirements. See OML 2013-14. We conclude that a reasonable member of the public reading 
the November 14, 2017 meeting minutes would not understand the nature of the conversation 
that took place. As such, we find that the Board failed to create sufficiently detailed minutes in 
this instance.
IV. Allegations Concerning Other Meeting Minutes are Untimely.
The complaints allege that the Board consistently failed to include sufficient detail in its 
meeting minutes when discussing construction change orders. As evidence, the complainant 
points to the minutes of the February 23, 2016 meeting. We do not perform broad audits of 
public bodies, see OML 107-154, so we consider only the February 23, 2016 minutes identified 
by the complainant. The Open Meeting Law states that complaints must be filed with the public 
body within 30 days of the alleged violation. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b). Any complaints regarding 
the timely creation, approval, sufficiency, or accuracy of open session minutes must be filed 
within 30 days of the meeting during which they were approved. See OML Declination 9-4- 
2012 (Manchester-by-the-Sea Zoning Board of Appeals). Because the present allegation was 
made more than thirty days after the minutes at issue were approved, we find that it is untimely 
and we decline to review it.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board created insufficiently detailed 
meeting minutes. We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance, and we caution that 
similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law. Because we 
understand that the Board has already amended the September 14, 2016, March 8, 2017, and 
August 16, 2017 minutes, we order no further relief with respect to those minutes. We order the 
Board to amend the November 14, 2017 minutes to provide the missing detail. This remedial 
action should occur within thirty (30) days of receipt of this letter, and the Board should certify 
compliance with this order to our office in writing, pursuant to 940 CMR 29.07(4).
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions.
