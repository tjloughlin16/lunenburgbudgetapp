 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Ms. Trierweiler: 
This office received a complaint from Christine Potts on June 8, 2021, alleging that the 
Medfield Permanent Planning and Building Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on 
April 5, and you responded on behalf of the Committee by letter dated May 19.1, 2, 3 In her 
complaint, Ms. Potts alleges that 1) the Committee failed to timely approve meeting minutes for 
all meetings that took place in 2018, and 2) the Committee failed to respond to a request for 
meeting minutes within 10 days.  
Following our review, we resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 
940 CMR 29.07(2)(a) and find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law as alleged. 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate minutes of all 
meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members 
present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other 
exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including 
the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting minutes must be reviewed and 
approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). The Open Meeting Law itself does not define 
“timely manner.” However, the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations provide that 
“timely manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, 
whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.” 940 C.M.R. 
29.1 (2).

The Open Meeting Law requires that minutes of an open session, if they exist and 
whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 
days. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). Under the Open Meeting Law, a public body has 10 calendar days 
from the date a request is received to provide a response. A public body is only required to 
produce any meeting minutes, whether in draft or approved form, which existed at the time of a 
request. Handwritten notes, depending on the condition they are in, may be considered a draft of 
the minutes that need to be produced, and “materials used in the preparation of minutes” are 
public records. See OML 2018-117; OML 2013-131; OML 2013-64; OML 2013-31. However, a 
public body is not required to produce meeting minutes in response to a request that do not 
already exist. See OML 2020-151. If minutes do not yet exist at the time of a request, the public 
body is still required to respond to the request within 10 calendar days with an explanation of 
whether the minutes do or do not exist in either approved or draft form. See OML 2021-46.  
The Committee concedes that it did not approve meeting minutes for any meetings that 
took place in 2018, prior to Ms. Potts filing her complaint on April 5, 2021. The Committee 
relied on a Town staff member to draft all Committee meeting minutes in late 2017 and 2018. 
However, the staff member fell behind and was not able to complete the meeting minutes before 
his retirement in January of 2019. We understand the demands on a volunteer public body with 
limited staff, however the public body is still obligated to create and approve minutes of its 
meetings in a timely manner. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2015-86.4 If staff resources are not 
available to create minutes for a public body to approve, then public body members must take on 
this responsibility to ensure compliance with the Law. See OML 2017-115. Here, the 
Committee’s members were responsible for creating and approving all meeting minutes within 
30 days or three meetings, whichever was later. As the Committee failed to approve meeting 
minutes for more than a year, beginning in late 2017, we find that the Committee failed to 
approve meeting minutes in a timely manner for all meetings which took place in 2018.  
Finally, we find that the Committee failed to timely respond to a request for meeting 
minutes. On February 24 at 2:49 p.m. the Town Administrator emailed Ms. Potts that she was 
still searching for the Committee’s 2017 meeting minutes, which Ms. Potts had previously 
requested. A few minutes later, Ms. Potts sent a new request to the Committee’s Chair requesting 
all of the Committee’s 2018 meeting minutes. Ms. Potts did not receive a response from the 
Committee to the new February 24 request until March 22, when the Town Administrator 
emailed Ms. Potts to inform her that the Town Administrator had found the early-2017 minutes, 
but still could not locate the late-2017 or 2018 meeting minutes. As the Committee did not 
respond to Ms. Potts’ February 24 request for minutes within 10 calendar days, we find that the 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law.  
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by 1) failing to timely approve meeting minutes for all meetings that took place in 2018, and 2) 
failing to respond to a request for meeting minutes within 10 calendar days. We order the 
Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution the 
Committee that a determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law.
