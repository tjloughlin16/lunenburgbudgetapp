DISCUSSION I. The Board Properly Met in Executive Session on February 5, February 19, September 24 and October 15 Under Purpose 3.  The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an open session, with some exceptions. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). One permissible reason to convene in executive session is “to discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”). This purpose offers the narrow opportunity to discuss strategy with respect to litigation that is pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise demonstrably likely; the mere possibility of litigation is not sufficient to invoke Purpose 3. See Doherty v. School Committee of Boston, 386 Mass. 643, 648 (1982); Ferryman v. School Committee of Boston, 17 Mass. App. Ct. 346, 352 (1983); OML 2012-05.8 The public body invoking this executive session purpose must have a bargaining or litigating position to protect, and must declare in open session that having the discussion in open session may have a detrimental effect on that position. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3); OML 2011-47. To fall within Purpose 3, the discussion must relate specifically to discussions of strategy; general discussions regarding bargaining or litigation that do not relate to strategy do not fall within the limited scope of Purpose 3. Purpose 3 covers discussions of litigation strategy for cases pending in a court of law, as well as administrative proceedings or post-judgment discussions concerning whether to appeal a decision. See OML 2017-9; OML 2012-116. In particular, we have previously stated that MCAD adjudicatory hearing procedures closely resemble traditional litigation,9 and therefore matters before MCAD constitute pending litigation. See OML 2015-13; OML 2012-81. Here, Mr. Coulombe’s attorney informed Attorney Taylor that: “[t]he Chief wants to explore his options regarding MCAD and Civil Rights complaints.” Therefore, as a threshold matter, we find that the Chief’s referenced MCAD and civil rights complaints constituted “litigation” for purposes of the Open Meeting Law’s executive session Purpose 3.   Next we must determine whether the referenced litigation was “pending or clearly and imminently threatened or otherwise demonstrably likely.” A written communication that either explicitly or implicitly threatens litigation may provide sufficient basis for a public body to reasonably believe that litigation is imminently threatened. See OML 2016-6; OML 2012-76; OML 2012-05. Here, the Board believed that the litigation it sought to discuss in executive session was imminently threatened. This belief was reasonable based on the January 29 “litigation hold” email explicitly stating that the Chief was considering MCAD or civil rights claims. We Find No Evidence that The Board Deliberated Over Email.   The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21. In relevant part, a “‘meeting’ is defined as a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.” G.L. c. 30A, § 18. The Law defines deliberation as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction[.]” G.L. c. 30A, § 18 (emphasis added). The expression of an opinion by one public body member on matters within the body’s jurisdiction to a quorum of a public body is considered a deliberation, even if no other public body member responds. See OML 2016-104; OML 2015-33; OML 2012-73.  A public body may not engage in a serial communication whereby a quorum communicates in a non-contemporaneous manner outside of a meeting on a particular matter within the public body’s jurisdiction. See OML 2015-3; McCrea v. Flaherty, 71 Mass. App. Ct. 637 (2008) (holding that private serial communications violate the spirit of the Open Meeting Law and may not be used to circumvent the intent of the Law). A public body also may not use a non-member, such as a staff member, to communicate on matters that the Board would otherwise save for discussion at an open meeting. See District Attorney for the Northern District v. School Committee of Wayland, 451 Mass. 561, 570-571 (2009) (“Governmental bodies may not circumvent the requirements of the Open Meeting Law by conducting deliberations via private messages, whether electronically, in person, over the telephone, or in any other form.”)  The complaint alleges that the Board deliberated over email about matters within its jurisdiction. As evidence, the complainant shared with us many emails which were originally sent from October 2018 through February 2019 which Mr. Coulombe received from the Board through a public records request. We reviewed these emails and did not find any evidence of deliberation outside of a meeting, because while some Board members expressed opinions on matters before the Board in the emails, none of these emails were sent to a quorum of the Board. Rather, these emails were sent to the Town Manager alone, and therefore do not constitute deliberation, as the Town Manager is not a member of the Board. See OML Declination 2-24-20 (Duxbury Planning Board). For the same reason, we find that any emails from the Town Manager to the Board do not constitute improper deliberation. See OML 2014-80.   We also find that the Board did not engage in non-contemporaneous communications among a quorum of Board members on a topic within the Board’s jurisdiction. The Open Meeting Law does not permit public body members to engage in serial deliberations through a staff member to circumvent the prohibition on deliberation outside of a meeting. See OML 2018-49. During our review, we noted that in several emails from the Town Manager to Town Counsel, the Town Manager appeared to ask questions on behalf of the entire Board. For example, some of the Town Manager’s emails included statements such as “the Selectmen ask…” or “I have heard some concern from members of the Selectmen[.]” These emails appeared to relay questions or concerns from multiple Board members, suggesting that Board members may have deliberated outside of a public meeting and communicated collective

IV. The Board Permissibly Amended its September 24 Meeting Notice to Add an 
Unanticipated Topic.  
 Except in an emergency, the Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of 
every meeting “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal 
holidays.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). “ Notice shall ... contain the date, time and place of such meeting 
and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” Id. 
The Open Meeting Law does not require the chair of a public body to guess what will occur at a 
meeting. See OML 2018-119; OML 2013-197. A public body may amend a notice within 48 
hours of a meeting if a previously unanticipated topic comes to the Chair’s attention. See 940 
CMR 29.03(1)(d); OML 2020-29; OML 2019-131. Although a Chair generally may be aware 
that a discussion is ongoing, the Open Meeting Law only requires the meeting notice to contain 
the topics the Chair reasonably anticipates discussing at the particular meeting for which the 
notice is posted. See OML 2018-159. When amending a meeting notice within the 48 hours prior 
to a meeting, a public body must conspicuously record the date and time that the meeting notice 
is amended, as well as the date and time the notice was initially posted, on or with the amended 
notice. See 940 CMR 29.03(l)(d).

V. The Board’s Minutes for the Second February 19 Executive Session Are Insufficiently Detailed.  Although not alleged in the complaints, because the executive session minutes had not yet been provided to Mr. Coulombe, we also find that the Board’s meeting minutes for the February 19 executive session which began at approximately 8:05 p.m. were insufficiently detailed. The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to “create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a) (emphasis added).  When reviewing minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy requirement. See OML 2017-104; OML 2013-64. By substantial compliance, we mean that the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred. See OML 2019-145; OML 2012-106. Although a transcript of the discussions is not required, and the minutes need not include every remark or opinion presented, minutes must include a substantive summary of the discussion on each topic. See OML 2020-18; OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55. Minutes of executive sessions must include the same level of detail as is required for open sessions. See OML 2018-26; OML 2014-43. Here, the minutes of the Board’s 8:05 p.m. executive session at its February 19 meeting do not provide sufficient detail regarding each topic discussed. Despite the executive session having lasted more than an hour, the minutes merely consist of a list of motions made, votes taken, and, at some points, a mention that a discussion occurred, without summarizing the discussion.17 The Open Meeting Law requires that meeting minutes include more than a statement that a public body held a discussion about a specified topic; the Law requires that the minutes summarize the substance of the public body’s discussion. record the comments of every speaker, and we acknowledge the difficulty of doing so where 
there are many speakers whose remarks may even overlap, if a particular individual speaks at 
some length or is the only one to offer an argument for or against a proposal, that person and his 
or her comments should be identified in the minutes. See OML 2019-167; OML 2012-29.  
CONCLUSION 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
approving insufficiently detailed minutes for a February 19 executive session. In addition, the 
Board’s February 5 and 19 meeting notices and announcements prior to entering executive 
session were insufficiently detailed. We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with 
the Open Meeting Law and caution the Board that a determination by our office of a similar 
violation in the future may be considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law.  
 Additionally, we order the Board, within 60 days from receipt of this determination, to 
amend the minutes of its February 19 second executive session, held at 8:05 p.m., to include 
sufficient detail. Because we find that each of the executive session discussions fell within a 
proper executive session purpose, we do not order the release of these executive session minutes 
until the purpose for the executive session concludes.  
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any questions.
