# Towns like us: what they pay, what they spend

Analysis, generated by `scripts/build_town_comparison.py`. Every figure below is computed from the documents listed at the end; nothing is typed.

> **Four comparison sets, because they answer four questions.** The six towns that share a border. The five this project uses as peers. The towns the math picks out of all 351. And the places Lunenburg children are actually schooled, which is not a peer set at all.

![A map of Massachusetts with every town outlined and the 32 towns this report compares filled and pinned at their Census centroids, coloured by whether they border Lunenburg, are peers this project chose, resemble Lunenburg on structure, or behave like it.](charts/towns-like-us-map.svg)

## What this report found

- **Wealth per child predicts school spending. State aid, size and poverty do not.** 61% of the difference is none of wealth, aid, size, poverty or tax base.
- **Regional districts do not spend less on administration: $700 a pupil against $693.** On ADMINISTRATION, no. The transport money is a separate line and a real one.
- **8 of 161 towns charge among the least and spend among the most on each pupil.** 6 have the least property value and income behind each child, so the state covers it. 2 have many houses.
- **A town’s tax bill follows house prices: about $995 more per extra $100,000 of home.** The most expensive fifth of towns average $13,795 a year. The cheapest fifth, $5,376.
- **Taxing local business at the maximum rate the law allows would save a home $224.25.** The same shift adds about $2,336.75 to a business. The town’s own assessor advised against it.
- **Lunenburg spends $4,281 less per pupil than the middle comparable district.** Not for want of state aid — it gets more than most. Its taxable value per pupil is below the middle.
- **Lunenburg spends 1.27x what the state says its schools need. The middle town spends 1.46x.** At the middle ratio Lunenburg would spend $2,646 more per pupil.
- **9 of 10 towns most like Lunenburg spend more per pupil than Lunenburg does.** 7 of those same 10 also have a smaller average tax bill than Lunenburg.
- **Lunenburg’s TOTAL school spending grew 3.2% a year — 7th fastest of these 11 towns.** It kept its pupils. The look-alikes lost up to 34.4%, which lifts per-pupil without spending more.
- **Not one of the 10 towns that most resemble Lunenburg also behaves most like it.** Who counts as “a town like ours” depends entirely on which question you ask.
- **Towns that have won 3 or more overrides average a $10,641 tax bill; towns with none, $6,809.** An override lifts the levy ceiling for good, so a vote from decades ago is still in the bill.
- **Only 32 of 177 children schooled outside Lunenburg attend a school one town funds.** 97 are at Monty Tech, which Lunenburg pays into; 38 at charter or online schools.
- **Ashby has asked its voters for an override 60 times and won 7. Lunenburg: 10 and 4.** Leominster has never asked once. Neighbours under the same costs behave completely differently.

## Can a town fund its schools well without taxing its residents? Only 8 of 161 can — and here is how

This is the question underneath every comparison anybody makes, so it goes first. Take the cheapest quarter of these 161 towns by average single-family tax bill — **$5,962 or less** — and the top quarter by spending per pupil — **$24,394 or more**. 8 towns are in both.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|

| Southbridge | $5,070 | $25,651 | $15,389 | 1.2 | the state pays |
| North Adams | $3,894 | $25,570 | $13,683 | 1.9 | the state pays |
| Holyoke | $5,319 | $25,371 | $18,395 | 0.9 | the state pays |
| Springfield | $4,254 | $25,349 | $19,233 | 0.9 | the state pays |
| Wareham | $4,087 | $24,538 | $6,627 | 4.1 | a great many houses per child |
| Chicopee | $4,580 | $24,501 | $13,926 | 1.5 | the state pays |
| Lawrence | $4,140 | $24,394 | $21,277 | 0.3 | the state pays |
| **Lunenburg** | **$7,444** | **$18,027** | **$5,617** | **2.2** | — |

**There are exactly two ways, and Lunenburg has neither.** 6 of the 8 are among the communities where the state’s own formula finds the least LOCAL EFFORT available — Chapter 70 measures a town by its equalized property valuation and its residents’ income, and covers what those two cannot reach: the state puts in a median of $16,892 per child across them, against $3,617 for the middle town here. The other 2 are resort and retirement towns — a great many houses, very few children — so a modest bill spread over 6.0 homes per pupil still raises a great deal for each child. None of the 8 is doing something a town could simply decide to do.

**And business base is not a third way — which is worth saying plainly, because it is the lever people reach for first.** Tested on the same rule as the other two (twice the frame’s median share of the tax base, 13.4%), **0 of the 8** qualify. Nor does it do much quietly: among the 63 towns whose average home is between $400,000 and $650,000 — the band Lunenburg’s $517,296 sits in — those with more business than the median charge $6,492 against $6,630 and spend $22,006 per pupil against $21,375. Real, and small. The town’s own assessor put the same question a different way and got $224.25 a year.

*What this does not show.* Anything about what those schools achieve — there is no outcome measure in this report. And being carried by the state is not an enviable position: it follows from having among the least property value and income behind each child in Massachusetts, with everything else that comes with that. What the table rules OUT is the hope that some town has found a third way. Within these 161, none has.

**It is wealth per child, and it is not close.** The next three rows are the same thing measured differently — home value, business value and income are what taxable value is made of. What is NOT in it: how much the state sends (1%), how many pupils a district has (0%), the share of children from low-income families (0%), and how much of the tax base is business (0%).

**And the most important number is the one that is missing.** Put the five strongest together and they account for **39%** of the difference between districts. So **61% of what separates a district spending the most from one spending the least is none of wealth, aid, size, poverty or tax base.**

*What this does not show.* Which way any of it runs, and what that 61% IS. A town with more wealth behind each child may spend more because it can, or may have attracted that wealth by spending more; one year read across many towns cannot separate them. And the unexplained share is NOT evidence of choice — it is the absence of evidence for the four explanations people reach for. Special education caseload, debt service, contract history and regional structure are all candidates this report does not hold.

## Does regionalising save money?

It is the answer most often offered in this town, and it is argued on shared overhead — so the measure is administration per pupil, which DESE publishes on its own.

| | Districts | Median administration per pupil | With instructional leadership | Median spent per pupil |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Single-town districts | 161 | $693 | $2,054 | $22,308 |
| Regional districts | 49 | $700 | $2,103 | $22,749 |
| **Lunenburg** | | **$581** | **$1,690** | **$18,027** |

**On administration they are not cheaper.** What does cut overhead is SIZE rather than structure, and only somewhat: the smallest quarter of districts (median 1,242 pupils) spend $880 a pupil on administration, the largest quarter (median 6,517) spend $667. Lunenburg already spends $581 — 156th of 210, well into the lean end — so there is little administrative saving here to harvest.

**It reconciles, which is why it can be trusted.** Lunenburg’s grants and revolving come to $3,044,898 here; this report derives $2,945,488 a completely different way, as all-funds spending less net school spending. Two files, two routes, the same answer.

**And one line does most of the work.** Harvard charges $4,964 a pupil of TEACHER salaries to grants and revolving accounts. Lunenburg charges $726. That single difference is most of the gap between what the two draw from outside their appropriations.

*What this does not show, and it is the question the table raises.* WHICH fund. A grant is money won from outside; a revolving fund is money the district itself takes in and may spend without an appropriation. Two kinds are evidenced directly in this archive — the athletics revolving fund, whose cashbook is held, and school choice tuition RECEIVED, which is a cherry sheet line; others such as preschool, food service and rentals are the usual sort and are named as examples, not as a claim about any district’s mix. This file does not separate them, so a district with a large school-choice intake looks identical here to one that writes successful grant applications, and those are not the same opportunity. [Registered](/what-we-cannot-answer), with DESE’s End of Year Pupil and Financial Report as the document that would settle it.

### If three towns share one school, what does each one put in?

Chapter 70 is computed per TOWN, never per member of a district — so each town’s obligation is published separately even where the children share one school system.

| *Lunenburg, for scale* | *Lunenburg* | *1,666* | *$8,571* | *$5,617* |

**Regionalising does not change Chapter 70.** The formula is computed per MUNICIPALITY from that town’s equalized property valuation and its residents’ income, and the district’s figure is the sum of its members’. Ashby’s obligation is Ashby’s whether it belongs to a regional district or not, and joining one would not move it. Any state money that DOES follow from being regional is a different line — the transportation reimbursement under c.71 s.16C, which is a cherry sheet item and not Chapter 70. This project holds no cherry sheet data, so it cannot measure that, and it is [registered](/what-we-cannot-answer) as the gap it is rather than argued either way.

**What is NOT published is the assessment** — what each town actually votes to pay the district. That is fixed by the regional agreement and voted town meeting by town meeting, and it is the one link in the chain no statewide file carries.

**And the member towns do not add up to their district, which is the useful part.** Summing them gives 104.1% to 114.8% of the district’s own figure, because a town’s Chapter 70 covers ALL of its children — including those at a vocational school, a charter or another district by choice — while the district’s covers only the ones it teaches. The proof that this is the explanation rather than an error: within each district the required and foundation ratios are the same number to a tenth of a point, which is what happens when one factor scales both. This report refuses to build if they ever drift apart.

**What Lunenburg would need.** Its business base is $127,465 per pupil against a median of $234,713 across the 161 towns. Reaching that median means **$178,664,500 of new business value** — worth about **$2,570,982 a year** of levy at the current rate. Lunenburg’s assessors certify a median of $24,176,125 of NEW GROWTH of every kind each year, so that is roughly **7 years if every dollar of growth were commercial**, and most of it is housing. **And that is the right size to judge it by.** $2,570,982 a year of extra levy capacity is roughly the size of the gaps this town argues about — so commercial growth is a credible answer to *how do we stop running deficits* and is not an answer to *how do I pay less*. ## What other districts do that Lunenburg does not — and how we know

Five things a comparison like this is usually used to argue for. Each one says where Lunenburg actually sits, whether it is something the town could DECIDE, and how much the data will carry. Two of the five turn out to be things Lunenburg already has.

### Spend further above what the state says your schools need

THE ONE THAT ANSWERS THE QUESTION. The state computes a foundation budget for each town from its own children and its own wealth, so comparing each town to its OWN floor is fair in a way a raw per-pupil figure is not. Lunenburg spends 1.27x its floor; the middle district here spends 1.46x. At that middle ratio Lunenburg would spend $2,646 more per pupil.

*Is it a decision?* Yes, the town could choose differently.  *How sure?* **measured** — DESE per-pupil expenditure SY2025 over the FY2026 town foundation budget per foundation pupil.

*Spending is all funds, so a town that wins more grants shows a higher ratio without its taxpayers deciding anything. And this measures distance from a formula, not whether any amount is right.*

### Be required to put in more

Lunenburg is required to raise $8,571 per child. The middle Massachusetts town is required to raise $10,714. The requirement follows a town’s property wealth and income, so it is not something anybody here votes on — but it IS a floor, not a ceiling.

*Is it a decision?* No. This is circumstance, not policy.  *How sure?* **measured** — DESE Chapter 70 municipal contribution file, FY2026: required local contribution over foundation enrolment.

*A required contribution is a statutory MINIMUM. Most towns appropriate above it, and how far above is the decision in the row above.*

### Spend more on each pupil

$18,027 per pupil, 158th of 161 comparable districts. The middle of those districts spends $22,308 — $4,281 more than Lunenburg.

*Is it a decision?* Yes, the town could choose differently.  *How sure?* **measured** — DESE per-pupil total expenditures, SY2025, all funds.

*Per-pupil spending is money in, not results out. Nothing here says whether Lunenburg is run leanly or is short of what its children need, and no figure in this report could.*

### Get more help from the state

Lunenburg already does. The state expects to cover 40% of its foundation budget, against 24% for the middle district here — 55th of 161. Low spending is NOT explained by thin state aid.

*Is it a decision?* No. This is circumstance, not policy.  *How sure?* **measured** — DESE Chapter 70 municipal contribution file, FY2026: the town foundation budget less the required local contribution.

*A foundation budget is the state’s formula for an adequate education, not what anybody spends, and a required contribution is a minimum rather than what a town raises.*

### Have more taxable value behind each child

CORRECTED, and it goes the other way. Counting HOMES, Lunenburg looks comfortable: 2.2 per pupil against 1.8 for the middle district. Counting VALUE — which is what a tax bill is actually levied on — it has $1,463,439 per pupil against a median of $1,800,204, 119th of 161. More houses per child than most towns and less money behind each child, because its houses are worth less.

*Is it a decision?* No. This is circumstance, not policy.  *How sure?* **measured** — DLS assessed value by class FY2026 over DESE full-time-equivalent pupils SY2025; parcels from the same DLS file.

*This is ALL taxable value — houses, flats, shops, plant. It is also the quantity a commercial base adds to, which is why business and housing are ONE lever here and not two: a warehouse that brings no children and a second home that brings no children do the same thing. What differs is size — business is 8.7% of Lunenburg’s base and housing is the rest.*

### Ask the voters for an override more often

Lunenburg has put 10 questions and won 4. Towns that have won 3 or more average a $10,641 tax bill; towns that have never won one average $6,809.

*Is it a decision?* Yes, the town could choose differently.  *How sure?* **not established** — DLS override and underride votes, every question since FY1990.

*WHICH CAUSES WHICH is exactly what this cannot say. A town with a higher bill may pass overrides because its residents can afford them, rather than having a higher bill because it passed them — and wealth would produce both. Asking more often is a decision; that it would raise Lunenburg’s spending is not shown here.*

### Grow the commercial base

Littleton adds 3.23% of its levy limit in new growth each year against Lunenburg’s 1.75%. But across all 161 towns, new growth and the tax bill are unrelated, and the town’s own assessor priced the largest possible shift onto business at $224.25 a year.

*Is it a decision?* Yes, the town could choose differently.  *How sure?* **measured** — DLS new growth, ten-year mean to FY2026; the correlation across the frame; Select Board minutes of 25 November 2025.

*What growth buys is levy CAPACITY — room under the limit — not a smaller bill for an existing household. Those are different things and are routinely argued as though they were one.*

**The bill is the house, and the household.** Home value and income per capita track the bill almost identically and nothing here separates them — they are largely measuring the same thing. *What this does not show.* Which way any of it runs, and it cannot: this is one year read across many towns. A town with expensive houses can afford a higher bill and also chooses one. What would settle the question for Lunenburg specifically is not a comparison at all — it is the town’s own levy history against its own new growth, which is on [what growth would have to look like](/growth).

>
> “At the maximum allowable CIP shift of 1.50, the residential tax rate would decrease to $13.70 per thousand while the commercial tax rate would rise to $21.58 per thousand”
>
> “a savings of only $224.25 for a $350,000 residential property”
>
> “a tax increase of approximately $2,336.75 for a comparable commercial property”
>
> — Select Board minutes, 25 November 2025 ([our copy](https://lunenburgbudgetproject.org/docs/minutes/text/select-board/2025-11-25-minutes-7534.txt), [the town’s](https://www.lunenburgma.gov/AgendaCenter/ViewFile/Minutes/_11252025-7534))

**Her arithmetic reconciles to the state’s file.** The same minute states an average single-family bill of $7,443.89 on an average value of $517,296; the Division of Local Services publishes $7,444 on $517,296 for the same year. Two independent records of the same two quantities, agreeing to the cent — which is what makes the rest of the analysis worth repeating, because the split-rate figures themselves are hers and we have not recomputed them.

She recommended against a split rate, noting that Lunenburg’s commercial share is under a tenth of the base — this report computes 8.7% for FY2026. **A shift moves the bill between classes. It does not reduce the levy**, which is set before any of this and is what the correlation above is really saying.

A caption model hears “fifteen hundred”, “$1,500” and “$50” alike, so no figure from it may be repeated as something the meeting said.

This report does not introduce the question. It gives the same question a published denominator: 161 towns that run their own K–12 district, matched to Lunenburg by the things a town does not choose, rather than a handful picked to make a point.

## The questions this report could not answer, and what would answer them

Each of these stopped a conclusion. They are registered in `sources/data/money-gaps.csv` and appear on [what we cannot answer](/what-we-cannot-answer), so the next person to hit the same wall finds them rather than describing them again in different words.

| The question | Why it stops here | The one document that would settle it |
|---|---|---|
| What does Lunenburg actually appropriate above the state’s minimum, year by year, beside the same for comparable towns? | The required local contribution is a floor and this report measures one year of the gap above it. The gap over time is the whole decision and is not assembled anywhere. | DESE’s net school spending series per district — published statewide only through SY2022, and held here for FY2026 for six local districts. |
| Which fund pays the teacher salaries a district charges to grants and revolving accounts? | Harvard charges $4,964 a pupil of teacher pay to grants and revolving where Lunenburg charges $726. | How does a regional district divide its assessment between member towns, and what does each actually appropriate? | Chapter 70 is published per town, so what each member is REQUIRED to raise is known. ## What the figures count

Two calendars, never mixed. Tax figures are FY2026, the last year the Division of Local Services has a rate for every town: an AVERAGE single-family bill on an AVERAGE single-family assessment, which is not any one house. School figures are SY2025, the last year DESE has published spending: dollars per full-time-equivalent pupil, ALL FUNDS, so state aid and grants are inside it and it is not what the town raises. An override row is a question PUT to a ballot, not a dollar levied.

## What this report cannot establish

- Whose comparison set is right. The six neighbours are computed from boundary polygons and are a fact; the five peers are OUR choice, and nothing in the town’s own record names a cohort of comparable communities for tax purposes. The twins are a distance in a space we chose the axes of.
- What any destination costs. A count of children enrolled somewhere is not a tuition, an assessment or a share of one, and nothing here says which fund pays for any of it.
- How a regional district divides its assessment between member towns, and what each member town actually appropriates for schools. What each is REQUIRED to raise per child IS published and is used here; what is not published is the step between that minimum and the district’s books.
- What any town actually appropriates, anywhere in this report. A required local contribution is a statutory floor and most towns go above it; how far above is the decision this comparison is really about, and it is not in these files.
- Whether a town spending more per pupil educates a child better. Per-pupil spending is an input. Nothing in this report is an outcome measure.
- Which way any correlation runs. Every figure here is a cross-section of one year across many towns, which cannot separate a town choosing a higher bill from a town able to afford one.

Three of these are registered in `sources/data/money-gaps.csv` and appear on [what we cannot answer](/what-we-cannot-answer), each with the one document that would close it: the town’s own list of comparable communities, the apportionment behind each regional district’s assessment, and what Lunenburg pays per child for each destination outside its schools.
