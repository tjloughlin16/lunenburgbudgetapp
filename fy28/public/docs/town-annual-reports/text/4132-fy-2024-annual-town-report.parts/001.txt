In Memoriam 
James J. Larkin, affectionately known as Jim, left the world on March 31, 2024, leav-
ing a legacy of love and laughter. Born on June 24, 1940, in Newton, MA, Jim’s reli-
able good nature and funny wit made him a beacon of joy to all who met him. Jim 
was a devoted husband to his wife Sheila of 45 years, who survives him. He was a 
very positive role model and loving stepfather to Darrell Maki. He was preceded in 
death by his parents, Robert and Mary (Lynch) Larkin and his brother Robert Larkin 
Jr.  
 
Jim was a dedicated systems engineer in the technology sector at GenRad and Poly-
com before his retirement. He was a passionate member of the Maserati Club, the 
Mid State Antique Auto Club, and the Antique Auto Club of America. May God 
grant him a front row seat at the Antique Auto shows in Heaven.  
 
His interest in family genealogy and the history of Lunenburg led him to become a 
curator for the Lunenburg Historical Society, a role he cherished, especially Whalom 
Park memorabilia. He served in the US Navy from 1961-1966. Jim was also an avid 
metal detector, always looking for hidden treasures. Jim, his siblings, and family fre-
quently shared many stories of their bird watching sightings.

Lunenburg was settled in 1718 and incorporated in 1728. It occupies a land area of approximately 26.4 
square miles. Lunenburg is located in north central Massachusetts  approximately  43 miles from  Bos-
ton,  It is bordered on the west by the Town of Ashby and the City of Fitchburg, on the south by the 
Town of Lancaster and the City of Leominster, on the east by the Town of Shirley and on the north by 
the Town of Townsend. 
 
Lunenburg is a semi-rural community located in northern Worcester County.  Lake Whalom and the for-
mer Whalom Amusement Park delineated the Whalom District from the remainder of Lunenburg. This 
district was developed as a housing base for many factory workers working in the mills and factories of 
Leominster and Fitchburg. In many ways the characteristics of the Whalom District closely reflect charac-
teristics of these cities more than of the rest of Lunenburg. The Hickory Hills Lake area was developed as 
a private community comprised largely of vacation cottages. Shirley Reservoir is a public water body also 
developed with mainly vacation cottages that have now been modified to year-round residences.

REPORT OF THE TOWN MANAGER 
Fiscal Year 2024 
 
Note: The Fiscal Year 2024 Report of the Town Manager was not authored by Town Manager Heather Lemieux.  The content 
herein is based on a review and synthesis of periodic Town Manager Reports provided to the Select Board.  
Fiscal Year 2024 (FY24) was a year of meaningful progress for the Town of Lunenburg, marked by the advancement of long -
planned capital and infrastructure projects, significant strides in restoring the public ’s confidence in the property tax assessment 
process, important legislative wins for our senior residents, a coordinated response to multiple storm events, and continued work 
to address staffing needs across Town departments. Despite ongoing challenges, the dedicated staff, appointed and elected off i-
cials, and volunteers of the community continued to deliver a high level of service to Lunenburg residents.  Notably, Town Ma n-
ager Heather Lemieux tendered her resignation, after over seven years of service, to the Select Board on June 4, 2024.  
Below are the highlights of the key topics and accomplishments from Fiscal Year 2024 within the Office of the Town Manager.  
 
Town Manager Goals 
As in prior years, the Town Manager's goals for FY2024 centered on three primary areas: continuing to advance the redevelop-
ment/reuse of 925 Massachusetts Avenue, review of financial policies and address long -term staffing and unknown infrastructure 
needs to help inform the budget process, and employee recruitment, recognition, and retention. Progress was made across all t hree 
areas during the fiscal year. 
 
925 Massachusetts Avenue Redevelopment 
The redevelopment of 925 Massachusetts Avenue remained a top Town Manager goal throughout FY2024, with substantial pro-
gress made toward readying the site for disposal and future reuse. 
 
In the fall of 2023, the EPA-funded contractor completed the removal of the hydraulic lift and underground holding tank at the 
site, with hazardous materials submitted for proper off-site disposal. The Town's environmental consultant, Joe McLaughlin of the 
BETA Group, continued to coordinate with the EPA and MassDEP on follow -up environmental work. The EPA approved addi-
tional Brownfields funding to conduct soil borings, monitoring well installations, and groundwater sampling to address remaining 
data gaps beneath the building. 
 
In February 2024, the Town Manager met with MassDevelopment and their consultant from BSC Group, engaged through the 
Real Estate Technical Assistance program, to develop conceptual plans, perform a market analysis, and draft a Request for Pro-
posals for the disposal of the property. A site visit took place in March 2024, with work on a strict timeline to expend the technical 
assistance funds by June 30, 2024. The Historical Society also expressed interest in preserving the cupola atop the 925 Mass Ave 
building, and the Town Manager referred the request to the APDC for review and incorporation into the property's disposition 
plans. Conceptual plans yielded two options: maintaining the same footprint of the building if it were to be a renovation and  one 
that would be new construction.  
 
Infrastructure, Facilities, and Capital Projects 
 
Flat Hill Culvert Project 
The Flat Hill Road Culvert replacement was a major project in FY2024. Funded in part by a $500,000 Small Bridge Grant, the 
project went out to bid in early 2024 with the lowest responsive and responsible bid coming in at $830,230. The Town Manager 
successfully secured an additional $100,000 from MassDOT to cover higher -than-estimated costs, bringing total available funding 
to approximately $975,000. Construction was planned to begin in the fall of 2024 when lake water levels are lowered, with an 
estimated two-month road closure.  
 
Page Street Culvert Project 
The Page Street Culvert replacement, performed by Powell Stone & Gravel, was completed in September 2023 on time and under 
budget. The road was closed briefly to allow excavation and installation of the new pipe, and the guardrail was installed sho rtly 
thereafter.

LED Streetlight Conversion1 
 
The LED streetlight conversion project, awarded to Pine Ridge Technologies, was completed in December 2023. Approximately 
358 streetlights were converted throughout Town. The project was funded through the Green Communities Grant, and upon 
completion, the Town received a finalized Unitil incentive of $37,900, exceeding the originally projected $33,400. Total gran t-
eligible expenditures were approximately $139,000. The Town also finalized an Outdoor Light Billing Agreement with Unitil 
establishing the billing structure for the new fixtures going forward. 
 
Senior Center ADA Patio 
 
The Senior Center ADA Patio project, long in capital planning, was completed and celebrated with a ribbon cutting ceremony on  
October 11, 2023. The project was the result of collaborative effort over many years and represents a significant improvement  in 
accessibility and outdoor programming space at the Lunenburg Adult Activity Center.  
 
TC Passios Building 
 
Persistent roof leaks at the TC Passios building required ongoing attention throughout FY2024. The Facilities Department 
patched multiple leak areas and sought quotes for a phased roof repair, with a warrant article submitted to the Special Town 
Meeting. In April 2024, a significant leak damaged the Public Access studio. The Town Manager and Facilities Director decided 
to relocate the studio to the dry rear wing of the building, which occurred after the Annual Town Meeting.  
 
Public Safety Building 
In December 2023, a severe rain and wind event tore off a 30-foot section of metal siding at the Public 
Safety Building, exposing the fire apparatus bays. The Facilities Director responded immediately, securing 
the building that same day. Insurance covered the siding repair, which was completed in the spring of 
2024. The Public Safety Carpet replacement project was also bid in late 2023, with bids due in December.  
The bid was awarded to Atkinson Carpet and Flooring in the amount of $43,790. 
 
Welcome to Lunenburg Sign  
 
A “Welcome to Lunenburg” sign that was provided to the town by the North Central Chamber of Commerce and was installed 
on Lancaster Avenue, near the Stillman Farm.  
 
Lunenburg Adult Activity Center Generator 
 
The LAAC Generator project, included in the FY23 Capital Plan, was bid in the fall of 2023. Four bids were received and the 
project was awarded to Morse Brothers of Westminster for $99,940. With a generator lead time of 16 -18 weeks, installation was 
completed in the spring of 2024. 
 
Marshall Park 
 
The Marshall Park revitalization project advanced significantly in FY2024. A Request for Qualifications for an Owner's Projec t 
Manager was issued in early 2024. After the Park Commission evaluated respondents and conducted interviews, the Town final-
ized a contract with the selected OPM for a not-to-exceed fee of $250,000, covering services through designer/engineer selection  
and completion of PARC Grant-funded construction by June 2025. The Parks Commission also engaged a firm for the prelimi-
nary permitting required for the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers permit related to the $1,000,000 Land and Water Conservation 
Fund grant. The Town also finalized the Right of Entry Agreement with the Lunenburg Skate Park organization, with construc-
tion planned to begin in 2024. 
 
Roadway Maintenance and Infrastructure 
 
Roadway maintenance was active throughout FY2024. Sealcoating was completed in the fall of 2023 on twelve roads. PJ Keat-
ing paved Elmwood Road and West Groton Road in the spring of 2024. Premier Fence completed guardrail repairs on North-
field Road, Leominster Shirley Road, and Graham Street. The DPW crew continued routine maintenance including pothole re-
pairs, drainage clearing, roadside vegetation cutting, and sign repairs throughout the year. The Town also received a $21,979  
Rural Roads apportionment from a MassDOT supplemental transportation bond distribution in April 2024.  
 
Route 2A Road Safety Audit 
 
A Road Safety Audit was conducted in September 2023 by VHB and MassDOT, with the Town Manager, Police Chief, Fire 
Chief, and DPW Director participating. MassDOT repainted lane markings on Route 2A in October 2023, and the final RSA 
report was submitted in November 2023. MassDOT subsequently developed a work order to address additional low -cost safety 
improvements at the two intersections.

Financial Management 
 
Tax Assessment and Billing 
 
FY2024 continued to involve significant complexity in tax assessment and billing. With the Town's property revaluation not 
completed until February 2024, the Bureau of Local Assessment approved a valuation delay, and the Town issued third quarter 
preliminary tax bills using a Pro Forma Recap approved by DOR in December 2023. The Treasurer/Collector and Interim Princi-
pal Assessor, Rena Swezey, worked extensively to test billing processes in Munis before generating final bills, and a detaile d tax 
bill insert was prepared to explain the unusual billing cycle to taxpayers.  
After the FY24 fourth quarter actual tax bills were mailed, the Assessors Department discovered a software error in Munis tha t 
caused an incorrect total taxable value to be printed on bills for property owners with exemptions. The payable amount on eve ry 
bill was correct. The Town proactively sent correction letters to all affected taxpayers with copies of the accurate Vision P roperty 
Cards, and a ticket was submitted to Tyler Technologies to resolve the issue.  
FY25 preliminary tax billing also required outside contractor assistance due to persistent software challenges with Tyler Tec h-
nologies. These bills incorporated the estimated additional tax levy from the override approved by voters in May 2024.  
The Town's New Growth for FY24 came in at approximately $630,000, exceeding the original estimate by $230,000 and provid-

CONSERV ATION  
The Town of Lunenburg Conservation Commission is a seven-member commission, each appointed 
to 3-year terms by the Select Board, whose duties are codified under the Conservation Commission 
Act MGL chapter 40 s 8C. The Commission enforces the Massachusetts Wetland Protection Act 
MGL chapter 131 s 40 and Chapter 239 of the local bylaws for the protection of wetlands. The Com-
mission also manages, and controls town owned lands retained for conservation and passive recrea-
tion use. 
 
The Fiscal Year 2024-25 was a very busy and active agenda containing both public hearings and 
meetings for a wide range of projects from commercial development, subdivisions and small single 
family upkeep projects and larger scale industrial/commercial proposals. The Commission also held 
numerous inspections on weekends prior to meetings to familiarize themselves with the upcoming 
proposals that would appear on the agenda. The Conservation Administrator no longer attends site 
walks with the Commissioners which has affected communications. During this period the Conserva-
tion Commission supported (numbers may be inaccurate due to lack of unified tracking system) 25 
NOIs, 42 Administrative Determinations, 15 RDAs, 69 COCs, and numerous other requests.  
 
The Conservation Commission continues to implement forest stewardship plans developed using 
grants as outlined in the last annual reports. This management effort recently completed at the Rayno 
property on Northfield Road resulting in $28,248 deposited into the Timber -Rights fund. The funding 
from the sale of harvested timber provides the seed money to make it possible for the Commission to 
apply for grants to maintain and improve Lunenburg’s Conservation Lands. The Conservation Com-
mission continues to execute on the awarded MASS Recreational Trail Program Grant that was 
awarded in 2023. The purpose of this grant was to fund the engineering design and permitting work 
to create parking access in Table Rock and Rayno Conservation Areas. The grant scope also covered 
improving Accessible Access to the Cowdrey Conservation Area as accessed from Townsend Harbor 
Road. In early 2025, the permits were issued for Table Rock and Rayno Areas. This effort is ongoing 
to complete the engineering design and permitting work in the Cowdrey.  
 
The Pollinator Habitat portion of the Hollis Rd property provides for a diversity of native flowers 
throughout the growing season. The Habitat promotes insect egg laying and nesting sites for pollina-
tors. The Habitat provides food sources for birds and numerous insects including native bees and but-
terflies. This unique “Hidden Gem” works as an ambassador for native pollinators to our community. 
We are very thankful for the dedicated volunteers supporting the Lunenburg Community Pollinator 
Habitat.

DEPARTMENT OF PUBLIC WORKS 
 
The Department of Public Works is made up of the Highway, Cemetery, Sewer, and Vehicle Maintenance Divisions.  
The Department maintains roads, bridges, sewer infrastructure, and provides services essential to the quality of life and 
welfare of the citizens of Lunenburg.  The staff consists of one Director, one Executive Assistant (shared with the Facili-
ties Department), one Highway Superintendent, 3 Heavy Equipment Operators, 2 Driver/Laborers, one Mechanic, one 
Cemetery Superintendent, two Seasonal Cemetery Laborers, one Sewer Business Manager, and one Assistant to the 
Sewer Business Manager.  
 
Departmental Mission: 
The mission of the Department of Public Works is to provide high quality, cost effective, efficient, and safe services to 
protect, preserve and improve the Town’s roadway infrastructure and related assets in a manner which meets and en-
hances the current and future social and economic needs of the community; to contribute to a healthy, safe and quality 
environment for the Town’s citizens and visitors; and to provide environmentally sound means of solid waste and 
wastewater disposal.    
 
Departmental Goals and Objectives 
Be prepared to respond to any weather, roadway infrastructure, or accident-related emergency that may arise on a 24 
hour per day, three hundred and sixty-five day per year basis. 
Maintain and enhance a highly qualified and dedicated staff of public works professionals. 
Provide preventative maintenance in a proactive manner to reduce reactive maintenance as follows: 
Maintain and update the sewer system and associated pumping stations. 
Maintain and repair roadways per the 2022 pavement management plan. 
Maintain and repair sidewalks per the 2020 PROW ADA Transition plan. 
Increase the number of roadsides mowed by developing a roadside maintenance plan. 
Increase the number of catch basin and stormwater structures cleaned and the number of miles of roadways 
swept per the June 2020 Operations and Maintenance Plan. 
Establish a comprehensive evaluation of bridges and culverts, and prioritize by condition, use, and plans for re-
habilitation. 
Increase training offered by our insurance provider to obtain credits to lessen premiums.   
Find new sources (Federal, State, and other grants) of funding to supplement the operational budget. 
Continue to maintain a high level of courteous communication and productive interaction between members of boards 
and commissions as well as Town residents. 
Strive to modernize the operations of the Department by obtaining and using the most up to date equipment and meth-
ods, including asset management.

Working to stay in compliance with our EPA NPDES MS4 Permit continues to still be an important function of the

Tree Removal   
A Tree Risk Management Inventory of almost 1,200 trees on the roads in Lunenburg was completed in 2015.  The inven-
tory prioritizes the removal and/or pruning of the trees.  However, storm damage and natural attrition creates a backlog 
of tree work that exceeds the Department’s allotted budget for tree removal.  The Department strives to fulfill each resi-
dent’s request for tree-work but must prioritize work based upon the determined degree of danger to person and property 
and location.  The DPW continued to act in a reactionary manner as needs arose.  All tree-work other than brush remov-
al, chipping, and the removal of small diameter trees will continue to be outsourced due to the lack of a bucket truck, 
crane, and a licensed tree professional.  Each spring and after storms, the DPW staff remove and dispose of woody de-
bris within the Town’s right-of-way.  An aggressive program by Unitil in recent years has lessened the burden of the 
Town by removing those trees that may cause an interruption of electrical service to the community.  The DPW appreci-
ates and thanks Unitil for their responsiveness in removing hazardous trees when asked to do so. 
 
Roadside Vegetation Maintenance 
The DPW staff responded to many requests to cut and clear overgrown vegetation from roadsides.  This work was most-
ly done using our new utility tractor with a boom rotary mower attachment (22-foot horizontal reach and 19.5-foot verti-
cal reach).  Some of this work continues to me completed using lawn mowers, saws, and other hand tools.   The DPW is 
developing a town-wide program of selective curbside mowing and vegetation clearing using the new tractor and boom 
mower that will significantly enhance our roadside vegetation maintenance program.   
 
Landfill 
The closed and capped landfill off Youngs Road is inspected by a consultant semiannually and is still in compliance with 
the requirements of the permit issued by the Massachusetts Division of Environmental Protection (DEP).  Gas monitor-
ing and groundwater contamination sampling and testing is conducted by a consultant.  The landfill site including the 
perimeter fencing is inspected on a regular schedule by DPW personnel.  The DPW hires a local contractor to mow the 
cap per DEP Requirements. 
 
Adjacent to the closed landfill is a storage area for catch-basin materials and street sweepings both determined to be haz-
ardous waste by the EPA.  The DEP has issued a Beneficial Use Determination (BUD) permit which allows the storage 
of these materials.  A consultant performs the yearly testing of these materials.   
 
On seven Saturdays in the fall and the spring, DPW personnel oversee and manage Yard Waste Days.  Residents may

Harvey & Sons, Inc.  Recyclable materials were also collected curbside weekly by Harvey.  Pay as You Throw bags may 
be bought at Hannaford Supermarket, Smokestack Roasters, Maki Building Center, Honey Farms, Jaxx Country Variety, 
Lunenburg Gulf, and Baker’s Whalom Variety. The DPW remains the point of contact between residents and our trash

Residents may dispose of household hazardous waste for a fee at the Devens Regional Household Hazardous Waste Col-
lection Center on published dates and times.  All Lunenburg residents have access to more fee-based recycling services 
at the CHARM center found at the Townsend Recycling Center at 33 Greenville Road, Townsend, MA.   
 
Lake Shirley Dam 
In the early 1990’s, with the structure in need of repair, The Lake Shirley Association turned over ownership of the dam 
to the Town.  After the completion of the total reconstruction of the dam, the responsibility for the maintenance, moni-
toring, and record keeping were completed by a small number of volunteers from the Lake Association.  Compliance 
with State dam regulations, the establishment of an operations manual as well as an emergency action plan was placed 
under the umbrella of the Department of Public Works in 2004-2005.   The dam is inspected by a professional engineer 
licensed in Massachusetts.  The most recent inspection resulted in a positive report, and the dam is still in compliance 
with regulations. 
 
Winter Operations 
The DPW was prepared for and responded to all snow and ice weather events with the goal of providing a high level of 
service in a timely manner while at the same time trying to minimize costs.  Lunenburg joins with 17 other towns in a 
cooperative bid to obtain the best possible pricing for rock salt.  However, the price of rock salt continues to steadily in-
crease every year.  The DPW maintains a small fleet of snow removal equipment (trucks, plows, spreaders, loader, back-
hoe, and sidewalk plow).  The fleet is supplemented with vendors as needed.  Due to breakdowns of the Town’s aged 
fleet of large dump trucks, more vendors were needed; thus, adding to costs.  Four new snow fighter trucks (large trucks 
with a salt spreader, front plow, and wing plow) have been ordered and are expected to be available in Fiscal Years 2025 
and 2026. 
 
The clearing of snow and ice from roadways is a critical operation of the DPW as it is a matter of public safety.   
The Town and its residents are fortunate to have a dedicated group of DPW personnel and private vendors who tirelessly 
and successfully challenge the worst Nature has to offer regardless of the severity or duration of a storm.  “DPW work-
ers are the original Storm Troopers.”

FINANCE COMMITTEE REPORT  
 
The Lunenburg Finance Committee is pleased to report that Lunenburg’s financial position is solid. In planning for Fis-
cal Year 2024, the Town has continued the sound, conservative management practices from prior years and has produced 
a budget that is responsible and maintains appropriate funding for the needs of all departments and Town residents.  
The process of developing and presenting a budget to Annual Town Meeting begins in the early fall with the Town Man-
ager meeting with department heads to discuss expenses, wants, and needs. The Town Manager then presents the pro-
posed budget to the Finance Committee in mid-February. Over the following 90 days, the Finance Committee meets 
with the various department heads to discuss their individual line item budget requests. The Finance Committee then 
votes on the recommendation of the finalized omnibus budget provided by the Town Manager and reports to Annual 
Town Meeting. As the Legislative body acting on the Town’s behalf, the Finance Committee recommends the approval 
of the budget as presented at Annual Town Meeting. 
Historically, the Capital Plan has been funded though free cash, taxes revenues, and unspent capital appropriation from 
prior years. For FY24, the available free cash is substantially lower due to the need to fill the overlay account with 
enough funds to offset a worst-case scenario with resident tax abatements. Without the use of ARPA funds, the proposed 
Capital Plan would fund $1,039,010, a 52% decrease from FY23. With the inclusion of the ARPA funds, the proposed 
Capital Plan funds $2,025,010, a 6.4% decrease from FY23. This conservative approach allows the Town to fund critical 
items in the Capital Plan while reserving enough money to cover potential abatements. 
Understandably, an important issue on the minds of many residents revolves around the current assessments and subse-
quent tax levy. While the personal positions, understanding, and potential outcomes of the taxpayers may widely vary, 
there are a fair number of certainties.  
1. The increases felt by some taxpayers do not indicate a windfall of cash for the Town. Lunenburg is restricted by 
Proposition 2½ (Mass. Gen. L. c. 59, § 21C) and, as such, is unable to raise the overall levy of the Town by any 
more that 2½% of the previous year’s levy limit plus any New Growth and Excludible Debt. The table above out-
lines the changes in taxes, showing the modest increase from year to year. 
2. Many Town residents have filed abatements, disputing the assessments made of their homes. Should all abatements 
be granted, the Town would be short of revenue by approximately $1,500,000. To best cover this potential outcome, 
the Town Manager has recommended the transfer of $1,500,000 from Free Cash to the Overlay Account.   
3. The abatement process and Overlay Account are the proper methods of both addressing the issue and reversing the 
financial burden places on some of the Town residents.

Noteworthy topics: 
• The end of FY23 will see a 14.56% reduction of our Debt Service and Excludible Debt, from $3,174,257.53 (FY23)

Board, and all the department heads that gave their time and effort to produce the budget presented at Annual Town

What is the Senior Property Tax Work-Off? 
 
The Senior Property Tax Work-Off Program offers income eligible senior citizens age 60+ an opportunity to have 
an abatement applied to their property tax bill. Participants work in various town departments doing jobs such as 
general office work, custodial, library shelving, meals on wheels, school assistance, etc., to earn the tax abatement. 
The 2024 tax abatement maximum is $1,500 and applied to the participant’s 3rd quarter real estate tax bill.  
 
The current hourly rate is $15.00 per hour. 
 
Eligibility Requirements 
• Applicants must be 60 years of age or older 
• Must occupy and be the owner of the property as of July 1st 
• Receive a property tax bill in their name or their spouse’s name (60 years+) 
• Meet the income guidelines of (proof of income is required): 
     one person household - $50,000  
     two person household - $75,000 
• Earnings are exempt from state taxes 
• Earnings are subject to OBRA if participants are not receiving a pension from either Worcester Regional Re-
tirement or Massachusetts State Teachers Retirement 
• Final amount earned will be applied to the 3rd quarter real estate tax  
• All new applicants are required to have a CORI background check 
• All Property Tax Workers must re- apply and be accepted annually for participation in the program

VETERANS’  SERVICES 
 
The Commonwealth of Massachusetts has the most comprehensive system of assistance offered to Veterans, 
their families and surviving spouses in all of the United States. Every city or town in Massachusetts, in accord-
ance with chapter 115 of the Massachusetts General Laws, must have available to its citizens a Veterans’ Ser-
vices Officer (VSO), either full or part-time, as determined by the community population, in order to serve the 
needs of its veterans. If they want to File VA Claims they must be State Certified thru the VA and are called 
Veteran Agents. Lunenburg has a part-time Veteran Agent at the Ritter Building at 960 Massachusetts Ave. 
The Office hours are Thursdays from 9:00 AM thru 12:00 PM.  
Veterans, families, or widows who qualify can be provided monthly financial assistance (Chapter 115 Bene-
fits), which are reimbursements for specific living expenses such as heating fuel, medical care, and pharmacy 
co-payments. With the Veteran Agent’s approval, the Town of Lunenburg provides the financial assistance, 
but then the town is reimbursed by the State’s Department of Veterans’ Services (DVS). It is the function of 
the Veterans’ Services Office to identify qualifying members of the community and assist with enrollment into 
the DVS program. Additionally, certain Veterans and/or widows may also be eligible for federal benefits from 
the Veterans Administration (VA). Only Massachusetts offers both of these services to every community.  
During Fiscal Year 2024 (July 1, 2023- June 30, 2024), Lunenburg is serving an average of 7 recipients 
(Veterans, Families, or Widows) per month on Chapter 115. Lunenburg paid out $44,454.57 in benefits, or an 
average of $3,704.55 per month. Lunenburg was reimbursed $37,233.82 by DVS, which is typically 75% of 
what was given as assistance, the maximum allowable share. There are a couple of services that the Town is 
reimbursed 100%.  
 
Some examples of Veterans’ Service Office assistance services include:  
• Enrollment in chapter 115, State Veterans benefits  
• Assistance obtaining military service records or replacement medals/decorations  
• Tuition waivers at Massachusetts state colleges  
• Housing assistance, including Veterans Shelters and Veterans Homes  
• Payments of “Welcome Home” bonuses for deployed Veterans  
• Assistance obtaining certain Massachusetts automobile and motorcycle license plates  
• Employment assistance

PLANNING BOARD 
 
The Planning Board consists of 5 members elected for terms of 5 years each. It makes careful studies of the resources, 
possibilities, and needs of the Town and plans for, and reviews, a variety of proposed short and long range development. 
 
Included in this is making and maintaining a comprehensive, land use related Master Plan, setting forth in graphic and 
textual form policies to govern the future growth and development of the entire Town, and that effort continued in FY24. 
Related to that, the Board also oversees the statutory power to regulate the subdivision of land and the creation of public 
ways within the Town by the adoption and administration of rules and regulations governing such development. More 
broadly, the Board makes recommendations to Town Meeting on all matters affecting land use and growth, including the 
Zoning By-Laws of the Town. Under its statutory and local permitting authority, it reviewed a variety of applications for 
development and initiatives for regulatory amendments over 23 meetings in FY24, as outlined below. All applications to 
the board are supported by a fee structure and use of peer review services paid for by the applicants, to offset administra-
tive costs to the Town. 
 
SUBDIVISIONS  
In FY24, under its Subdivision Rules and Regulations, the board held one public hearing to amend the application dead-
lines. Submitted applications included Subdivision Approval Not Required (ANR) plans, which are allowed by the Sub-
division Control Act and are an important part of the board’s regulatory authority over divisions of land. Eleven (11) 
ANR Plans not requiring approval under the Lunenburg Subdivision Rules and Regulations, but still requiring Board 
endorsement, were approved in FY24. One (1) Definitive Plan application was also reviewed and approved. Under relat-
ed Zoning Bylaws Regulations, 1 Common Driveways application was approved.  
 
STORMWATER MANAGEMENT  
Lunenburg’s Stormwater Bylaw requires Board review and approval for any development/redevelopment project that 
disturbs 1 acre or more of land. This Bylaw safeguards public health, safety, welfare and the environment by minimizing 
adverse impacts of contaminated stormwater both off-site and downstream. These requirements are more likely to affect 
large commercial projects and multi-family dwellings, rather than average homeowner wanting to put in a shed, garden, 
pool, etc. In FY24, the Board approved 5 stormwater permits.  
 
SITE PLAN REVIEW  
An application for construction of a 367,000 square foot warehouse and distribution building at 260 Falulah Road was 
approved in FY23, and the site was being prepared for construction in FY24. The majority of the building will be located 
on Airport Road/Crawford Street in Fitchburg, but a portion will extend into Lunenburg. In FY24, construction of a 
372,000 square foot warehouse and distribution building approved in FY23 off Leominster-Shirley Road near the Shirley 
town line was completed. As a result of this approval, improvements that were made to Leominster-Shirley Road with 
the addition of an eastbound left-turn lane into the project and the widening of the road to the north (towards Reservoir 
Road) on the project side of the street continued to improve safety, access and circulation. The Board also received and 
approved 2 Site Plan Review applications in FY24, and several modifications to previously approved projects were con-
sidered and acted upon.  
 
SCENIC ROADS  
Lunenburg has 4 designated Scenic Roads; Lancaster Avenue, Flat Hill Road, Northfield Road, and Leominster Road. 
Over the past year you may have noticed building lots, such as some of those mentioned in the paragraph above, being 
created on some of these roads. As these are Scenic Roads, any repair, maintenance, reconstruction, or paving work in 
the Town’s right-of-way cannot involve the cutting or removal of trees or the tearing down or destruction of stone walls 
without consent of the Planning Board. In FY24, 1 application was reviewed and approved by the Board.
