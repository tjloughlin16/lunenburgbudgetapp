MEMORIAM
CHESTER J. "CHET" PLISKA
1914 - 1995
"Chet" was born in Fitchburg MA on September 24, 1914. He
attended Fitchburg public grammar schools and graduated from
St. Bernard's High School in 1934. While there he captained all
three sports: football, basketball and baseball. He later joined the
National Guard for many years. A few years after marrying his
wife, Helen O'Leary in 1938, together they built a home in
Lunenburg and raised three daughters.
It was his interest in sports and recreation that inspired him to become one of the founders of
the Whalom Recreation Association and was instrumental in building a playground and
basketball tennis court in the Whalom section. For many years "Chet" was active in the
Whalom Recreation Summer Fair held each August at the courts in Whalom that drew people
from miles around. It was a highlight of the summer. Funds for this went toward the
playground and courts which the Recreation Association later gave to the Town of Lunenburg
Parks Department. For over fifty years he served the Town of Lunenburg and its people in
many ways. Though born in the city, he became a member of the Lunenburg Grange shortly
upon arrival in Town and became interested in rural and agricultural affairs and was a member
for nearly fifty years. Through things learned at the Grange, and from its members, he became
an avid gardener himself and his vegetable garden became his spring hobby.
In the early 1950's when Lunenburg and the Whalom area were experiencing growth, he
participated in many committees as St. Boniface Parish was changing and expanding and Our
Lady of the Lake Parish was formed to service the growing population of Lunenburg;
particularly the Whalom section. He was an Assessor for 17 years and served on many Town
Committees. He was a founding member of the Lunenburg Memorial Fund and was
instrumental in securing the Eagle House for Lunenburg Senior Citizens. He spent many hours
working on renovating the Eagle House preparing it for use of all the Seniors in Town. In his
younger days he spent many hours of volunteer time for the youth of Lunenburg with his
concern for the recreation areas; and in his senior years his focus turned toward the aging.
Along with his Eagle House activities, he was past president of the Lunenburg Friendly Seniors
and a Council on Aging member that worked diligently to secure funds for seniors to get Town
meals, a van, and other needed things. He was the first Silver Haired Legislator from the Town
of Lunenburg. On August 25, 1995 he celebrated his 57th wedding anniversary. His love and
concern for the Town of Lunenburg was evident in all aspects in which he participated. It was
not uncommon for aspiring politicians of various state and national office to contact "Chet"
when it came to finding out about Lunenburg and its people. In the 52 years living in
Lunenburg, more than 50 years were spent serving the community.

MEMORIAM
THOMAS W. LAWSON
1908 - 1996
Tom was born on July 9, 1908 in Dartmouth and lived in
Lunenburg since 1950. He graduated from Dartmouth High
School in 1926 and the Stockbridge School of Agriculture,
receiving a certificate there in 1928 and an associate's degree
in 1982.
Tom owned and operated the former Sunshine Dairy
Company for 20 years before retiring in 1967. He was a life
member of the Fitchburg-Lunenburg Lions Club with a 34
year perfect attendance record. He was a director, treasurer,
lion tamer, president and zone chairman. In 1992 he received
the club's Melvin Jones Fellow and the 35-year Chevron; he
also received the Lions 33A Humanitarian Award.
Tom was also active in the Boy Scouts of America, serving 35 years on the Scouting Review
Board. He received the highest award for adults in scouting, the Silver Beaver, in 1976. He
was a member at-large of the Nashoba Valley Boy Scout Council and its advisory board. He
was active in Grange affairs, being a member for more than 60 years of the Groton Grange, the
Middlesex-Worcester Pomona, National Grange, and the Massachusetts State Grange. He was
also a member of the Wilder Lodge of Masons, Leominster Royal Arch Chapter of Masons,
Jerusalem Commandery, Leominster Lodge of the Independent Order of Odd Fellows, and the
King David Encampment.
For more than 30 years he was a volunteer for the American Red Cross, Deaconess-Nashoba
Hospital in Ayer and the Eagle House Senior Center. He was also a member of the following
organizations: Nashua River Watershed Association, Lunenburg Conservation Commission,
Old Sturbridge Village Museum, the Lunenburg, Townsend, Leominster and Fitchburg
Historical Societies, the Wachusett Philatelic Society, the Eastwood Club in Fitchburg, the
Dartmouth High Alumni Association, the Stockbridge School Alumni Association, and the
Friends of Wapack Trail Group.

BOARD OF SELECTMEN
Several ongoing issues in Town experienced a great deal of progress this year. One
of these has been the ongoing Facilities Planning Study which has identified a
number of areas in Town which must be addressed due to the number of failing
septic systems. The Board of Selectmen acting in their capacity as S ver
Commissioners have begun discussions with the City of Leominster with the intention of joining an
intermunicipal agreement similar to the one which exists with the City of Fitchburg. Also completed this
year was the extension of the Summer Street sewer line. The cost of which was borne by property
owners John Donnelly and Earl Ruggerio, who have gifted this line to the Town.
Cooperation among boards has been ongoing and has resulted in numerous meetings among all Town
departments to discuss various capital, space and funding needs.
The Town Hall renovation\restoration work is slowly progressing with the completion of the wheelchair
lift to the second floor and the installation of a roof over the handicap access ramp.
The Lunenburg\ Townsend Rotary Club made a commitment of $2,500 to the refurbishing or building of a
new Gazebo. With the renewed interest in the Band Concerts, a committee is being established to
organize the building of the Gazebo. So far the response has been great with a number of calls for
donation of materials, time and labor.
The Selectmen would like to acknowledge the following for their generous contributions to beautifying
the center of Town: the landscaping work done in front of the Town Hall that was completed by
Lakeview Nurseries, the Lunenburg Grange donation of window boxes, the Lunenburg Woman's Club,
with the aid of the Boy Scouts\Girl Scouts, planting of daffodils around the center as well as in and
around the Town Hall, and the Montachusett Area Newcomers Christmas decorations in front of the
Town Hall.
In response to a petition which was submitted to the Board of Selectmen, and because of public safety
reasons, the Board requested a traffic flow study for the Route 13 (Electric AvenueWhalom Road)
intersection which was completed by the Montachusett Regional Planning Commission. The results of
this survey was submitted to the Mass Highway Department along with the Town's request for the
installation of a traffic signal at this increasingly dangerous intersection.
The last item to mention is the passing of two long term committee members, Chester Pliska and Thomas
Lawson of our community who gave a lifetime of service to the Town of Lunenburg. Their many years of
time and effort given to serve the Town has been appreciated.
Your Board has enjoyed working in service to the Town, working with each other and with you the
citizens. The Board wishes to extend its' thanks and gratitude to all the volunteers who have given of
themselves over the past year.

CAPITAL PLANNING COMMITTEE
The FY'97 Capital Plan Articles passed at the Annual 1996 Town Meeting were for a $210,000.00
borrowing article for a front end loader and the 950 Massachusetts Avenue property. A Taxation Article
was passed for $149,650.00 that included, High School auditorium seating, re-paving of School parking
lots, Town Hall furnace/air conditioning, handicap access ramp roof, resealing High School tennis
courts, upgrades at the Library, Rolling Acres drainage, and ventilation system at the Highway Barn.
There was also $52,800.00 spent on Capital items under $5,000.00 that were noted in Departmental
budgets as line items.
The FY'96 Capital Plan allocation was $287,140.00. Monies spent was $279,998.12. The balance is
$5,359.09. There are ongoing projects in FY'96 in the amount of $2,276.09. The monies available for re-
appropriation are $3,083.00.
The monies allocated in the FY'95 Plan was $633,200.00 and the amount spent was $580,620.12.
Balance is $52,579.88. However, $5,369.58 of that balance are for ongoing projects. Therefore, the
money available regarding appropriation of the above is $47,210.30
The Committee, using the Emergency Procedure Policy approved the purchase of a truck. There was an
accident with a Highway truck in October, 1996 and this truck was taken off the road for many months.
Two additional trucks were also taken off the road due to safety, making it necessary to provide one
additional truck. A used truck was purchased for $18,000.00. The Finance Committee requested
funding out of budget and will supplement the necessary amount at the end of the fiscal year. This
purchase fit the criteria of need and was carefully looked at by the Board of Selectmen, Finance
Committee and the Capital Planning Committee.
Another emergency request was approved for the Computer Advisory Committee for $7,300.00. The
original amount allocated did not include conversion cost to a new software system and purchase of
three PC's which are required to run the new software system for the Town Collector, Treasurer and
Accountant Offices. It would not have been cost effective not to proceed with this approval.
The Town Meeting of 1994 passed a Capital Expenditure for a new roof at the Middle School. The money
amount was for $130,000.00. This amount guarantees a reimbursement by the School Building
Assistance Bureau for 70% of this cost. However, the bids came in under that amount, and the final cost
was $84,383.70. Therefore, the reimbursement factor did not apply. This is an example of a problem that
the Committee is addressing with Policy and Procedure. A request has been made to all Departments to
report any changes in the purchasing amount that differs from the allocated amount. This enables the
Finance Committee and the Town Treasurer to employ funding and/or borrowing procedures
appropriately.
The Committee has worked with the Town Accountant to address the use of unspent allocated Capital
Funds in an appropriate and most cost effective manner for the Town. The Committee has written a
policy to direct these Capital monies to meet emergencies, save for Long Range Capital Expenditures
and/or assist in the funding of the next FY plan. These Capital funds are never used within a
department's operating budget.

CENTRAL MASSACHUSETTS MOSQUITO CONTROL PROJECT
The Central Massachusetts Mosquito Control Project (CMMP) currently provides its services to 28
cities and towns throughout Middlesex and Worcester Counties. The project's headquarters is
located at 111 Otis Street, Northboro, Ma. Tours of the headquarters or visits to field work sites may
be arranged by calling the office in advance.
The CMMCP practices Integrated Mosquito Management (IMO), blending state of the art methods and
techniques with expertise, experience, and scientific research to provide our member communities
with modern, environmentally sound, cost effective mosquito control.
The Mosquito Awareness program which we offer to elementary schools in our district has become
very popular. Project staff meet with students and teachers to discuss mosquito biology, mosquito
habitat, and control procedures. Much of the presentation is directed towards what the children and
their families can do to prevent mosquitoes from breeding around their homes. Live sample of
mosquito larvae are included with the presentation, and are left in the classrooms so that students can
watch them develop. Slides, videos, handouts, and coloring books help to make this an interesting
program.
As part of our effort to reduce the need for pesticides we continue to expand our water management
program. By cleaning clogged and overgrown waterways, mosquito breeding can be reduced,
wetlands are restored, and water quality is improved.
Bti mosquito larvacide is used to treat areas where mosquito larvae are found. We routinely check
known breeding sites, but also encourage the public to notify us of any areas they suspect could
breed mosquitoes. Our field crews will investigate all such sites and treat if needed.
Our goal is to handle all mosquito problems with water management or larviciding but we recognize
that there are times when adult mosquito spraying is the only viable solution. In such cases
residential and recreational areas are treated with either hand-held or pick-up mounted sprayers.
The projects surveillance program monitors adult mosquito and larval population density and is the
backbone for prescribing various control techniques. Rain gauges are set out and data collected by
our surveillance crews in an effort to predict when mosquito breeding will occur.
The projects video "Working for You" is available to anyone interested in learning about mosquito
control and the services provided by the Central Massachusetts Mosquito Control Project.

COMPUTER ADVISORY COMMITTEE
Over the past twelve months the Computer Advisory Committee (CAC) has created,
submitted and awarded bids on two major Request for Proposals (RFP). The first
RFP was for financial software which will integrate all of the Town's financial
processes into one system. One of the main objectives in purchasing this software was to eliminate the
current duplication of work being performed As part of the overall plan, a new server was purchased for
the Town Hall and a WindowsNT platform has been implemented. As the installation has progressed, it
became apparent that the heaviest users of the software were left with older machines, therefore, the
Committee recommended to the Capital Planning Committee that three additional machines be replaced
in fiscal year 1997 in order to optimize workflow in the Town Offices.
The second request, secured much needed expertise in maintaining the Town's complex computing
environment. This year the Committee is seeking continued funding of a part-time computer consultant.
A great deal has been accomplished this year largely due to the assistance of numerous volunteers. As
the Town continues to grow, and its computer processes become more complex, the need for additional
assistance must be assessed.
Communication with the School's Technology Committee has recently been established and a plan to
integrate technology futures is being developed It is the intention of the Committee to begin working
together in all common areas to leverage each others area of expertise and develop a town-wide
computer plan.
To date, the CAC has purchased nine personal computers and two printers; as specified in the computer
replacement plan presented at last year's Annual Town Meeting. All of the personal computers which
were replaced have been allocated to other departments. It is imperative that the Town continue to
manage it's computer equipment in an cost effective manner.
Moving forward, it is critical that all town departments be able to communicate with each other
electronically. Therefore, the School and Town Committee's jointly recommended that the Town
negotiate with it's cable provider to implement an Institutional Network, commonly referred to as I-Net.
I-Net offers the Town the means to communicate electronically, which otherwise would be cost
prohibitive. The Committee's have recommended that I-Net be implemented in ten municipal facilities.
The Computer Advisory Committee has been approached by a number of departments requesting
internet access. Internet access will allow departments to download information from state agencies,
research various areas and to communicate with other municipalities. The cost of dial-up internet
access from a local provider is approximately $30.00 a month per site. The Committee has
recommended that initially one computer be located in the Town Hall to be available to all Town
departments. Eventually, the Committee hopes to accomplish town-wide internet access.
CONSERVATION COMMISSION
The Lunenburg Conservation Commission consists of seven appointed volunteer
townspeople who are empowered to administer the Massachusetts Wetland Protection
Act and the Lunenburg Wetlands Protection Bylaw. There may also be a variable
number of associate members. The Commission meets on the first and third
Wednesday of each month for the purpose of:
Conducting public hearings for reviewing Notices of Intent and Requests for Determination of
Applicability under the Wetlands Protection Act and the Lunenburg Wetlands Protection Bylaw;
Holding informational meeting with interested citizens;

===PAGE 17===
Conducting planning sessions for management and improvement of Conservation Lands, and
acquisition of new land parcels for Conservation;
Implementation and enforcement of Lunenburg's interests as set forth in the Massachusetts and
Lunenburg Wetlands Protection regulations;
In 1996, the Commission reviewed 31 Notices of Intent and 9 Requests for Determination of Applicability.
In addition, the Commission issued 2 Extensions of Orders of Conditions, 2 Enforcement Orders, 29
Orders of Conditions and 9 Certificates of Compliance. Recent years have had consistently high activity
due to the resurgence of building and development in Town.
Three new occurrences of rare or endangered wetland wildlife were documented in Lunenburg during
1996. These are testament to the still largely unspoiled character of the Town. The Commission also
certified two "vernal pools" with the cooperation of site developers. These, and seven other vernal pools
will enjoy additional protection under the Wetland Protection Act.
Near mid-year Julia McKinney left her position of Secretary to the Commission to pursue other interests.
Julie helped the Commission in its mission with enthusiasm and we wish her well in her future pursuits.

welcoming and dedicated to conservation and many other local causes. We were all made a little better
by having known him. He is, and will be, dearly missed.

COUNCIL ON AGING
In Fiscal Year 1996, based on statistics compiled for the Executive Office of Elder Affairs,
48% of Lunenburg residents over the age of 60 were recipients of services, programs or
activities provided by Council on Aging (COA) staff and volunteers at the Eagle House
Senior Center. All persons over the age of 60 are sent copies of the COA newsletter
three times a year, with copies of other monthly issues available in various location in the
community. Non-elders were also served in the areas of assistance to families, handicapped
transportation, flu shots, general information and intergenerational activities.
One of the most utilized and cost effective programs the COA coordinates is the elder van transportation
to destinations in Lunenburg and abutting communities. Some 4,000 one way trips for medical
appointments, shopping, and errands were utilized in FY96. The cost per elder is $.50 each way, with
$1.00 each way to the hospital in Ayer. This service is made possible through the Montachusett Area
Regional Transportation Authority (MART). MART provided the 10 passenger wheelchair-access
equipped van, and reimbursed the Town $21,883.00 to cover operational costs such as fuel, salary for
drivers, and a portion of telephone costs and staff salaries. For this, the Town was assessed $11,563.00
on the "Cherry Sheet". The assessment also covers cost of membership in the transit authority as well as
any bus service available to all town residents. Trips to out-of-district medical facilities are coordinated
by COA staff, with vans from MART providing the transportation.
Another vital program coordinated by COA staff is the home delivered "Meals on Wheels" and
congregate meals program in conjunction with the Montachusett Opportunity Council (MOC) Nutrition
Program. Volunteers delivered almost 4,000 noon, supper and week-end meals in FY96, with some 2,900
noon meals served at the Eagle House The Town contributed $1,000.00 to the program with the
suggested donation by recipients $1.75 per meal.
The COA staff provides general information to elders and non-elders, answering questions not only
about local offerings, but also responding to inquiries about other social service agencies. A first this
past year was the use of the Eagle House by Lunenburg Civil Defense as an emergency shelter during
the early December storm and resulting power outages. The shelter was manned at times by COA staff
and Red Cross volunteers.
At the annual Volunteer Recognition Reception 87 volunteers were honored for contributing a total of
3,516 hours, valued at $32,215.00. Honored with an Eagle Pin for contributing a total of 500 hours of
service were Edith Gay, Bea Stepton, and Jean Hobbs. Time contributed by volunteers is most essential
to elder services in Lunenburg which could not function at the level they do without this valuable asset.
New volunteers are always needed and welcome. Those interested in any capacity are asked to call the
Eagle House, at 582-4166.
The COA, staff, and volunteers look forward to the completion of the building project for an addition to

There will be a larger COA office, a separate office for the Director to meet and counsel clients, a more

BOARD OF HEALTH
Two years after the implementation of the new "Title 5", the Board of Health continues to deal with the
effect of changes in the code upon Lunenburg residents.
While much of the new code is more lenient than prior regulations, two changes have caused difficulty
for Lunenburg homeowners. The new Title 5 requires a septic system inspection by a State certified
Inspector whenever a property changes owners. In Lunenburg, approximately 25 percent of systems
inspected fail, and by law must be replaced. The new code also changed the way groundwater depth is
determined. Because of high water levels in many areas of town, many replacement systems must be
constructed partially above ground level, resulting in unsightly mounds in our yards. While its authority
to grant variances to this requirement is extremely limited, the Board approves such requests whenever
possible.
Requests for loans for Septic repairs under a State program were processed, and five failed systems
were approved for repair.
At present, it seems unlikely that any significant easing of Title 5 is in the offing, and it is incumbent upon
us to plan now for the future.
A Sewer Needs Study is nearing completion. Briefly, the proposed starter system will most likely begin
in the center of town, with a terminus in the Whalom area, where it will connect to a neighboring city.
This one line of pipe will effectively address two of the four most severe problem areas of the town; i.e.
the Town Center area, where several town owned septic system (including the school buildings) have
failed and require replacement, and the Whalom area. Tie ins all along the route will be possible as well.
The Board of Health strongly urges you to fully study the proposal and carefully consider its positive
impact on the Town when called upon to vote on it.
The rabid animal epidemic has waned over the past year, though several recent reports in the area
indicate that it may not yet have run its course.
The Board of Health, through its agent, the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health, continues to provide
dental examinations and fluoride treatments in our schools. Numerous clinics are also held at various
times of the year for a variety of conditions. These are advertised in advance in the local newspapers or
you may contact our office for information.
The Board of Health is composed of three of your neighbors, who have volunteered their time to serve
you, the citizens of Lunenburg. If you have any questions or problems that you think we may be able to
help you with, please call our office at 582-4135.

The Planning Board approached pro-actively an important issue by presenting a Bylaw to the Town
Meeting concerning Telecommunication and Cellular Towers. The Federal Act of 1995 allows
communities to set locations, and site standards, therefore directing our own destiny concerning the
building of towers in our community. The Bylaw was passed at Town Meeting, and after careful scrutiny,
was approved by the Attorney General's office. There are three very important issues concerning this
Bylaw: Towers are only allowed in the Industric Zone, only mono type towers are permissible, and
multiple carrier use of towers (aiding in the reduction of the number of Towers in our community).
It was of great interest to this Board that the Bylaw protecting this Town with a reasonable approach to
the siting of these towers was written up in two National Planning Journals. Many other communities
across the State have requested copies of this Bylaw. Presently several communities are using this
Bylaw as a model. The compliments to Lunenburg for addressing the issue and putting into place
protective regulations have been numerous and rewarding.
The Board is concerned over the monumental task of providing infra-structure services in a growing
Community. The immediate needs are numerous. The sewering of high priority areas and a school
building program are on the Town's future agenda. Concurrently there are space needs in the
Governmental Offices, Fire and Police service, Library, and Cemetery. Additionally, there are needs in
the Highway and Park Department. Therefore, the Planning Board will take another pro active approach
to designing Lunenburg's destiny by presenting two Bylaws for the 1997 Town Meeting. These Bylaws
will aid in managing growth spurts. One is a mandatory cluster Bylaw for parcels over twenty-five acres.

FIRE DEPARTMENT
The Lunenburg Fire Department experienced a very busy year for 1996. The increased
in our run total are indicative of a growing community, with a population that is requiring
more and more of our services. During calendar year 1996, we responded to 400
rescue\ambulance calls. This is an increase of 22 calls from the previous year. We
responded to 413 fire related incidents. These increased by 90 calls from the year
before. In addition to the fire and rescue\ambulance calls, our personnel conducted 350
inspections mandated by various Fire Prevention and Building Codes.
The makeup of our fire department consists of 3 full time firefighters, including the Chief. The full time
personnel cover calls during the day, along with any of our paid\call firefighters that are available. At
night and on weekends our call force handles calls. Whenever needed, we can call on our neighbors
through the Mid State Mutual Aid System to assist us when our resources are used up. The present
system is providing excellent fire & EMS service at this time, although we do have occasional times when
we are shorthanded. As the community grows and more and more demands are placed on our
personnel, we may be forced to hire more full time people, but I don't' see that happening in the very
near future.
We are still in desperate need of more adequate facilities to carry out our mission. Not only do we need
more space for storage of apparatus, but we must address space needs for living quarters,
decontamination rooms, material storage, training areas, maintenance areas, office space, parking
areas, apparatus cleaning and apparatus maintenance areas, etc. The building that we operate from
now is 20 to 90 years old, depending on which addition you refer to and the site that it sits on is totally
inadequate with regards to parking and safety. We need an adequate facility that is not only functional
but is also safe for our people to work out of and is also safe for the people that have business with us.
This year, we were very excited to see the implementation of E-911 in our community. Our dispatchers
are fully trained and certified E-911 telecommunicators. This service to the townspeople is the very best
means of identifying and pinpointing where and when the 911 calls are coming from, and enables our
dispatchers to get the proper emergency response out to you as quickly as possible. We have been very
busy educating the school children, senior citizens, business groups and citizens in our community on
how E-911 works. If we can answer any questions you may have relative to the operation of E-911,
please call us on our general business line 582-4155. A special thanks to all our emergency service
dispatchers for their dedication and training and also for the services they provide to us on a daily basis.
Your firefighters and EMT's are totally committed and dedicated to providing you with the best service.
They expend a tremendous amount of time and effort to train and keep their skills at the highest possible
level so that they will be ready to answer your call for help when you need it. I am very proud to be Chief
of such a dedicated group of people. I thank them for their service.

MONTACHUSETT REGIONAL VOCATIONAL TECHNICAL SCHOOL
Monty Tech began its second quarter century of operation with its largest enrollment
in recent years; a 10-year renewal of its accreditation, and plans to expand and
modernize its 25-year old facility to accommodate the school's growth and diverse
student population and bring it into the third millennium.
Superintendent-Director Stratos G. Dukakis is extremely pleased to report that 1996
began on a very high note. After a lengthy and involved review process, Monty Tech was awarded a
10-year renewal of its accreditation status by the New England Association of Schools and Colleges,
which accredits schools in the six New England states. In its evaluation, the NEASC listed a number of
commendations, including praise for the school's outstanding faculty and excellent academic and
vocational-technical offerings. It is also recommended expanding and modernizing the existing
facility, which was built in the late 60's and early 70's, to address the increasing enrollment;
requirements of state and federal mandates, and recent advances in technology.
Already aware of the need for expanding\modernizing, a subcommittee was established, at the behest
of Superintendent Dukakis, to study the issue and put forth a recommendation. After nearly two years
of study and review, the School Committee, at its November, 1996, meeting, approved a $14.66 million
project designed to address the school's current and long-range building needs. Its fate is now in the
hands of the member communities.
Monty Tech's yearlong commemoration of its twenty-fifth year of educational excellence to the
Montachusett region continued into 1996. The school opened its doors September 20, 1971.
Activities and evens to mark this auspicious occasion were held throughout the first half of 1996. The
official observance culminated in May with a weekend of activities, including a luncheon for retired
staff members, a dinner dance and a re-enactment of the groundbreaking ceremony. Many political
leaders, former graduates and retired staff members took part in the events.
The first year of implementation of the school's new motivational incentive program, known as
"Bulldog Pride" was termed "a huge success." Modeled after a similar program used in a voc-tech
school in Butler, PA, the program is designed to increase school attendance, decrease discipline
problems and create a better school atmosphere. It targets four areas: achievement, attendance,
discipline and recognition. Eligible students qualify to win prizes ranging from gift certificates and
stereo cassette players to color televisions and cars.
Members of the Class of 1996 were awarded their diplomas and trade certificates at Monty Tech's
twenty-fifth Commencement Exercises held Friday, June 7, in the Vietnam Veterans' Memorial
Physical Education Complex at Mount Wachusett Community College, Gardner. Keynote speaker was
State Senator Robert A. Antonioni (D-Leominster).
At the annual Senior Awards Night program, held Tuesday, June 4, in the school gymnasium,
members of the Class of 1996 were presented with awards and scholarships in recognition of their
many accomplishments in the areas of academics, vocational-technical skills, athletics, student
government and citizenship.
In keeping with recent post-graduate trends, approximately 35 percent of the Class of 1996 is
furthering their education at two and four-year colleges and universities, as well as technical and
business schools: 55 percent chose to go directly into the work force, most in fields directly related to
their majors, and 10 percent opted for military service.
The 1996-97 school year officially got under way Tuesday, August 27, for both new and returning
students in grades 9 through 12. Freshmen and juniors attended academic classes the first week of
school, while sophomore and seniors spent the week in their vocational-technical programs.
Indicative of the current increase in interest in career-oriented education, enrollment for the opening
of school was 1, 042, one of the highest in recent years. The Class of 2000 numbered 350, an all-time
high. Education leaders across the nation predict this trend will continue.

THOMAS C. PASSIOS ELEMENTARY SCHOOL
Many exciting things are happening at the Thomas C. Passios Elementary School. The
Elementary School holds as its goal the education of each of its seven hundred and
seventy-two children. It strives toward promoting growth in a variety of areas using as
many means and methods as possible. To this end, the School seeks regularly to assess
and improve its curriculum, to meet the needs of a diverse student population, and to
provide for the emotional, social, intellectual and physical needs of each child.
Curriculum revision brought about several changes at the Elementary School this year. Through the
implementation of our new science curriculum, K-12, our Science and Technology for Children units
capture children's natural curiosity, stimulate their interest in science, and teach them important
science concepts, along with critical-thinking skills by using a carefully designed inquiry-basedapproach
that involves children.
Another addition that we are pleased to include this year is the implementation of Project Read
strategies in grades one and two. Project Read is a nationally recognized program that strengthens
children's phonics knowledge in reading using a multi-sensory approach. Although the Elementary
School has always taught reading using both a literature-based and phonic approach, the
implementation of Project Read strategies will further strengthen every child's reading/spelling
knowledge.
The Elementary School was fortunate to be able to add an additional preschool class this year. With this
additional class we will be better able to address the educational needs of an increasing number of three
and four year olds in Lunenburg while complying with special education numbers.
This year also brought back a small group grade two classroom. Based largely on space issues, this
small grade two classroom provides the much needed one-on-one and small group assistance for
children. Children are identified at the end of the first grade year as needing more time and or
assistance during the second grade. Much time is spent on reading and math, two areas critical to a
child's success in school. We are confident that with increased teacher attention due to the reduced
numbers, these children will be better equipped to enter grade three.
Technology is moving ahead quickly at the Elementary School. Seven new PC's were purchased this
past summer to begin a mini-lab at the Elementary School. Sandra Stafford, librarian, will instruct
children in grades two through four on the use of computers in research. In addition, we are excited to
have three staff members currently enrolled in a masters program through Lesley College which will
focus on integrating technology into the curriculum. We are extremely excited about the prospects of
having "in-house experts" to guide our journey into the unknown world of technology.
Again this year, the fundraising efforts of the PTO have resulted in $3000. worth of technology materials
for the Elementary School. With this money, our staff and students will have the benefit of a scanner, a
Presenter TView (This machine projects material from a computer onto a television screen.), a zip drive,
and three black/white computer cameras.
For the past several years, the Elementary School has provided numerous activities/events to broaden
the cultural knowledge of our children. This year we are focusing our activities on the country of Japan,
its people, culture, and customs. To assist us in this endeavor we have been fortunate to have a teacher
from Japan on staff for a six month period. Ayako Hayashi is affiliated with Harmony Company and the
International Internship Program. Since Ayako arrived in November, she has endeared herself to every
child and teacher in the Elementary and Primary Schools, first through the art program and subsequently
in their classrooms. It is our intent that through exposure to various cultures, the children of Lunenburg
will gain an appreciation and respect for the differences in our world.

TURKEY HILL MIDDLE SCHOOL
The 1995-1996 school year was a busy, academically rewarding and productive year for
students, staff and administration. At Turkey Hill Middle School, young adolescents
learn and grow in a safe, nurturing and intellectually stimulating environment. Our
beliefs include the following key components:
Our curriculum is an up to date academic program that challenges students and
promotes excellence in the areas of mathematics, social studies, English, science and
world languages. Art, music, physical education, health, family and consumer science and technology
education enhances the core curriculum. All areas of instruction provide each student with the
opportunity to pursue and explore different areas of interest, while encouraging them to be creative,
motivated learners.
We strive to encourage a school climate in which all human beings will experience a warm, respectful,
caring atmosphere.
Academic standards and expectations continue to be a high priority. The entire staff strives to provide
the maximum number of opportunities for all students to learn.

Plan. Council members Pam Trainque, Irene Masciarelli, Dan Evers, Tom Sugrue, Elizabeth Burnap,
Sharon Kimball, Kerry McCarthy, Sharon McCullah and Mary Cringan are proud of their
accomplishments and believe that THMS is a fine educational institution. The five objectives in the

SPECIAL EDUCATION DEPARTMENT
name
The Special Education Department of the Lunenburg Public Schools currently services
approximately 200 students. This represents 9% of the total school population and
falls below the state average. Both regular education and special education staffs,
parents, and the community as a whole should be commended for offering an
environment where differences are celebrated.
Eligible students ages 3 to 22 are served by special education in accordance with state and federal laws.
Programs range from related services of speech and language therapy, physical therapy, occupational
therapy, social skills groups, adaptive physical education and counseling services to individual tutoring
or core academic instruction either in the regular classroom or in a special setting with the assistance of
a special educator. Related services are given within the framework of educational needs and are
adjuncts to needs in academic instruction based on documented learning disabilities and/or physical
disabilities.
In accordance with current thinking and the constitutional guarantee of a free, appropriate public
education in the least restrictive environment regardless of disability, the Lunenburg Public School
system is seeking ways to educate all Lunenburg students in their own community. With this in mind,
there has been an on-going study of space availability and emphasis of new programming through the
budgetary process. Future plans to develop comprehensive strands specializing in varying needs for 3
year-old pre-schoolers to transition planning for 22 year olds is underway. Services are given and
programs are developed with the goal to afford special education students access to higher education,
jobs, and full participation in society.
Currently, twenty-three Lunenburg students are served in out-of-district programs. These include:
residential, private 766-approved day programs, and collaborative classes. With the support of regular
education administration, staff preparation and training, and advisory from the Lunenburg PAC-SAL, the
development of a three-year plan is mandated to outline best practices to include these students in our
school community whenever appropriate.
All parents are invited to join and participate in Lunenburg's special education parents' group called
PAC-SAL. The group meets monthly and advises on budget, programming and training needs. In
addition, PAC-SAL sponsors special programs, guest speakers, films and interactive workshops.
Through the PAC-SAL newsletter, all calendars are advertised.
The Special Education Department of the Lunenburg Public School system employs a creative, well-
qualified and committed staff of teachers, therapists, tutors, aides, psychologists, and a social worker.
The quality of special services can be maintained only when augmenting a strong regular education base
with small class size and sufficient resources including regular education remedial programs. Further
collaboration among all parties will insure that the quality of education continue to assist each and every
student in maximizing his or her own potential.
Lastly, the Lunenburg Special Education Department bid farewell to Director Linda Chase. It expresses
appreciation for her leadership over the past ten years.

BOARD OF ASSESSORS
Lunenburg has now completed its second full year under the regional agreement with Ashby and
Townsend. The arrangement we have with our neighboring communities calls for the shared expense of
retaining a professional, full-time assessor to administer the Town's property valuation and taxation
function. We are happy to report that the tri-town agreement has been cost effective and well received
by the taxpaying public. The Massachusetts Department of Revenue is hoping that other rural
communities having trouble retaining qualified assessment personnel and who have had to retain the
services of costly valuation consultants will consider Lunenburg's solution.
Part of the success of regionalization can be attributed to a very capable staff. Norma Frost, our
administrative assessor, handles the day-to-day office operations; insuring that vital property records
are properly maintained, public information requests satisfied, budgets prepared, numerous reports
completed and filed, and Board activities supported. Dorothy Musgrove, our part-time clerk, supports
the office by processing deed transfers, handling automobile excise abatements, inputting new
construction data, and providing the public with her courteous and knowledgeable public assistance.
Harald Scheid, our regional assessor, is responsible for all property assessment functions including
market research, valuation model development, tax assessment preparation, valuation defense, tax
forecasting, and Department of Revenue reporting. He can normally be found in our Lunenburg office on
Tuesdays and Fridays, but is on call to address important issues that might arise while he is in Ashby or
Townsend.
This year saw Paul Dickey run for successful re-election to a second term. Our chairman, Paul Dickey,
along with assessors Chris Comeau and Paul Gagliardi served in a policy making and assessment review
capacity. In May, the Board elected Chris Comeau chairman. Paul Gagliardi left the Board in November
after moving from Lunenburg. Carl (Ernie) Sund, a previous Board member, was reappointed by the
Board of Selectmen to fill Paul Gagliardi's position.
Our office carried out a revaluation of all town properties in 1996 - the first revaluation since 1992.
Having completed a 5 year property inspection program, assessor Harald Scheid used our extensive
property database to estimate new property valuations. In comparing the selling prices of properties
that sold in 1994 and 1995 with assessed valuations, he found that on average valuations were running
below selling price by 10-12 percent, suggesting that property values have increased since the last
revaluation. Though the revaluation's primary goal was to increase assessed valuations to adjust for
increasing property values, the revaluation served a secondary goal of improving assessment equity.
Corrective measures dealing with both undervalued and overvalued properties will lead to a more
equitable assessment base
You will note in this years report that we have again provided a breakdown of the average residential tax
bill, showing you how your property tax dollars will be spent in fiscal year 1997. The costs are broken
down into eight separate service categories. The columns show the actual budgeted amount, its
percentage of the total tax levy, and its cost to the average residential taxpayer.
