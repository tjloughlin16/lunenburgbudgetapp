If approved by the Great and General Court, the Act, as amended, would read as follows: SECTION 1. Any holder of an elected
office in the Town of Lunenburg may be recalled therefrom by the registered voters of the town as herein provided, except the maximum
number of members of a multimember board that may be recalled is a simple majority.
SECTION 2. Any one hundred registered voters of the town may initiate a recall petition by filing with the town clerk an affidavit
containing the name of the officer sought to be recalled and a statement of the grounds for recall. The town clerk shall thereupon deliver
to said voters making the affidavit copies of petition blanks demanding such recall, copies of which printed forms he shall keep
available. such blanks shall be issued by the town clerk, with his signature and official seal attached thereto. They shall be dated, shall be
addressed to the selectmen and shall contain the names of all the persons to whom they are issued, the name of the person whose recall is
sought, the grounds of recall as stated in the affidavit, and shall demand the election of a successor in the said office. A copy of the petition
shall be entered in a record book to be kept in the office of the town clerk. Said recall petition shall be returned and filed with the town clerk
within twenty days after the filing of the affidavit, and shall have been signed by at least twenty-five per cent of the registered voters of the
town, who shall add to their signatures the street and number, if any, of their residences.
The town clerk, shall within twenty-four hours of receipt submit the petition to the registrars of voters in the town, and the registrars
shall within five working days certify thereon the number of signatures which are names of registered voters of the town.
SECTION 3. If the petition shall be found and certified by the town clerk to be sufficient he shall submit the same with his certificate
to the selectmen within five working days, and the selectmen shall within five working days give written notice of the receipt of the
certificate to the officer sought to be recalled and shall, if the officer does not resign within five days thereafter, order an election to be held
on a date fixed by them not less than sixty nor more than ninety days' after the date of the town clerk's certificate that a sufficient petition
has been filed: provided however that if any other town election is to occur within ninety days after the date of the certificate the selectmen
shall postpone the holding of the recall election to the date of such other election. No person shall be subject to recall if his term of office
expires within ninety days of the certificate. If a vacancy occurs in said office after a recall election has been ordered, the election shall
nevertheless proceed as provided in this section.
SECTION 4. Any officer sought to be removed may be a candidate to succeed himself, and unless he requests otherwise in writing,
the town clerk shall place his name on the ballot without nomination. The nomination of other candidates, the publication of the warrant
for the removal election, and the conduct of the same, shall all be in accordance with the provisions of law relating to elections, unless
otherwise provided in this act.
SECTION 5. The incumbent shall continue to perform the duties of his office until the recall election. If then re-elected, he shall
continue in office for the remainder of his unexpired term subject to recall as before, except as provided in this section. If not re-elected in
the recall election, he shall be deemed removed upon the qualification of his successor, who shall hold office during the unexpired term. If
the successor fails to qualify within five days after receiving notification of his election, the incumbent shall thereupon be deemed
removed and the office vacant.
SECTION 6. Ballots used in a recall election shall submit the following propositions in the order indicated:
For the recall of (name of officer).
Against the recall of (name of officer)
Immediately at the right of each proposition there shall be a square in which the voter, by making a cross mark (x) may vote for either of the
said proposition. Under the propositions shall appear the word "Candidates", the directions to the voters required by section forty-two of
Chapter fifty-four of the General Laws, and beneath this the names of candidates nominated in accordance with the provisions of law
relating to elections. If two-thirds of the votes cast upon the question of recall is in the affirmative, the candidate receiving the highest
number of votes shall be declared elected. If more than one-third of the votes on the question are in the negative, the ballots for candidates
need not be counted.
SECTION 7. No recall petition shall be filed against an officer within ninety days after he takes office, nor, in the case of an officer
subjected to a recall election and not recalled thereby, until at least ninety days after the election at whilch his recall was submitted to the
voters of the town.
SECTION 8. No person who has been recalled from an office or who has resigned from office while recall proceedings were
pending against him, shall be appointed to any town office within one year after such recall or such resignation.
SECTION 9. This act shall be submitted for acceptance to the voters of the Town of Lunenburg at the next annual town election or a
special town election, whichever occurs first, in the form of the following question which shall be placed on the official ballot at such
election- "Shall an act passed by the General Court in the year nineten hundred and eighty-two, entitled "An Act providing for recall
elections in the Town of Lunenburg' be accepted?" If a majority of the votes cast in answer to said question is in the affirmative, this act
shall take effect, but not otherwise. "Wording changed by Act of Legislature, Chapter 506 of the Acts of 1983. Approved by the Governor

YOUR SCHOOLS
BROOKS HOUSE: (Houses Office of School Superintendent)
The "Brooks House" and property was originally deeded to the Town of Lunenburg on December 3, 1937 by Ida
and Mary Brooks. In September 1946, when the school department was experiencing an overcrowded condition at the
elementary level, the school committee, "after careful deliberation, planned to solve the problem by seeking the
use of the so-called Brooks residence. The selectmen cooperated in granting permission for this house to be
taken over by the school department." (from the 1946 Town Report)
In checking the Town Report for. 1893, it is noted that Lunenburg was part of a four-town school union com-
prising the communities of Ashby, Lunenburg, Townsend, and Winchendon. In 1905, Winchendon withdrew, and Union
#24 remained a three-town union until July 1, 1954, when Lunenburg withdrew and had a full-time superintendent
of schools.

LUNENBURG PRIMARY SCHOOL:
This building was built in 1925 at a total cost of $54,000., including building, land, and equipment, to
house grades seven through twelve. In 1957, when the present high school was built, the building was
refurbished to contain grade four and two sections of grade five and was known as the Elementary Annex. From
1957 to 1968, of grade four and either one, two, or three sections of grade five were housed in the building.
During 1968-69, when double sessions were in existence, grade three attended in the AM session and grade four in
the PM session. From 1969 to 1978, grade three was located in this school. In 1978, the name was changed to
Lunenburg Primary School and housed kindergarten and grade one pupils and effective in the 1983-84 school year,
our pre-school program as well.

TURKEY HILL MIDDLE SCHOOL: (Currently houses grades 6-8)
"The new Turkey Hill Middle School is scheduled to open for classes on February 24, 1969. Initially, stu-
dents and teachers will occupy only the classroom wing while remaining work on the gym, cafetorium, and kitchen
is being completed. The complete building should be ready for occupancy by April 1, 1969. (Architect: Stoner

and the school administration are extremely pleased with the new school. As we become able to actually see the
language laboratory, the science laboratory, the beautiful library, the convertible classroom and many other
features, we felt assured that the Town will indeed be proud of the new addition to the Lunenburg School System
designed to assure that our children obtain the type of education so vitally needed in our world today." (from
the 1968 Town Report)

LUNENBURG HIGH SCHOOL: (Currently houses grades 9-12)
"On the basis of a complete and thorough set of educational specifications drawn up by Dr. Leo T. Doherty
of Worcester, in cooperation with members of the Lunenburg Jr.-Sr. High School Building Committee, the new
building was planned to provide classroom space for a maximum of 650 pupils. The gymnasium and auditorium leach
of which accommodates 600), the cafeteria which will take care of 300 at a sitting, and the other central facil-
ities such as homemaking and industrial arts departments, were considered adequate to meet the necessary demands
of a junior-senior high school population up to 1000 pupils. In other words, to accommodate a student enrollment
exceeding 650 pupils, regular classroom space must be added.
"The first bonds were issued on December 1, 1955. Later bonds totalling $110,000., were sold as the money
became needed. The terms of all the bonds is fifteen years.
"The building was ready for occupancy in 1957 at a total cost of $1,216,992.95. The town has acquired a
new school which is not only extremely functional but attractive as well. Absence of frills and full use of
available space make this building one of the most economical in Massachusetts and another tribute to its Archi-
tect, Mr. Harry J. Korslund." (from the 1958 Town Report)
In 1963, twelve classrooms were added to the building.

JANUARY 1983 - JUNE 1983:
We had a very successful last half of the school year in terms of student accomplishments and our ability to
meet their needs. We had a budget which allowed us to properly staff our schools and at the same time provide
our staff and students with the equipment and material necessary to provide a quality educational system. During
the year we were able to adjust many of the losses we had experienced during the preceding years. This was
accomplished by the town providing a budget with an 8.4% increase over the previous year. With this budget we
made tremendous strides in our programs and in providing the material necessary to accomplish our goals.
In May, 1983 the town voted the schools an increase of 4.3%. This increase was not sufficient to maintain
our staff and expenses at the previous levels. Rather than cut teachers and increase our class sizes, we made
the decision to cut expenses. Consequently, we cut our expenses over the previous year's expenditures by seventy
thousand plus dollars. This cut has severely curtailed our ability to properly fund our expenses this year.
SEPTEMBER 1983 - DECEMBER 1983:
Along with our losses in the expense portion of our budget, we also found ourselves with an increased
enrollment. For the first time in many, many years, we have more students this year than we had the year before.
We are not completely sure why or how this happened, but this placed added burdens on our financial situation.
In our attempt to realize sensible and proper class sizes, we cut the expense portion of our budget to find the
$180,000. we had to cut from our original request. In doing this we left many of our accounts with not the
proper amount to adequately assure ourselves that we would be able to accomplish all that needed to be done.
We must continue to give credit to the large number of people who continue to support our athletic programs.
This community continues to provide great financial resources both to the interscholastic program at the high
school and to the other sports programs in the community organized by the Park Department, Little League, Babe
Ruth League, Pop Warner League, etc. The young people of this community share in the contribution made by these
organizations with their participation and enthusiasm. We all enjoy the process when we see the benefits result-
ing from young people enjoying themselves in worthwhile endeavors.
We are very fortunate to have an atmosphere of support for our schools that we receive from our staff,
parents, students and administrators on a daily basis. Many of the problems that are faced by other school
systems are removed from us by us possessing quality educators, enlightened parental involvement, and informed
administration. In addition to these factors we are blessed with students who love, believe in, and commit them-
selves to receiving a quality educational experience. We attempt to demonstrate the results of all of the
individuals involved by productions in drama, music, art festivals, sports and academic competition. We have

THE FUTURE:
The future for the Lunenburg Public Schools remains critical only in terms of financing. I feel strongly
that we have demonstrated our commitment as a school system to providing this community with a quality school
system. We have demonstrated this by our support to retain quality educational professionals, by introducing
new programs that meet the needs of our ever increasing capabilities of our students, by our sensitivity of
monitoring our budgets to reflect staff adjustments and reorganization of our school structure and by demanding
from all who are employed no less than the best. We are however at the limit. We must now approach the issue
of how do we finance the schools in the future. We have tightened up the budget in every area possible; we are
demanding and receiving from staff every available ounce of energy they have to give. We cannot expect more
"fat" to be trimmed. There is no more that can be done. If more is to be cut then the citizens must accept
less as a product. If this happens, then it also happens in the areas of discipline, expectations, results and
more importantly, in pride.
We must look to a new method of financing the schools. What has happened under Proposition 21 in the
community is that the schools have steadfastly received less of the percentage of the tax base each year. This
is shown by the fact that in FY-80 we received a 77.6% of the tax dollar to support schools. In FY-84, we
received 60.0% of the tax dollar for schools. In four short years we have lost almost 18% of the tax dollars
that used to be allotted to schools. This means that we have needed to tighten up with the hope that we would
not lose too much. We are however at that point. Consequently, we need to decide how to make funds available
for a quality school system.
I submit that we must as a community need to look at school funding in the following manner:
1. At least 65% of the tax increase allowed by Proposition 2½ be applied to schools.
2. All of the money appropriated as Chapter 70 (school aid) monies be available to schools.
3. All school transportation monies returned to the community be made available to the schools.
It may be conceivable that when these three sources of revenues are combined, there may be more money
available for schools than necessary. However, I will indicate that I would never submit a budget for schools
that was more than what was necessary. I do feel however that the increase in state aid has been increased in
order to support the losses in school financing under Proposition 21. In any event, schools in Lunenburg should
not be financed by a formula of "what is left" to give to the schools, but rather what monies are needed to
continue or return to a quality school system.

LUNENBURG PRE-SCHOOL:
The Lunenburg Pre-School Program, which was formerly a SPECIAL NEEDS program, has been expanded this year.
Originally the pre-school served only three, four, and five year olds who were determined to be in need of man-
dated special education services.
Through a grant written by Mrs. Janet Maki, funds were secured to develop an integrated pre-school program
which includes a number of "regular education" tuition students and some "special education" students. The pro-
gram is known as LEIP (Lunenburg Early Integration Program). We currently boast a pre-school population of
fourteen children.
The major goal of this program is to ready all children involved for the "regular education" kindergarten
at the Primary School. The pre-school focuses its attention on socialization and readiness skills. There is a
heavy infusion of language development throughout all readiness and socialization activities. Special services
are provided through the special needs department for those children who require such assistance. All children,
however, in addition to the regular curriculum, are treated to art, arts and crafts, physical education, music,
and cooking activities.
As a result of the reorganization at the Elementary School, Mrs. Janet Maki became the pre-school teacher
this year. Mrs. Vicki Whittenkraus serves as a grant-funded Language Communications Specialist for the pre-
schoolers.
Pre-schoolers attend a class five mornings a week. The program has been a great success. It is our hope
to be able to accept additional tuition students in future years and to expand this program.

TEAMING FOR READING AT THE FIRST GRADE:
Reading instruction at the first grade level has been re-structured. All children are now grouped homo-
geneously for reading and move from homeroom teacher to reading teacher for approximately two hours (reading
block) a day.
The purpose of this ability grouping is to insure a more individualized approach for instruction. It also
allows a child to move comfortably at his/her own pace. Homogeneous grouping prevents the "pushing" of a child
who is not ready to move on and also guarantees that a child who is ready to move at a faster pace may do SO.
Instruction is geared to the specific level of each group. Materials are carefully matched to each group's
needs. The first grade teachers constantly monitor the reading achievement of their students and plan their
instruction accordingly.
The children have adjusted very well to this new structure and appear to enjoy the opportunity of having
exposure to more than one teacher in the first grade.

Over the last several years, the high school has made important and substantial changes in its curriculum.
The purpose of these changes has been to improve the quality of instruction and the level of student academic
performance.
104
===PAGE 107===
Two guidelines have been followed. The first has been to insure that all students receive a sound basic
education. Secondly, the curriculum has been reviewed to insure its flexibility: courses must both challenge
students to the limit of their intellectual capabilities, and at the same time encourage them to explore the
full range of academic opportunities at the high school.
To achieve the first objective, students at the grade 9 level are guided to enroll in mostly basic academic
courses, to insure that they have fundamental academic and intellectual skills. Secondly, courses have been
added in the various departments with emphasis upon instruction in basic skills in all areas. Thirdly, the
flexibility of the curriculum has been improved by the addition of a number of courses which students may take
for a semester (half-year). The use of semester courses encourages students to explore areas of the curriculum
in which they might not be able to schedule themselves for a full year. This will allow (in future years) the
establishment of distribution guidelines under which students will explore all areas of the comprehensive
curriculum.
There are a number of new course developments in the various departments which the high school is pleased
to implement as part of its overall academic objectives.
ENGLISH AND WRITING PROGRAM:
The English Department has increased its emphasis on writing skills in order to improve the writing of all
students. Intensive English classes of small groups have been established at each grade level to allow more
individual instruction for students needing special help in reading and writing. Across all grades and levels
of ability, emphasis is increased upon frequent expository writing assignments and documentation of student
progress in composition.
A Business English course has been established with the cooperation of the Business and English Depart-
ments. The purpose of this course is to allow students to blend their interest in writing for business purposes
with the reading and interpretation of literature taught by a member of the English faculty. This cooperative
effort has been most successful in focusing student's attention on the needs for improved writing for business
situations, as well as the appreciation of literature as a personal satisfaction for all students.
SOCIAL STUDIES:
A number of changes have been made in Social Studies to insure basic academic skills, and to increase
student exploration of the social sciences. The basic grade nine course, World Cultures, was revised by the
department in the summer of 1983, including a new textbook. The revision of the course retains the study of
some ancient history, but also provides new studies of areas of the world including developing and third world
nations. This will provide a better global perspective for students.
