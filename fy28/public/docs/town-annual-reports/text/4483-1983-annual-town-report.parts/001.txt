THE 1983 ANNUAL TOWN REPORT IS DEDICATED TO
BERTHA SUSAN BROWN
"Lunenburg, with its high elevation and open expanses, became
known for its healthy air and healthy people" begins one chapter in a
local history book. Perhaps no one exemplifies this better than Bertha
Susan Brown of Northfield Road who celebrated her 100th birthday in
December.
Miss Brown is the possessor of the Boston Post Cane award, signifying
her status as the oldest resident of Lunenburg. Coincidentally, her grand-
father, Luke Chase, for whom Chase Road was named, was the first holder
of the Cane.
In fine physical and mental health, Bertha Brown is a treasurehouse
of local history, which she is willing to share with interested listeners.
Miss Brown is a retired elementary school teacher. Her contributions to
society continue even today as she knits and donates articles to charity
organizations.
Lunenburg congratulates and affectionately salutes Bertha Susan Brown.

Board of Selectmen: Seated Left to right- August N. Misner, Chairman James T. Van Buren and Ann P. Hall.
Standing - Clerk Francelina C. Vaughan and Executive Secretary Barry J. Stell.
For the-Selectmen, 1983 was a year of change and challenge -- change reflected in new personnel who
face a continuing challenge of maintaining a level of expected services with a shrinking revenue base.
Dennis Carrier succeeded Jim Deming as Fire Chief. Al Gauthier was replaced by Leon Langille as
Veterans' Agent. Upon the resignation of Austin Fitzsimmons, Roger Williams was appointed as Building
Inspector. After an extended leave of absence, Rick Eley resigned and Barry Stell became the new Executive
Secretary. In addition, Ed Harriman was appointed Auditor to replace the late Jim Bascom.
We are confident that the new appointees will serve Lunenburg with as much diligence and ability as their
predecessors, whose total service encompassed 122 years.
The challenge of Prop 2½ -- providing services with less money -- continued in 1983. Although the
inflationary spiral slowed, expenses continued to rise in both labor and materials.
Examples abound throughout the town. Although the 1983 Town Meeting expressed a desire to replace
the Holman Street culvert, obtaining the materials and skills necessary has proved impossible within the
dollar amount voted. The police contract for the current fiscal year remains unsettled. Replacing needed
capital equipment continues to task the imagination and resources of both department heads and Board
members.
In an effort to contain costs and increase revenues, the Board has negotiated a new street lighting
agreement; voted new fees for the Sanitary Landfill, Automatic Amusement Devices and Dog Licenses;
auctioned unneeded and unwanted town property; and even shortened the length of this award winning Town
Report.
4

The Board feels strongly that the town cannot afford the services it has come to expect without paying for
the same. Therefore, we have formed ad hoc committees on communications and computers in an attempt to
limit costs while increasing services. Grants have been (and are being) applied for under federal and state
programs to provide and/or replace services for the townspeople.
To preserve the character of our community we must act on our own initiative before that initiative is lost
to outside regulators. We recognize the intrusion of this external regulation into local government has
become a fact of municipal life in the 1980's. For this reason, the Board encourages the town to accept the
provisions of the General Laws allowing for professional management of the town's financial affairs and to
implement the recommendations of the Town Management Study Committee to establish a strong Executive
Secretary and approve a Town Charter Commission.

Auditor
I have examined, for the period July 1, 1983 to December 31, 1983, the books and accounts of the officer of
the Town of Lunenburg who is entrusted with receipt, custody or expenditure of money, including all original
bills and vouchers on which money has been paid from the Treasury. I have also reconciled the Treasurer's
cash balances and investments (see below). My examinations were made in accordance with generally
accepted auditing standards. These present fairly the balances in these accounts as of December 31, 1983.
Consistent with the practices of many municipalities in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, the Town of
Lunenburg has not maintained historical cost records of its property, plant and equipment. I have recom-
mended in a letter to the Board of Selectmen, dated March 6, 1984, that this be done to conform with generally
accepted accounting principles and provide insurable values. During my audit I have made the following
recommendations and they have been implemented to provide better control:
1. A standard weekly payroll time card approved by department heads.
2. Department approval on every original invoice and a standard voucher approval form.
3. Review and account for all pre-printed sequential checks including those cancelled or voided each
month.
4. Correction of inconsistencies on pay for part-time employees on holidays.

Planning Board
Planning Board: Seated - Marion M. Benson and Chairman Charles H. B. Arning. Standing - James A. Mann
and James R. Sharkey. Not present when photo was taken was James M. Sudolnik.
The major goal of the Planning Board is to begin to implement the objectives of the Master Plan as
presented to the Town at the last Annual Town Meeting. We have already taken positive steps to begin this
process.
Lunenburg, in regard to many areas of concern, is at a cross-roads where hard decisions need to be made.
The Planning Board is one of the public boards that is beginning to make these hard choices between
conflicting interests. The process of public hearings is an essential ingredient in the formulation of these
choices. Historically, the Planning Board hearings have been poorly attended. It is critical that the Townspeo-
ple be involved in this decision-making process. In order for the Town to continue to preserve its heritage as a
rural, agricultural community while at the same time being open to innovative opportunities to expand the tax
base and create appropriate housing opportunities, the citizens of Lunenburg must become more involved in
this process.
Our plans for the coming year are to push for a special committee to study the Town Center, not only the
Town Hall, but also the traffic and scenic elements of the Center as well; to conduct a physical survey of the
watershed area as outlined in the Master Plan to increase the zoning from one to two acres in specific
locations where there is a need to further protect our water resources (based on the outcome of the survey);
and if this is passed, to develop a Cluster Zoning By-law which will allow a builder to better utilize large parcels
of acreage for housing opportunities that lessen the impact of sewage and water use on the water resources of
the Town.
The Planning Board signed 22 plans that did not require subdivision approval generating $220 dollars in
revenue for the Town. The Planning Board approved a new subdivision off Flat Hill Road by Mr. Jouko Kulju
called Oakwood Estates. In addition, the Planning Board has approved Canterbury Drive and the subdivision
of the same name.

Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors: left to right - Secretary Jacqueline A. Keeler, Chairman William F. Reed, Bernard J. Paton
and Kathryn M. Herrick.
The Board of Assessors wishes to thank the property owners of the Town for their cooperation during the
past two years. The year 1982 was hectic with the revaluation of the Town being completed by Robert J.
Finnegan and Associates, Inc. and all the attendant hearings and abatements which were conducted.
The end result of the revaluation was certified as being correct by the Department of Revenue, and based
on the number of requests for abatements, we feel that a proper and equal job of assessing valuations was
done. We have 4,000 parcels of property in the Town, and 212 requests for abatements were filed (5.3%). After
consideration and action on these requests for abatements, 8 taxpayers have appealed our decisions and filed
for appellate tax court hearings.
The main subject of the abatement requests was the revaluation of the land in the Town. The homes and
buildings were not, in the main, overvalued. The problem with the land was that Lunenburg had a long history
of extremely low assessments on land. This was done to enable the taxpayers to hold large tracts of land at
little expense and to maintain the rural character of the Town.
The State Department of Revenue insisted that the law be observed and that all land be assessed at its full
and fair market value. This value was determined by using land sale prices over the years 1979 to 1981, and
these figures established your land values for 1982.
The tax rate for Fiscal Year 1984 was set at $19.92 with the total valuation of the Town being $193,289,258.
We are working under the fiscal restraints of Proposition 2½ without any great reductions in services to you.
That portion of the budget to be raised by taxation can only be increased by 2½% annually, and this law is
forcing local and state governments to look to other sources of funds to continue operaton of municipal
services.

We wish to apprise you of the need for a mini-revaluation and certification of the assessed valuation of the
Town for Fiscal Year 1986 which starts July 1, 1985. We are required by law to conduct this evaluation every
three years, and will conduct this revaluation starting January 2, 1985 through June 31, 1985. We will request
the funds via an article at the Annual Town Meeting in May 1984. Upon completion of submission of bids to
this office, we intend to engage a revaluation contractor to provide a field review of each piece of property with
buildings sold during years 1982-1984 and all new construction completed during the same period. This
information will be used to update the current assessed valuations and should satisfy the Department of
Revenue's requirements for recertification.
We do wish to report that housing starts are up and land sales show a steady increase in the cost of
building lots. The average price of a new home is increasing from the mid-fifties to the seventy-thousand-
dollar range. We find that ranch-style homes (single story) are not as popular as story-and-a-half or two-story
homes in new construction being built throughout the Town.

Park Commission
The past year was one of evaluation, decision and failure. We took a long hard look at our facilities,
equipment, operative policies, progams and financial resources. It was clearly evident that the Park Depart-
ment was approaching a point where salary expense and maintenance costs would result in the elimination of
recreational activities. We made a decision not to cut or eliminate recreational programs, but to control costs
and seek additional funding. We recognized that the greatest resource of any community is its youth and that
the money had to be channeled into recreational programs. To accomplish this service, charges were
increased and user fees were proposed.
In an attempt to upgrade our facilities, we applied for a federal grant to add facilities and repair current
ones. Our application failed to attract much needed dollars. The town beach house is in need of repair and our
emphasis at town meetings will be to acquire these funds. We continue to apply bandaids where major surgery
is needed and we will pursue all alternatives to upgrade our facilities.
We would like to take this opportunity to acknowledge those individuals who make this all possible. We
would like to thank Greg Bingham for his time and effort and we all wish him well in his new career endeavor.
Tom Leppala has replaced Greg and in his first six months has demonstrated his creativity and initiative. Jerry
Beloin, the town beach director, supervised our highly visible beach program. Usage of this town beach was
up 20% from the prior year. The summer recreation program administered by Dick Stachowicz continued to

Highway Department
America's roads are falling apart. Half the coun-
try's paved roads will require major reconstruction
by the end of the decade, but Highway and road
repair and maintenance funds simply have not kept
pace with the deterioration. The states' meager
maintenance funds are spent on newer streets and
roads at the expense of the older ones. Many of the
older roads have deteriorated to a point beyond
maintenance help, and require rehabilitation.
Potholes and other highway deterioration lead to
greater deterioration at a faster pace, longer gas
mileage, damage vehicles, and add generally to veh-
icles' wear-and-tear.
Much of the Federal and State funds are derived
from gasoline taxes. Over the years, repair and
maintenance funds have shrunk because gasoline
consumption has been declining in recent years. As
the price has gone up, many motorists have curbed
their driving. At the same time, newer vehicles get
better mileage, requiring less gasoline. And, high
inflation in construction costs has shrunk the
amount of construction that can be financed from
existing highway taxes.
Skimpy budgets often translate into minimal
highway and street repair and have precipitated a
search for new methods and technologies to
improve upon the costly total reconstruction
system.
George G. Munyon, Jr., Highway Superintendent
Once a crack in a road develops, moisture seeps through to eventually erode the sub-base material
supporting the concrete or asphalt road. After a couple of season, including several freezes and thaws,
thermal contraction and expansion eat away the pavement, leaving a pothole. In the past the only effective
solution was Highway Reconstruction. The pavement is broken up and hauled away, the roadbed is leveled
and smoothed and new supports laid, then the new pavement is laid. Because of cost, most repair attempts -
involving a waterproof sealer and an asphalt overlay - are much less complicated and costly. But neither the
sealer nor the overlay treat the basic structural problems involved in reflective cracking.
New repair methods are already in use in many areas of the country, with the emphasis on lower cost and
less interruption of traffic flow. One such system uses high-strength glass fiber reinforced fabric to cover
cracks. This Department has also experimented with a "Chipseal" process to repair Lunenburg's roads. We
hope these new methods are approved by the State authorities as they will speed road maintenance in the
future as well as stretch their budget dollars allocated for street repair.

Town Management Study Committee Report
ARTICLE 32 of the Lunenburg Annual Town Meeting Warrant, June 1983 authorized the Board of
Selectmen and the Town Moderator to appoint a five member study committee to investigate the
establishment of a Selectmen-Town Manager form of government and to consider expansion of the Board of
Selectmen, the establishment of a separate Board of Health, or any other change to the executive branch it
deemed advisable; said committee to report back to the next Annual Town Meeting, provided that at least
thirty days prior to Town Meeting an informative public hearing is held. Pursuant to this vote, the following
persons were appointed by the Selectmen and the Moderator to serve on such committee: Diane J. Allen,
Melissa D. Cobb, Herbert E. Dunnington, Walter B. Keeler, and James W. Rockefeller.
In the course of its study, our Committee met with the Selectmen and most other town boards and
committees to solicit their input and suggestions relative to professional management for the town.
Additionally, we met with Town Managers from the towns of Acton and Winchendon. We collected data and
materials from the Executive Office of Communities and Development, Division of Community Services of the
Commonwealth of Massachusetts, along with much pertinent information from other towns.
CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
The ever growing complexities of town government are resulting in a time and energy commitment
beyond the scope of part-time administrators. We find that our Selectmen spend approximately twenty hours
a week carrying out routine administrative responsibilities, leaving little time for long-range planning or for
research necessary for informed decision making. We conclude that some relief should be forthcoming as
soon as possible.
This committee believes that the "Town Manager with a Strong Charter" form of government would give
our townspeople the maximum benefit and maximum efficiency. This was also the finding of the study
committee of 1966-67 on the same subject. An analogy, we think, is the professional administrator that School
Committees employ in the form of a School Superintendent. It is difficult to imagine a school committee
administering a school system, yet we ask part-time Selectmen to administer the major balance of town
government.
Our research indicated that there were three avenues to the establishment of an administrative
management post in local town government:
1.
Establishing the position of Executive Secretary, Town Administrator or Town Manager by
securing the adoption of a Home Rule Charter.
2.
Securing the Adoption of a Special Act of the Legislature.
3.
Adopting a Bylaw or Vote of the Town Meeting, thus establishing the Executive Secretary
position as found in Massachusetts General Laws, which provides as follows:
SECTION 23A CHAPTER 41: A town may by vote or by-law, authorize and empower the select-
men to appoint an executive secretary who may be appointed by them for a term of one or three
years and to remove him at their discretion. An executive secretary appointed under the pro-
visions of this section shall be sworn to the faithful performance of his duties. During the time
that he holds office he shall hold no elective town office, but he may be appointed by the select-
men or, with the approval, by any other town officer, board, committee or commission, to any
other town office or position consistent with his office. He shall receive such aggregate compen-
sation, not exceeding the amount appropriated therefor, as the selectmen may determine. He
shall act by and for the selectmen in any matter which they may assign to him relating to the
administration of the affairs of the town or of any town office or department under their super-
vision and control, or, with the approval of the selectmen, may perform such other duties as may
be requested of him by any other town officer, board, committee or commission. (1956, 145).

An immediate form of relief would be the adoption of option three, Section 23A Chapter 41 of the General
Laws as previously outlined. This would allow the selectmen to legally delagate any of their lawful duties to the
executive secretary. This form is generally known as the "Weak Town Manager" form and has been and is
being employed by more and more of the towns in our size and population category. The plan has the obvious
advantage of relieving the part-time selectmen of a tangle of administrative details thereby freeing time to
devote to the study of major town problems and long-range planning. The executive secretary can assist the
selectmen by supplying information relative to questions the board must act upon. The executive secretary
may also have a particular value as a unifying force in the town government by representing an important
channel of communications between the board of selectmen and town officials and citizens.
This committee also feels the town should consider the establishment of a Charter Commission to work
toward the adoption of a "Home Rule Charter." This commission would look at the entire town government
along with Lunenburg's by-laws and recommend to the town changes and additions that would maximize the
efficiency and services of local government. Under the provision of the Home Rule Amendment, Article
LXXXIX of the Massachusetts Constitution, any city or town, upon petition of 15% of the registered voters, may
vote to elect a nine member charter commission to form a new charter. During the ten month, or eighteen
month, period after its election, the commission prepares a comprehensive charter document establishing the
framework for the administration and re-organizaiton of local government structure. To become effective, a
charter proposal must be adopted by a majority electorate at the next annual town election. We feel this
process would take approximatley two to three years to accomplish.
With regard to increasing the membership of the Board of Selectmen and any change in the Board of
Health, we recommend that such changes be reconsidered at a later date, if warranted, after the establishment
of executive secretary or a Charter Commission.
This committee wishes to thank the Board of Selectmen for the opportunity to serve in this study and
thank all who contributed to our efforts.

Montachusett Regional Vocational
Technical School District
Nineteen hundred eighty-three was a banner year for Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical
School in many ways.
Graduation in June was held in the Field House at Mount Wachusett Community College. Two hundred
graduates with parents, friends and faculty attended the impressive ceremony. An honorary vocational
certificate was presented to Morrill Sprague, school committee member from Harvard, an individual who has
been outstandingly supportive of Vocational Education and Monty Tech over many years.
Five hundred twenty-five applications were received for grade nine, three hundred thirty were accepted,
and thus the largest freshman class in the history of the school entered. The total student enrollment on
October 1, 1983, was one thousand fifty-nine.
Students have been active in the National Honor Society, the Vocational and Industrial Clubs of America
(VICA), the Vocational Programs Advisory comittees, two Bloodmobile drives, and collection of "Quarters for
Charity". Two hundred twenty-six pints of blood were donated to the Red Cross. Over $800 was collected in
the annual "Quarters for Christmas" drive and will be divided equally between the towns of Harvard and
Sterling. The District has reason to be proud of the recognition of merit of the Monty Tech students who won
many awards during the year.
The Food Service students took a trip to Bermuda during which they had the opportunity to tour
Stonington Junior College, Paget, Bermuda, where a special program was presented for their benefit. All
costs were paid by the students who earned the money through school activities.
Federal grant money, received through the Department of Education, made possible the purchase of new
equipment which would have been otherwise inaccesible. A new computer room with eighteen micro
computers is now available for students. To maximize the use of this room, a program on computer usage was
instituted for the faculty. During the summer, a program was offered for District students which included
utilization of these computers and other new equipment, and allowed those interested students to have
hands-on experience in selected vocational fields.
Three evening programs were held for the public during the year: Parents' Night, Career Awareness
Night, and our Annual Open House. All were well attended by enthusiastic throngs who appeared keenly
interested in the programs taught, the equipment used, and the work being performed by the students.
Kind donors continue to donate useful gifts to the school. Matthews Chevrolet of Fitchburg and Mathieu
Ford of Winchendon each loaned cars for use in the Driver Education course. Other industrial and business
firms made contributions.
Plans for expansion to include the three towns of Orange, Petersham and Westminster have continued to
occupy much of the District Committe's time. Final articles for town warrants have been submitted, and if
approved, the District will be comprised of two cities and eleven towns.
Once again, the students, staff and District Committee extend their gratitudes to all of the cities and towns
of the Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District for their continuing support of the
standards and goals of the school.

Town Forest Committee
Town Forest Committee: Seated left to right - Armas S. J. Heino, Chairman Scott W. Foster and Robert A.
Garneau.
The Town Forest Committee has continued, during the past year, to study the feasibility and methods of
managing the Town Forest off Gilchrest Street. The services of a professional forester were investigated.
Interviews were held with three individuals, one of whom, Bob Leupold, was chosen to aid the Committee.
Preliminary studies of the Gilchrest Street forest indicate the need for systematic thinning of crowded
growth and also for removal of poor or diseased trees. The conclusion of Mr. Leupold and the Committee was
that our need is to begin with a test program on a twenty to thirty acre plot.
The goal of the Committee is to have such a plot marked for thinning and ready to be put out to bid by
August, 1984. One independent contractor will be chosen to cut the plot in the fall of 1984. Cordwood will be
the only product harvested.
Proceeds from the first attempt, and subsequent ones, are earmarked for the surveying of existing town
owned lands, controlling erosion on and stabilizing existing forest roads, and payment for the forest
consultant.

Town Clerk, Tax Collector, Treasurer
It is with pleasure that I submit my first report as Town
Clerk, Tax Collector and Treasurer.
EDUCATION
Through completion of the first year of the certification
processes for Treasurer-Collectors as well as Town Clerks,
and through monthly meetings, seminars and schools, much
information is gained for the Town's benefit. More impor-
tant, a network is being set up providing a resource which
can be called upon to help deal with the unusual situations
which seem to occur on a regular basis.
APPOINTED vs. ELECTED OFFICIALS
More and more communities are opting to appoint many
officials which they used to elect, their reasoning being
that the appointive process would assure the selection of a
more qualified person. What appears to have been overlooked
is that the independence of the officials in performing
their duties must remain an important goal.
It seems that the well-intentioned should have more faith in
the ability of their electorate to choose best. Their
Robert J. Ebersole
community would be better served if they were to devote
their energies to seeing that elected officials receive full
support in their efforts to improve his or her expertise.
Food for thought for the voters in any community is the indisputable fact that each time they
choose to have appointed an official whom they had previously elected, they surrender a piece
of their share of the democratic process, and reduce the responsiveness of those in office to
the needs and desires of the people.
STAFFING
Due to action at the June Town Meeting, the Administra-
tion budget for the three offices was cut approximately

FREE CASH
Free cash, or more correctly, "Fund Balance Available for Appropriation", is the total amount of
money left over at the end of the Town's fiscal year, after deducting outstanding taxes and other
encumbrances. Each year Lunenburg continues to spend part of this to offset the budget.
The Town does not spend all of the free cash, as is true in private industry which maintains
reserves against future contingencies instead of paying dividends (reducing taxes) or spending it
(increasing budgets). It has been recommended that a community maintain between 3% and 5% of its
budget as free cash. The amount retained after the 1983 Annual Town Meeting was $220,883, or 3.5%
of the $6,370,481 budget voted.
Not only does this provide for a fund for emergencies, it also provides a source of income to the
Town in the form of interest. In the event that the tax bills are late due to revaluation delays
(as occured in FY 83), it allows the Town to postpone or not require borrowing, thereby saving
interest payments.
One of the major sources of free cash is that amount of free cash which was not spent in a prior
year. It is a NON-RECURRING RESOURCE, and, if spent, is gone forever. In essence, it is a savings
account. If the Town uses its savings to pay its light bill, the savings will be gone, and next
monththe light bill will be there again. It is better to restrict the growth of the budget and pay
bills out of current income, than to spend out of savings and hurt Lunenburg's security.
PROP 2½, THE VOTER AND EDUCATION
Since this tax cutting referendum was passed, the law has been fine tuned SO as to correct some of
the inequities which were in the original law. It is important to realize that while one provision
of 2½ placed a limit on the tax rate at $25.00, there was another limit imposed. A Town may not
increase its total levy more than 2½ over the prior year. This is a vital part of the law to
assure that cities and towns cannot arbitrarily and WITHOUT VOTER APPROVAL, increase taxes by
inflating the valuation base through revaluation. In fact, just as there may be a vote to override
2½, the law allows the town to vote to order a CUT in the tax levy. It is also significant to note
that for FY 83, 48% of the cities and towns in Massachusetts had a tax rate under $20.00. The
focus of the Town should not always be on the total amount that the Town may tax, but also to look
for ways to increasestate aid, local receipts and federal aid, as well as to determine what
services are necessary for the town to provide.
On the subject of state aid, Chapter 70 assistance to schools is often claimed to belong to the
schools. However, such assistance is merely the conduit through which the state distributes aid to
communities. Officials in the State House admit (albeit reluctantly) that this route was chosen
to make it look as if the current administration is fulfilling its campaign promises to give aid to
schools. However, in reality this aid is. only LOCAL REVENUE SHARING, to be spent as the Town sees
fit. As an alternative, the state would do well by sending additional assistance directly to the
schools without reducing aid to cities and towns, which in turn would further reduce reliance on

the property tax to fund education. In fact, by reviewing pre and post-2½ state aid to education

UNFUNDED PENSION LIABILITY
Due to growing concern for the unfunded pension liability in the State, which has been estimated
at 9 to 12 BILLION DOLLARS, as well as the perceived abuse of disability retirements, the Legisla-
ture passed two new laws which may help address the problem. The first made uniform the rules and
regulations that apply to the 105 retirement boards across the state, and provides for a more
impartial medical review system. The second law increased the amount of money available by
increasing employee contributions, expanding investment opportunities and reducing interest paid
to persons who leave the system before retirement age.
While this is an encouraging turn of events, there is much more that needs to be done to control
pension costs. One important recommendation currently under review, is a tightening of the Heart
and Lung provisions for police and fire personnel, by requiring participation in an ongoing physical
fitness health program. Lunenburg should continue to contribute to the fund set up in 1982 to
offset the Town's share of this continuing liability. As was stated at that time, eventually the
Town will be able to appropriate the interest gained, to reduce the amount that would otherwise
have to be raised by taxation.
