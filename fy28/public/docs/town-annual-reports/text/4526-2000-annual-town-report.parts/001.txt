MEMORIAM
WILBUR "BILL" BARNEY
1915 - 2000
Bill was a lifelong resident of Lunenburg who served on the Cemetery
Commission for 6 years. He had two brothers Grover (former Chief of
Police in Town) and Floyd (former Principal of Lunenburg High School).
Bill and his father started a fruit stand on Massachusetts Avenue which
eventually led to Bill building and operating Barney's Restaurant & Dairy
Bar with his wife Therese from 1948 to 1971. "Barney's" was a great place to eat and on
Sundays one could always have a heated discussion of Town politics. Bill and Therese
also owned and operated school bus contracts which serviced the Town for 21 years. If
you were fortunate enough to ride their buses, on the last day of school the bus stopped
at Barney's for a delicious ice cream sundae. Bill will be remembered for his generosity
and kind spirit.
LEONA M. BERTRAM
1920 - 2000
Leona "Lee" Bertram first came to the Town of Lunenburg in 1952 as a
summer resident. Originally a resident of Lynn, she and her husband
moved to Saugus and began to raise their family. The camp on Hickory
Hills Lake that she and her husband Mel purchased was a dream come
true. After just a few short summers in Lunenburg, she and her family
decided to make Lunenburg their year round home. Leona loved the community and her
little cottage on the Lake, she especially treasured the beautiful sunsets she enjoyed
every evening. Leona "Lee" Bertram served the Town from 1969 until her retirement in
1983. Lee was employed by the Police Department as the Police Chief's secretary and
also served as the department's first female matron officer. Leona was known
throughout her career as a remarkable and dedicated person. After retiring from the
department she remained a member of the Lunenburg Police Relief Association and
became a Friend of the Eagle House. Leona and Mel raised five children together and
several of their children and grandchildren have chosen to remain in Lunenburg, the
Town Leona loved so much.
DORIS LAHTI
1912 - 2000
Doris was a public health and school nurse for the Town of Lunenburg for
21 years before her retirement in 1975. She was a member of the
Lunenburg Women's Club and served on its board of directors for many
years. She was also a member and past treasurer of the Lunenburg
Friendly Seniors, a director and corresponding secretary of the Lunenburg
Community Council, a member of the Lunenburg Historical Society, an active member of
the Lunenburg High School Alumni Association and the Burbank Hospital School of
Nursing Alumni Association. Doris enjoyed gardening very much her vegetable and
flower garden were her "pride". She was an avid cribbage player and enjoyed playing
"skat" with friends every week. Her pleasurable hobby was knitting.

BOARD OF SELECTMEN/SEWER COMMISSIONERS
2000 began with a number of changes within this office, of note is the hiring of the Chief
Administrative Financial Officer (CAFO). A number of interviews and meetings were held
by the CAFO Screening Committee, which consisted of representatives of the Board of
Selectmen, School Committee, Assessors and the Planning Board. Three applicants were
interviewed by the Board which resulted in the hiring of William J. Gustus to fill the
position.
Town Meeting approved a number of Articles for building studies and plans pertaining to
the Library and Police/Fire Public Safety Facility which resulted in the evolvement of
the Capital Facilities Plan. In accordance with the Town Charter, the CAFO has been
working diligently with these Town departments, the School department and committees
to determine a 10-year implementation schedule and funding plan for the next 30 years.
It is anticipated that this will be presented to the voters at the next Annual Election and
Town Meeting.
The bidding procedure for the construction phase of the sewer system has begun and has
been divided into two stages, the first being Contract I which is the Whalom area and
second is Contract II, the center of Town. It is anticipated that the Whalom area
construction will commence in the spring and the Town Center in the summer of 2001.
The Town has also been awarded a $590,000 Community Development Block Grant to
assist homeowners with connection charges. Eligibility will be based upon specific
criteria and applications are expected to be available in the spring.
The Town has also received a matching grant of $35,000 for the exterior rehabilitation of
Town Hall. The renovations will include siding repairs, painting, shutter restoration and
rehabilitation of the tower and clock. Construction is anticipated to commence in the
spring and be completed by fall 2001.
In conclusion, this Board would like to thank the Lunenburg/Townsend Rotary Club for
Christmas decorations in the Gazebo, the Lunenburg Grange for the Town Hall summer
window boxes and the Turkey Hill Garden Club for the Town Hall Christmas decorations.
The Selectmen would also like to acknowledge all of the individuals who work so hard for
the Town and those who volunteer their time and commitment by serving on the various
Boards, Commissions and Committees within the Town.

CHIEF ADMINISTRATIVE FINANCIAL OFFICER
William J. Gustus
The year 2000 marked the implementation of the new Lunenburg Town Charter. My
tenure as Chief Administrative Financial Officer began with meetings with every elected
board and commission and all department heads. I have been very pleased with the
cooperation I have received from everyone connected with Lunenburg town government.
Together we have worked to implement the Town Charter.
9
===PAGE 13===
During the course of the year we have evaluated the financial operations of the town and
have implemented a number of procedures to insure the most cost effective procurement
of goods and services. Specifically, we have implemented an encumbrance accounting
system and a comprehensive purchase order system for all town departments. We now
procure 3 price quotations for virtually every item or service that we purchase on behalf
of the town. We have taken greater advantage of collective purchasing opportunities
offered by the state and other public purchasing collaboratives thereby insuring that we
buy at the lowest possible prices. We have also consolidated the payroll functions of
both the school department and all other town departments under the direction of the
Town Accountant in accordance with the provisions of the Charter thereby eliminating a
costly duplication of effort.
We have sought and secured several state grants for special town projects. We were
awarded $590,000 to assist homeowners in connecting to the new sewer, $35,000 to
assist in the exterior renovation of Town Hall, $29,000 to buy new fire safety equipment
and $25,000 for a new police patrolman.
The year 2000 was also a year of planning. We will be presenting to the next Annual
Town Meeting plans to consolidate the highway, sewer, parks, cemetery and facilities

CENTRAL MASSACHUSETTS MOSQUITO CONTROL
Kenneth Courtemanche, Superintendent Timothy Deschamps, Assistant Superintendent
The Central Massachusetts Mosquito Control Project (CMMCP) currently provides its
services to 29 cities and towns throughout Middlesex and Worcester counties. The
project's headquarters is located at 111 Otis Street, Northboro MA (508) 393-3055.
Tours of the headquarters or visits to field work sites may be arranged by calling the
office in advance.
The CMMCP practices Integrated Mosquito Management (IMM), blending state of the art
methods and techniques with expertise, experience, and scientific research to provide
our member communities with modern, environmentally sound, cost-effective mosquito
control.
During 2000 the Project conducted five hundred and sixty-two (562) adult mosquito
spraying operations in residential and recreational areas, and four hundred and forty-
four (444) larviciding operations. Twenty one thousand, seven hundred and nineteen
and nineteen (21,719) catch basins were treated with larvicidal briquettes to control the
mosquitoes that seek out these cool dark wet areas to breed. Two thousand, nine
hundred and twenty-two (2,922) culverts were cleaned in an attempt to eliminate
unnecessary standing water. This work was done in conjunction with cleaning, clearing,
and digging of eighty four thousand, one hundred and thirty two (84,132) feet of
streams, brooks and ditches. This represents almost sixteen miles of waterways which
were cleaned and improved.
Four ponds were cleaned and redug during 2000 to make them once again suitable for
native fish, frogs, and other wetlands wildlife and vegetation. Our pond cleaning projects
have been extremely successful at reducing the mosquito breeding in these areas, while
at the same time restoring the pond's value for fire fighting, agriculture, recreation
and/or aesthetics.
The Mosquito Awareness program which we offer to elementary schools in our district
has become very popular. Project staff meet with students and teachers to discuss
mosquito biology, mosquito habitat, and control procedures. Much of the presentation is
directed towards what the children and their families can do to prevent mosquitoes from
breeding around their homes. Live samples of mosquito larvae are included with the
presentation and are left in the classrooms so that students can watch them develop.
Slides, videos, handouts, and coloring books help to make this an interesting program.
As part of our effort to reduce the need for pesticides we continue to expand our water
management program. By cleaning clogged and overgrown waterways, mosquito
breeding can be reduced, wetlands restored and the water quality is improved. Recently,
12
===PAGE 16===
we acquired a new state of the art swamp going excavator to enhance our water
management work.
BTi mosquito larvacide is used to treat areas where mosquito larvae are found. We
routinely check known breeding sites, but also encourage the public to notify us of any
areas they suspect could breed mosquitoes. Our field crews will investigate all such sites
and treat as needed.
Our goal is to handle all mosquito problems with water management or larviciding but we
recognize that there are times when adult mosquito spraying is the only viable solution.
In such cases residential and recreational areas are treated with either hand-held or
pick-up mounted sprayers.
The project's surveillance program monitors adult mosquito and larval population
density and is the backbone for prescribing various control techniques. Rain gauges are
set out and data collected by our surveillance crews in an effort to predict when and
where mosquito breeding will occur.
The project's video "Working for You" is available to anyone interested in learning about
mosquito control and the services provided by the Central Massachusetts Mosquito
Control Project.
We would like to thank you for the support you have rendered during 2000 and we look
forward to helping you and your community with its mosquito problems in the future.
CONSERVATION COMMISSION
The Lunenburg Conservation Commission has seven members appointed by the Board of
Selectmen. The Commissioners are charged with administering the Massachusetts
Wetlands Protection Act, the Massachusetts River's Protection Act, Stormwater
Management Policy and the Lunenburg Wetlands Protection Bylaw. The Commission is
also responsible for management and acquisition of Conservation Lands and assisting the
public with wetland and ecological issues and concerns.
The year 2000 was another active year for the Commission. There were 48 Notice of
Intents, 33 continuations of Notice of Intent public hearings, 13 Requests for
Determination of Applicability, 2 Requests for Amendments, 1 Abbreviated Notice of
Resource Delineation, 4 Extensions on existing Order of Conditions, 8 Requests for
Certificate of Compliance, and 11 Informational Meetings. There was a dramatic
increase in reported violations, 4 of which resulted in the issuance of
Enforcement/Cease & Desist Orders. The remaining violations required only a warning
of the violation and instructions for compliance.
Matthew Marro, Conservation Agent, successfully applied for The Massachusetts Forest
Stewardship Grant and received a $2,000 grant to complete a Forest Stewardship Plan
for the Small Town Forest located on Mulpus Road. This is a 215-acre parcel abundant
with wildlife. It is the goal of the Commission to develop a management plan and utilize
this plan to help generate public awareness of the valuable resource that this land is to
the community. Currently there is only one trail, the Commission would like to expand
the trail system, enhance the wildlife habitat, provide signs, identify and explore the
harvest and bio-diversity issues.

FIRE DEPARTMENT
The Lunenburg Fire Department is currently experiencing a dramatic increase in calls for
service. Our total calls for year 2000 were up by 223 from 1999. We ran 150 more
ambulance calls than the previous year. These dramatic increases have really stretched
our abilities and placed a substantial burden on our limited personnel.

more critical. We are not able to operate any longer from our present quarters without
the very real possibility of something going drastically wrong or a very serious accident
occurring. We need to address our space and location problems now with a new facility
in a different location. Hopefully the voters will see fit to give us the tools and facilities

Susan Tallman, Director
The new Millennium found Ritter Memorial Library still facing serious deficiencies.
Ritter is STILL not in compliance with the American Disabilities Act. Only the main
floor has even marginal access for people in wheelchairs.
Parking is STILL inadequate.
Antiquated wiring, dim lighting and difficult access are STILL safety concerns.
The 4100 square foot Ritter Library is STILL barely larger than many private homes
and falls far short of the recommended national standards for public libraries of
towns our size (15,000-sq. ft. minimum).
There is STILL no quiet study area. There is STILL no meeting/program area that
does not block access to materials.
These are STILL the same problems the Trustees have been trying to rectify since 1985

for their public library. We loaned 87,331 items, an 8% increase over 1999. We exceed

per capita than comparable towns.

property tax implications of the various projects. Based on the foregoing we are further

PLANNING BOARD
The Lunenburg Planning Board is working with the increasing development of the Town.
There are several directions that developing is taking in our community. One direction
is residential development, which is manifesting itself into large size homes.
Lunenburg does not meet the State criteria for affordable housing, which puts the Town
in the position of receiving Comprehensive Permit Submittals. These submittals can
bypass certain zoning regulations and allow increased density. Comprehensive
permitting is a threat to our infrastructure particularly in the crisis situation of the
present needed facilities, mainly school, public safety, library and government
structures.
Town Meeting 2000 supported a Zoning Bylaw (Section 4.11.) to manage growth by
setting the maximum number of Building Permits (45) that can be issued in a fiscal year.
However, there are Statutory requirements that allow permitting beyond that number.
Roger Williams, Zoning Official, in a report entitled Status and Clarification of the Bylaw
Amendment noted some of the statutory exemptions, such as previously approved
subdivisions which enjoy exemption for eight years. His report stated that the number
31
===PAGE 35===
of new home permits will come close to sixty or seventy in the present fiscal year.
However, eventually time will moderate the number of permits to the above number.
Again, Comprehensive Permits will upset that density rate.
Further, there is not a balance in our housing stock. While certain parts of the
community have smaller size homes, we still have not addressed the need for the young
and the elderly. Assisted Living Facilities can be accomplished in Lunenburg, however,
the Planning Board is cognitive of the fact that the present zoning does not make it
attractive for a developer to work with the Town in this area. The Board is working on
keeping the essence of the present bylaw, but will present adjustments to make it
feasible for a project to be developed. Over fifty-five housing is also needed. A Federal
Law allows a community to place in their housing stock a project for a certain element of
citizenry without being prejudicial. The Board does not feel that large over fifty-five
developments are in keeping with the character of the Town but small village type
developments would satisfy the need. Balanced housing is an element that is being
directed by the State.
Presently there are two areas of concern. Stone Farm (On Mass Ave.) is in a Purchase and
Sale Agreement with a developer who has noted he will submit an application for a
Comprehensive Permit. The status of Whalom Park is uncertain at the time of this

needs of both the Town and School Departments. Duties for the department include
installations, troubleshooting and maintenance, disposal, purchasing, grant writing, and
strategic planning. The Technology Director works very closely with the Computer
Advisory Committee, Department Heads, and State Officials to accomplish

important branch will ensure proper functionality throughout each department.

converting the Assessor's dated CAMA System to Oracle, Phase I of the Fire/Police
conversion from VMS to NT/SQL Server, DOE's implementation of IMS (a new reporting
system comprised of 35 individual characteristics on every student), a Three Year
Comprehensive Technology Plan, and further implementation of the I-NET (ongoing

is pressed with an abundance of maintenance work. To this end, we are very happy with

TURKEY HILL MIDDLE SCHOOL
During the 2000-2001 school year, Turkey Hill Middle School made several
significant improvements in both our organizational and educational programs.
New staff came on board, and our professional development program stretched
and enhanced the work of our entire staff. In addition, improvements to the
physical plant helped to enhance the atmosphere of the school. Overall it was a
year of change and improvement.
For the first time, all grades from 5-8 were arranged in a middle school
organizational format. Every student was placed on a teaching team; every
classroom teacher had a group of students, which they shared together with their
teaching teammates. This approach created small communities of learners, which
permitted each student to feel part of a teaching team. This has been a goal of
Turkey Hill Middle School for several years. This philosophy creates a better
teaching and learning environment.
Young adolescents are different from both elementary students and high school
students. As such they should have a school organized to meet their specific
needs. Middle School aged students experience significant emotional, social,
physical and intellectual changes. Turkey Hill Middle School is now structured
with an organizational model that helps students deal effectively with these
changes.
During the 2000-2001 school year the position of Curriculum Facilitator was
added to the school, and Mariam Cronin was appointed to fill this position. The
position was funded to help meet the needs of high achieving students. The
school received a grant from the DOE to start a program for gifted and talented
students. As a result there were many exciting activities for high achieving or
gifted students. Students became members of the Culture Club and had the
opportunity to visit the Salem Witch Museum, House of the Seven Gables,
Museum of Fine Arts, Louisa May Alcott's house, etc. In addition, a literary
magazine was published, independent study projects were created by students
under the direction of faculty, and a Junior Great Books reading program was
initiated.
Besides working directly with students, Mrs. Cronin served as a mentor for
language arts and social studies teachers. She worked with these teachers both in
and out of the classroom helping them to strategize and develop new teaching
units.
During the year Turkey Hill Middle School used the professional development
days to initiate a staff development program, which focused on differentiating
instruction for students. The training provided staff with the tools to meet the
needs of students with differing abilities. This is the first year of a multi-year
effort. Our goal is to have all staff feel comfortable with the belief that they can
challenge every child.
MCAS scores remain an important benchmark for our school. These scores
provide an indicator of how well our students are grasping the Massachusetts
Frameworks. As such our staff again spent time to make sure the Turkey Hill
Middle School curricula are aligned with the Frameworks. The staff also studied
the "Item Analyses" to determine if there are gaps in the way we teach students.

THOMAS C. PASSIOS ELEMENTARY SCHOOL
The Thomas C. Passios Elementary School continues to strive toward educating the total
child. We utilize a variety of instructional methods and modalities in our approach and
try always to meet the individual needs of each child. We believe that the success of our
programs is dependent upon a healthy and productive partnership between home and
school. Parents play an active role in many of the daily programs and routines of the
school.
This year continues to be a challenging and exciting year for both the students, faculty,
and staff of the Elementary and Primary Schools. With a combined enrollment in both
schools of seven hundred twenty students, class sizes continue in the twenty to twenty-
five range, with the younger children benefiting from the small numbers.
The summer of 2000 proved to be very beneficial to many children in Lunenburg. Four
summer programs were offered to elementary children: a five week Preschool Program, a
five week program for children with special service requirements, and a three week
program for children in grades one and two who were experiencing difficulty in the areas
of reading and math. This program was sponsored through School Linked Services.
This fall Responsive Classroom was implemented throughout the Primary and
Elementary Schools. This social curriculum is designed to help build a school into a
learning community where high social and academic goals are both attained. Responsive
Classroom is built around central components that integrate teaching, learning, and
caring in the daily program. These components are set in the context of commonly
shared values, such as honesty, fairness and respect, and are implemented through the
development and strengthening of social skills, such as cooperation, assertion,
responsibility, empathy, and self-control. Three of the six key components of the
program are: classroom organization, morning meeting, rules and logical consequences.
Additional training next year will help us in the implementation of the remaining six
components: academic choice, guided discovery, and assessment and reporting.
The Elementary School continues to assess and revise curricula to meet the needs of its
students. As we continue to analyze our students' results on the Massachusetts
Comprehensive Assessment System (MCAS) and revise curricula and strategies, we
continue to grow as an excellent educational facility. We are extremely proud of the
work the faculty continues to undertake in this area each year. As teachers continue to
reflect on their teaching practice, students continue to benefit.
MCAS results indicate that in the areas of both English Language Arts and Math, the
number of Grade 4 students performing in the "failing" category continues to decline,
and appears to be shifting into the "needs improvement" category. However, testing
results indicate that sufficient numbers are not moving upward into the "proficient"
category. Further MCAS analysis indicated that students need continued focus on
writing, particularly on open ended response questions in the area of math.
This year the Elementary School Improvement Plan focused on the areas of reading
comprehension and writing. At this point in the year, the Council is proud that we have
accomplished many of our goals. This fall we implemented a writing assessment
schedule/practice in which all students in grades one through four were asked to write
on a given topic. Students' writing was evaluated using a rubric (scoring guide) and used
to create baseline data. All students were assessed again in March and again in
May/June. By implementing this program, teachers and parents will be able to
document individual student writing progress and re-teach as needed.

IOWA Results
In the spring of 2000 students in grades three, seven, and nine also were
administered the Iowa Tests. The Iowa Tests are standardized, norm-referenced tests.
This means that the tests were given using specified directions and under specific
conditions to a representative group of students nationwide. Scores from this national
sample become the "norms" that permit local schools to compare the performance of
their students with this national group. Unlike the MCAS scores, the scores from
norm-referenced tests do not tell what students know and what they don't know. They
tell only how a student or group's performance compares with that of others in the
norm group.
The scores reported below are national percentile rank scores. Percentile ranks
are reported in units that range from 1 to 99. A percentile rank score of 67 means that
the group earned a raw score that was higher than 67 percent of the students in the
norm group. It does not mean that the local group scored as well or better than 67
percent of districts nationwide.
