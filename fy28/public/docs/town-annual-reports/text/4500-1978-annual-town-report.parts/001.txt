SHERWOOD G WINCH:
RUDOLPH MASELLA
Board of Selectmen: left to right - Ann P. Hall, Chairman Sherwood G. Winchester and Rudolph Masella.
The Board of Selectmen, also serving as Board of Health and Licensing Authority, never have a dull agenda.
State and Federal changes in laws which regulate the operation of a municipality are never-ending. Budget season
is particularly time-consuming. With growth and new programs, inflated cost of operation, Selectmen are always
trying to find a balance between sensible spending, and providing the necessary services we need to live with. The
goal to achieve is "the best possible services at the lowest possible cost".
Proposition 13 -- its success in California has brought an awareness to the voter that something can be done
about their taxes. More importantly, it has made government aware that the voter will be heard and that things
will happen to not only stop increases but also reduce their tax burden. Hopefully, here in Massachusetts our Gov-
ernor and the Legislature will take heedof this possibility and reduce the number of programs which mandate to cities
and towns costly programs for which no State support monies are made available, thus causing local property taxes to
rise. The mood appears to be there in Boston -- let US all hope that the mood changes into action.
The problem of solid waste is still with us, and just like the daily garbage, it won't go away by itself. The
present landfill on Townsend Harbor Road is rapidly nearing its approved capacity, and the Board of Health will most
probably be forced to close the facility sometime in the coming year. A majority of the voters present at the Special
Town Meeting on June 12, 1978 approved the recommendations of the Refuse Disposal Planning Committee and the
Board of Selectmen, but the vote fell short of the required two-thirds, thus delaying a Town decision on future solid
waste disposal. The Board fervently hopes the voters of Lunenburg will make a decision to resolve the problem of
waste disposal in a manner acceptable to Town residents and to the State alike before further State and/or judicial
action makes the decision for US.
Lunenburg does notrun on elected officials alone. About one hundred civic-minded residents serve on various
committees, shaping and molding ways and means to run our Town. The Selectmen wish to thank them for their time
and supporting efforts.

Lunenburg residents were treated to a once in alifetime year in 1978. On January 1st there was aflag raising
on the Common which was to signal a year-long celebration of our 250th Anniversary. The 250th Anniversary Com-
mittee spent five years of planning and hard work to bring back the memories of 250 years. Among the many high-
lights of the year was an outstanding pageant, written and produced by Lunenburg residents, and enjoyed by all.
In October, Lunenburg was treated to the best parade we have ever had in our Town. Yes, Lovable Lunenburg is
very much alive and has come a long way since 1728. Thanks to all who participated in this marvelous year.
Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors: left to right - William F. Reed, Chairman John F. Nicoll and Bernard J. Paton.
The Board of Assessors has continued to attempt to maintain equality in their assessments by reviewing all prop-
erties, maintaining and evaluating sales records, and on-site viewing. The State Department of Revenue continues
to demand "full and fair market value" on all property. Lunenburg has factored several increases, however, these
increases are not keeping up with the inflation rate.
Although building permits are required for all new buildings and for alterations, the Board still finds many vio-
lations. New construction continues at a low rate with 26 new home starts last year. However, there has been a
considerable amount of alterations and out buildings added.
The Board has attended many meetings and seminars sponsored by the State and/or Assessor's Association to keep
informed of new laws and changes in the present ones. The new maps which were votedin at the last Town Meeting
were made and delivered. Included were also two small books which allows the Board to carry them in the field for
those areas where it is difficult to identify properties.

We wish to comment on the passage of Chapter 580, which was also known as " Proposition "on the ballot in
the November 1978 election and how the new law effects the assessment program in Lunenburg. We are under a man-
date from the Bureau of Revenue to bring the assessed valuation of the Town to 100% full and fair market value. We
have filed and had accepted, a plan of action to bring the Town to 100% over a period of years, and we have made
two 10% increases in all real properties in 1976 and 1977 to gradually comply with the mandate. We took no addi-
tional action in 1978 due to a severe decrease in new construction coupled with a considerable number of bank sales
of property which the banks had acquired through bankruptcies or foreclosures. We decided to wait and see if the
economy was cooling off prior to completing our 100% assessment program. We are now awaiting the completion of
guidelines from the State for implementation of the new law.
We, in the interval, remain under the mandate of the court to complete our re-valuation and arrive at one
hundred per cent valuation of all property in the Town. The new law also requires one hundred per cent valuation
and an inspection and certification by the State prior to setting the proportional percentages (55% Industrial, 50%
Commercial, and 45% residential with a $5000 valuation automatic abatement) on which the tax rate will be calcu-
lated. A house with a 100% value of $40,000 will be assessed at 45% or $18,000 less $5,000 abatement or $13,000.
The tax rate will be set by dividing the total taxable value of the Town into the amount of monies voted at Town
Meeting, and the result will be the rate per thousand. Tax bills will show both 100% valuation and taxable valua-
tion on each parcel of property.
We have been informed that the State Legislature will be asked to define "open land" which under the new law
will be valued at 100% and taxed on 25% of that valuation. We may find that the State will in effect be setting the
value of land throughout the Commonwealth. We feel that with several court cases already questioning the consti-
tutionality of the new law, coupled with the lack of guidelines and the questions on open land, the Town would be
better served by adopting a "wait and see" policy this year.

Library Trustees
The year 1978 witnessed the completion of the reorganizationof the Library's facilities begun last year. Steel
bookshelves, purchased second-hand from another library, now hold the nonfiction collection in the first two rooms,
upstairs. The back room upstairs, formerly the nonfiction room, has been
converted into a useful study area/meeting room. This room was carpeted,
hung with plants, and repainted with funds raised by the Friends of the
Ritter Memorial Library.
The twelve month reorganization has substantially improved the
Library's services and accommodations, yet really cost the taxpayersonly
a minimum amount of money. Careful purchasing of materials, shopping
for top quality used furniture, and performing all the labor ourselves paid
off by obtaining $8, 109.00 worth of merchandise for $2,618.00. Funds
from Library Trust Funds and the Friends of the Library further offset the
cost to the Town to a mere $385.00 -- less than 5% of the list cost.
The Library, as a result of this reorganizing effort, now has a
separate roomfor children's programs and collections, an integrated non-
fiction section, an attractive meeting room available for use by community
groups, and a main floor with the most requested and necessary materials
available to all. The primary focus has been to make it easier for all
people to use the Library's resources,

Special programs were also very much in evidence for the rest of Lunenburg's population this year. The Friends'

There were two "International Nights", with slides, discussions and foods fromdifferent foreign countries of interest

Circulation figures show both the effectsof inflation on the book-buying power of the budget and the conversion
of the Annex to a Primary School for the kindergarten and first grades. Circulation of adult books kept pace with
last year's figures, with a substantial increase in the use of paperback books. Record borrowing is down, perhaps
because there has been no provision for audio-visual materials in the Library budget for the past few years. Year-
end totals for adult items were: 18, 957 books (75 interlibrary loans), 873 records, 1, 200 magazines and 44 pamphlets;
66 films were borrowed through the regional library system.
A new pattern is emerging in the circulation of children's books. The summer reading program placed a definite
emphasis onolder children, and as a result the summer circulation figures were down from their all-time high in 1977.
Another change resulted from the move of the third graders from the Annex to the Elementary School. For several
years these students were the most enthusiastic users of all children's materials, and in their weekly visits to the Li-
brary they learned to make effective use of Library resources. Now that these children moved from the Annex to the
Elementary School and no longer come to the Library on a regular basis, circulation by class visits in nonfiction and
fiction for older readers have both declined by 99%. The Library limits the number of borrowed by the kinder-
garten children and first graders now visiting from the former Annex, since these students are just learning how to use
the Library, and, therefore, overall class circulation is down by about 50%. Children's circulation figures for 1978
were: 22,079 books, 143 magazines, 33 puzzles, 136 filmstrip kits and 549 records.
The constraints imposed by runaway inflation in book prices over the past several years is being keenly felt at
the Ritter Memorial Library. Fewer books can be purchased with budgeted funds each year. Fortunately, the gener-
osity of several individuals and community groups has provided resources over and above the regular Library budget.
A fine set of books has been given by Mrs. Sandra Demers to honor the 50th Wedding Anniversary of her parents, Mr.
& Mrs. Leo Molter; Mr. Edward Balcom has given outstanding reference volumes in memory of his wife Patricia Balcom
and son Edward. Mrs. Kathryn Curtis has remembered her father, the Library's original architect, Frederick L. Francis,
with a display case for recent fiction. The Lunenburg Women's Club presented the Library with money for a book in
memory of Georgia Brown, and the Lunenburg Memorial Fund remembered David Ellms with shelves and a display stand
for the Memorial Fund record book, and provided a collection of books in memory of Harold Welterlen.

Housing Authority
The Lunenburg Housing Authority is in its ninth year in the operation of the facilities at 131 White Street known
as the Pearl Brook Housing for the Elderly. Presently, we have forty-two apartments occupied by single persons and
six apartments occupied by couples,
The tenants have their own organized Pearl Brook Tenants' Association, which meets on a regular basis and
arranges interesting programs and projects for the benefit and enjoyment of its members. Mrs. Helen Byron holds the

Executive Secretary, will be available to answer questions.
The tenants of the Pearl Brook Housing for the Elderly participate in and enjoy the many interesting activities

Sewerage & Drainage Investigative Committee
In the first year of its existence the Sewerage & Drainage Investigative Committee determined that there are
sufficient sewerage and/or drainage problems existing in certain areas of the Town to warrant continued study in a
search for possible solutions to the problem. The Committee has also determined that both Federal and State agencies
have made recent changes in their policies concerning funding of sewerage facilities.
In the past, the official positionof the State and Federal agencieswas that the only solution to sewerage prob-
lems was extensive sewer systems leading to elaborate sewerage treatment plants providing for full and complete
treatment of sewerage. Recently, however, the agencies have changed their position and will now consider alterna-
tive methods of collection and treatment and, in fact, require that any proposed grant requests clearly demonstrate
that alternatives have been considered and that the recommended project is the most cost-effective solution to the
particular problem at hand.
With these thoughts in mind, the Committee retained the engineering firm of Dufresne-Henry. The engineers
were asked to investigate financial assistance programs currently available from State and Federal agencies for al-
ternative solutions for collection and treatment of sewerage which would be less extensive and less expensive than
the previously submitted sewer plan. There are, indeed, funds available for studies and construction of alternative
systems.
One major point which we wish to emphasize is that, as a condition of any of the Federal grants for study
and/or construction of sewerage facilities in the Town, a very basic requirement is that the entire Town be studied.
This does not imply that sewerage must be installed in the entire Town, but it does require that the initial investi-
gation study the entire Town and determine those areas requiring attention and the extent of any problems elsewhere
in the Town.
Representatives of Dufresne-Henry and this Committee met with State and Federal agencies for the purposes
of determining the feasibility of facilities planning to study effective alternative systems, the possibility of recouping
a portion of the Town funds already expended for engineering and design services related to the previously planned
connection to the Fitchburg sewers and to obtain possible implementation schedule for facilities planning and ob-
taining a priority rating for the Town for the above funds.
As a result of these meetings, and after a careful consideration of the reports submitted by Dufresne-Henry
to this Committee, we feel that there are alternatives for solving the most acute problems in the Town and particularly
in the Whalom Area at a cost considerably below the previous amounts that would have been necessary for a complete
sewer system connected to the Fitchburg Treatment Plant.
It is the recommendation of the Sewerage & Drainage Investigative Committee that the 1979 Annual Town
Meeting appropriate the sum of $18,000.00 to be matched by a Federal EPA Grant of $54,000.00, such sums to be
used to engage an engineering firm to make a detailed study to update the data from the 1973 Morgenroth report,
to identify existing problem areas, to determine future needs, to identify alternative plans to meet existing and also
future needs, and to prepare a preliminary plan for all reasonable alternatives and perform such surveys and make
such reports as required to support the evaluation and the development of alternative plans and reports as may be
required to meet the Federal and State Grant requirements.
We wish to emphasize that the above sum of money is to be used for engineering studies only and, at this time,
we are not recommending any appropriation for construction of any specific facilities at any specific location in the
community. The purpose of this study is to determine the particular extent of the problems in this community and
what would be the most cost-effective method of solving these problems. It is our opinion that this is the most pru-
dent course for the Town to follow at this time.

Freeman, David D. Blake, William J. Kearney, Mary L. Padula and Chester J. Pliska,

"DEDICATION TO HAROLD HARLEY"
"The Conservation Commission extends a warm welcome to our honored guest, Harold D.
Harley, and his wife Margaret, and a good afternoon to our invited guests. Knowing full
well that our honored guest does not like pomp and circumstance, we will keep this dedi-
cation ceremony short and simple.
We meet under these tall oaks, by agranite marker, to dedicate an area long admired and
loved by Harold. An area such as this is usually described on topo maps, property maps,
and deeds as a swamp, a bog, a marsh or a lowland, It brings to people's minds such
words as dismal, dreary, eerie, or even scary. To the true conservationist it is just the
opposite.
In spring, it is the cradle of life, a place of new birth, an ever-renewing fountain in the
chain of natural development.
In summer, it is an area of protection used by all types of wildlife, from the lowly sala-
mander, to that most graceful of animals, the white-tailed deer.
In the fall, it is a region of peace and serenity as nature winds down from a busy year of
rearing a new generation of plants and animals.
In the winter, it is a haven, a resting place, a deep warm bed that nurtures and protects
life during the frosty time, ready to face another year.
Mulpus Swamp is a classic lowland, fed by a strong and dependable stream. Through the
foresight of Harold and his contemporaries, the Town purchased this land. It encompasses
approximately 300 acres. It will be protected for all time for the enjoyment of our de-
scendants.

the Bridle Path 4-H Club with Dixie Beck and Judy Smith as leaders, and Donna Camber as assistant leader.
The 4-H Outdoor Adventure Club was launched three years ago, and its main objectives are to help members ap-
preciate and enjoy the beauty and magnitude of our physical world, while learning the responsibilities and obligations
of maintaining it. One of the main activities of the Club has been hiking, Members made their own backpacks,
planned the types of foods and other items necessary for hikes and then trekked off and explored nature at such sites

ing Clinic at the University of Massachusetts, and another clinic was held at Smithfield Farm in Lunenburg where
Mrs. Gail McAlpine and associates of Littleton, Mass., demonstrated showing in halter. Four Club members demon-
strated Western and English pleasure riding for the group, which also included guests from the Leominster, Westmin-

THE MUNICIPAL FINANCE PROBLEM
"For Every Complex Situation There is a Simple Answer and it is Always Wrong."
The graphs on the preceding page demonstrate that the major financial problem in our municipality and in cities
and towns throughout Massachusetts is OVERRELIANCE ON THE LOCAL PROPERTY TAX to pay for local, State and
Federal services. That overreliance, rather than overspending, is the real problem is demonstrated by the following
comparison based on Fiscal Year 1976 U. S. Census Bureau data:

The circle graphs on the preceding page also call attention to the even more alarming fact that in the Town of
Lunenburg, due primarily to underfunding of State Mandated Programs, during the last two years alone (from FY 1976
to FY 1978) State Aid as a source of local revenue has decreased from 30.9% to 24.6% while the property tax has
increased from 52.2% to 57.7%. Our graphs also illustrate that throughout the Nation in FY 1976, the last comparison
period for which statistics are available, Federal and State Aid (including sales and income taxes allotted to local
governments) provided 47.4%, or close to one-half of all local revenue, while the corresponding figure for all
Massachusetts cities and towns was 35.6% and for the Town of Lunenburg, 35. 1% nearer to one-third of all local
revenue. In FY 1978 Lunenburg's percentage dropped way down to 29.7%, with shrinking state funding between
FY 1976 and FY 1978 accounting for an estimated 10 of the 49.65 dollars which made up Lunenburg's FY 1979 Tax
Rate. Ten tax rate dollars means approximately $250 to a Lunenburg Taxpayer with an assessed value of $25,000
and even doubles that figure ($500) for a substantial number of Lunenburg home owners. PRETTY SHODDY TREAT-
MENT BY OUR STATE WOULDN'T YOU SAY?
However, this is not to say that at the local level we should not helpbring about general property tax relief by
attempting to reduce municipal expenditures and to this end our operations are being reviewed, alternatives ana-
lyzed and changes introduced and evaluated. It must, of course, be understood that even holding the line is trans-
formed by inflation into losses in purchasing power, losses which are not easily offset even when every possible effi-
ciency is introduced into municipal government.
Unfortunately, efficiencies take time to implement and are not, therefore, immediately visible on the tax rate
and, of course, aren't visible at all when the State reneges on its commitments thereby counteracting any benefits
which would otherwise be derived. Another costly oversight in today's frenzied tax cutting atmosphere is apt to be
recognition of the fact that some efficiencies, including many of the most promising, may initially require higher
costs as current revenues are invested in future returns rather than in current service delivery. Therefore, whereas
we commend the Governor for vigorously attacking the Property Tax dilemma, WE DO NOT INTEND TO BE FORCED
INTO CUT BACK TACTICS AIMED AT SHORT TERM ECONOMIES DESIGNED AROUND THE FULFILLMENT OF A
CAMPAIGN PROMISE!
In any cut back, administrative services are particularly vulnerable because they are not usually supported by a
vocal clientor citizen group. Some administrative areas can be cut butothers need to be strengthened if the goal of
minimizing service reductions, while spending less, is to be achieved. Fortunately Lunenburg voters, officials, and
Finance Committee people are much more aware of this fact than are the corresponding groups in many cities and
towns. As a resultof this awareness, comparatively speaking, our financial house is in exceedingly good order. For
example, more funds are provided for tax collections than in most of the surrounding towns and better tax collections
have permitted accounts receivable to be converted into fluid assets. Funds have also been provided for vigorous
cash management of all monies collected. As a result, in FY 1979, a conservatively estimated $100,000 will be
earnedon invested funds. (See page 68 for breakdown of investments.) Here again we serve notice on our Governor,
our legislators and any other State entity which attempts to in any way undercut or take away the financial advan-
tages which we have carefully built up
HANDS OFF !!!

In conclusion, our local effort to lower property taxes is being based on the principle of limiting local taxes
in combination with increased State Aid, (no strings to be attached), and in this regard we are in agreement with
the Governor and would concede that the Governor's bill is an important step in that direction. Nonetheless, it is
felt that municipal tax levy and spending caps as proposed, translate into types of service cuts which will not be
acceptable to the average Lunenburg citizen. A concentrated local effort is being made to reduce expenditures
through more efficient management, and we feel this can best be accomplished with little or no intervention by the
State. That is to say, the State should effectively "get off of our backs" and shift its emphasis to putting its own
house in order, a monumental task which would reap far-reaching benefits for the State, for the Communities and
most of all for the overburdened Property Owner.
