SELECTMEN
Normand J. Thompson, Chairman
Bernard J. Paton
Merton S. Meloon
The year 1964 has gone into history. The present Board of Selectmen have, as have
previous boards, looked back to analyze what has happened and now makes its report.
A Selectman is charged with the awesome responsibil of representing the will of the
people, protecting the public welfare and guiding the growth of the Community. As the
Town grows governmental problems become much more technical and complex. A Selectman
must acquire a work knowledge of at least the following: General Statutory powers and
duties of his office, his relative position with other governmental officials, municipal of icers
and employees; limentary procedure; public relations with the citizens and press; special
areas of administration including creating and enforcing By-laws; personnel and salary ad-
ministration; purchasing, budget preparations and public works. Asc Lunenburg Selectman
is also a member of the Board of Health and Welfare, and the Licensing and Zoning author-
ities, a Selectman in our Town must also have specific of public health, wel-
fare, licensing procedures, planning and zoning requirements.
The agenda for the Selectmen's weekly meeting contained an average of forty items
which often occupied the Board past midnight. Many citizens, singly and in groups, met
with the Selectmen bringing problems which would appear to fall within their jurisdiction.
Limited funds and facilities and actual lack of legal control over many matters often
prevents action by the Board of Selectmen regardless of how desirous they may be of
complying with requests. Townspeople are nonetheless more than welcome to attend
these weekly meetings to discuss any problem no matter how large or small, if we are
unaware of the problem we cannot be expected to solve it.
The year 1964 presented the usual amount of routine business and number of perennial
problems arising from expanding growth and rising costs, particularly with regard to health,
drainage and highway needs. An unusually large number of special committees were
appointed by the Selectmen and Moderator. Routine business included the granting of
531 licenses and permits, the processing of 101 zoning applications, and the selection
of qualified citizens to serve as jurors. Work continued on the White Street construction,
Whalom drainage and sidewalk programs and Shirley Road construction was let out to bid
and commenced. Other projects relating to drainage and road construction, although still
in the planning and engineering stages, involved a considerable amount of time and study.
Two long-time faithful servants of Lunenburg retired, our Fire Chief, Roger G. Bigelow
and Town Nurse, Miss Signe E. Schultz. The Town is proud of, and grateful for their devoted and dedicated service.
Board members attended a variety of State and area meetings including a Seminar on Town finances conducted at
Clark University. It is felt that contact with State and area officials permits an exchange of knowledge and ideas which
cannot helpbut provide greater insight into local problems. At the Massachusetts Selectmen's Annual Meeting, Lunenburg
Selectmen again had the honor of accepting for the fourth straight year, a First Place Award for the excellence of our
Town Report. For the third year in a row, Lunenburg's Report also won an award in the New England Municipal Report
competition. We extend our congratulations to Town Clerk, Mrs. Mary Padula, and members of her staff who cipated
in the preparation of the Annual Report.
The road to Municipal improvements has no end. Those who preceded our Board accomplished many things. No
matter how hard we may strive to make our Town a better place to live in, it is also a certainty that those who follow
will find things to change and improve.
We think it is particularly appropriate that the 1964 Report is dedicated to the cause of conservation, in view of
the serious drought which occurred in that year. Fresh water will probably be the first of his natural resources that
man will exhaust. It has been estimated that by 1980 our country's demand will exceed the natural supply. And ours
is a fortunate nation. Citizens should be ever aware that theirs is the right to use, not abuse, our natural resources
for land, wood and water have been described as nature's gifts for life and survival, man's hope for future progress.

ASSESSORS
Chester J. Pliska, Chairman
Paul E. Wickham
Arthur J. Brockelman, Sr.
The primary responsibility of the Board of Assessors is to determ ine the valuation of
all taxable real and personal property and annually assess taxes thereon based on the total
of all amounts appropriated at Town meetings and any other amounts required by law to
be raised by taxation less an estimate of the amount of receipts and available funds which
may be applied to the payment of these expenditures. The Assessors are also responsible
for determining the amount of excise to be levied on farm animals and for the commitment
of excise on motor vehicles, a record of which is received from the State. Photostatic
copies of deeds are used to amend records of property ownership as are transcripts of records
of probate. A considerable amount of paper work is involved in the assessment of taxes
and excise particularly when reassessments and abatements are involved. Many and varied
duties of the Board of Assessors are prescribed by law. All of the work performed by the
Assessors is subject to inspection by the State Commissioners of Taxation who, by law, are
given direct jurisdiction over the local boards.
A new law, which became effective on January 1, 1964, grants tax exemptions to
certain qualifying elderly persons who own property and are domiciled in Lunenburg. This
benefit for our senior citizens was publicized through news media and letters and by talk-
ing to various groups. The Board of Assessors assisted applicants in filing their formal
requests for exemption and all requests were promptly acted upon.
The Local Board organized meetings in conjunction with Assessors from area towns for
the purpose of exchanging ideas and seeking solutions to problems involved in certain
assessing practices. Four such meetings were held, the most recent one in Lunenburg on
November 30, 1964, when the local board acted as host to sixty assessors from seventeen
area towns.
The so-called "Springfield" decision of the Massachusetts Supreme Court held that
all property in the State must be valued equally at fair market value. In view of this now
famous decision and our Board's conviction that equalized values induce economic equality
and satisfy the taxpayers with the knowledge that everyone is being fairly treated, the
Board of Assessors is sponsoring an article in the 1965 annual meeting warrant which calls
for a citizen's study committee to look into the matter of a revaluation of the property in
our Town.
The local Board of Assessors has instituted a series of programs to promote the cause of property tax relief. A
resolution calling for a concerted effort on the part of all area Assessors was introduced to the Worcester County
Assessors Association as well as to the conference of area Assessors which was held in Lunenburg. The cooperation of
our Senators and Representatives was also sought. It is obvious that the General Court must act to provide cities and
towns with additional revenues or grant other tax powers directly to the municipalities. There is little doubt that
local treasuries have been severely taxed by State Legislation which has not provided commensurate revenue measures.
Our Legislators were also urged to vote against legislation which would have an adverse effect onmunicipal finances.
The Assessors also dispatched letters to Governor Peabody and incoming Governor Volpe and urged that new or addition-
al taxes to relieve the cities and towns be recommended. As a final step the Board of Assessors ask that other elected
officials and citizens of the Town join in this campaign.
The Board of Assessors concurs with the following quotation: "To most assessors, self satisfaction and the con-
viction of having done there best is often their sole reward. Yet I am convinced that any Board of Assessors whose
members are unprejudiced, honest, competent and devoted to their work eventually commands the respect of everybody
including the staff, civic authorities and the public." It is the desire of the present members of your board to EARN
your respect through their carrying out of their official duties and administering their office in line with the oath of
office each member has taken.

TOWN OFFICE
Mary L. Padula Treasurer, Town Clerk, Tax Collector
Secretary to Board of Selectmen
Many citizens have asked how the proposed new school building program will affect
our tax rate. This is not an easy question to answer as there are many variants in the basic
ingredients which go into a tax rate. These ingredients are: 1) Total amount of money to
be raised by taxation, 2) Estimate of receipts which offset the amount to be raised, and
3) Total assessed valuation of personal property and real estate. The tax rate is deter-
mined by subtracting estimated receipts from the amount to be raised by taxat and dividing
by the assessed valuation.
The first ingredient, total amount to be raised by taxation, is determined by you, the voter, at the Town meeting. In
1964, every $11,500 voted represented $1.00 on the Tax Rate. The second ingredient, estimated receipts to be used to
offset spending, is limited by State law which provides that estimates of any receipts, excepting those received from the
State, cannot exceed amounts received from simi lar sources the previous year, and that estimate of receipts from the State
must be exactly as certified by the State Tax Commission. As estimated receipts directly offset amounts appropriated, in
1964 each $11,500 increase in receipts offset a tax rate dollar's worth of appropriations. Increases in the third ingredient,
assessed valuation, also act as an offset to spending. However, it takes approximately $135,000 worth of increased
valuation to offset $11,500 worth of expenditures. (Example: If tax at beginning of year was $82.00 and expend-
itures increased $115,000 ($11,500 X 10) over the previous year, and receipts and valuation did not change, tax rate
would automatically go to $92.00. If, however, estimated receipts increased $11,500 thus offsetting $1.00 of the
increase, and assessed valuations increased $270,000 ($135,000 X 2) thus offsetting $2.00 of the increase, tax rate
would go to only $89.00 or an increase of $7.00 over the previous year's rate.)
Therefore, in order to estimate the effect of a school building program on the tax rate, it would first be necessary
to estimate the cost of the program and any other expenditures related to it. The biggest increase will, of course,
result from increased operating costs. For example, the "new" elementary school was occupied in September of 1953,
1954 being the first full year of operation. The 1954 school operating budget increased 57% over the 1952 budget.
The Junior-Senior High School was completed and occupied in September of 1957, 1958 being the first full year of
operation. The 1958 school operating budget showed an increase of $135,051 over the 1956 operating budget and,
taking into consideration increased estimated receipts, accounted for a net increase in all school spending of $102, 155
and resulted in a tax rate increase of $11.00.
The twelve room addition to the Junior-Senior High School was occupied in September of 1961, 1962 being the
first full year of operation. The 1962 school operating budget showed an increase of $149,968 over the 1960 operating
budget and, considering special articles and estimated receipts, showed a net increase of $128, 184 or $10,682 per
additional classroom.
It has already been established that during the first four years the payments of principal and interest on a bond
issue, relating to a new $800,000 elementary school, would add an average of $4.00 to the tax rate. Taking into
consideration the fact that the $10,682 per classroom relates to the maintenance of extra classroom space only, as
opposed to a whole new central plant, and in view of increased costs, it then would not seem unreasonable to estimate
that with a newsixteen room school all school spending could easily account for an additional $20.00 to $25.00 on the
tax rate. This increase does not allow for special sidewalk, road building or drainage projects which could accompany
and relate to the school building program. Neither does it include the purchase of additional equipment which is
usually required after a new school goes into operation. Increases in assessed valuation, which in the last 10 years averaged
$356,000 ($2.60 rate offset), have not been considered in the above estimate as we will be fortunate if increased
valuations offset non-school spending. Any increase in State Aid programs which may be voted by the Legislature in
the near future could act as an offset to these figures although, as arule, the benefit is not as great "taxwise" as it would
seem to be at first glance because most special grants are accompanied by requirements for additional spending.
The school which is being proposed and which will be occupied in 1966 is strictly for elementary purposes. In view
of a need for additional Junior High School rooms which could ariseas early as 1968, it is not unreasonable to assume that
we may be asked to build a Junior-High School four or five years after the elementary school is completed. It is
unfortunate that greater effort has not been made to somehow meet the two needs with one building program as, with-
out a new form of Tax Relief, the costs resulting from one building program will severely tax the average Lunenburg
homeowner, and the costs resulting from two programs could very well be prohibitive.

LEGAL
Robert J. Larkin, Town Counsel
Within the past two or three years, the Bureau of Government Research at the
University of Massachusetts published a paper entitled "The Role of the Town Counsel".
In the opening paragraph, a town counsel was described, in part, in the following words:
"A town counsel may be said to be a lawyer who devotes some part of his time to the legal affairs of the Town. He
seldom devotes his full time to the job, although there are periods, especially around the time of the annual Town
Meeting, when he may have the impression, rightly or wrongly, that he is working double time." This is certainly an
accurate appraisal insofar as it pertains to annual Town Meeting time. In all truth, the same could be said of other
periods throughout the year.
In 1963, the so-called Conflict of Interest law loomed large on municipal horizons, and many problems were
anticipated during 1964. However, they failed to materialize in Lunenburg, at least. We rendered opinions on just
three requests for rulings on matters involving a possible conflict. Each case was resolved without incident. We had
occasion during the year to confer with Town officers, department heads and special Town committees, for the purpose
of discussing special legal problems within the scope of their respective responsiblities and duties, and of working out
solutions where called for. These various offices included the Board of Health, School Committee, Superintendent of
Schools, Assessors, Police Department, Conservation Committee, School Building Committee, Personnel Committee,
Planning Board, Zoning Appeal Board and, of course, the Town Clerk, Tax Collector and Treasurer, and the Select-
men with whom we met almost every Monday night through the year. The problems posed by the many Departments
necessarily covered a wide range of unrelated subjects. The ShirleyRoad improvement project engaged a considerable
amount of time in connection with the acquisition of drainage easements which were necessary for the proper construc-
tion of the road. A few hearings before the Board of Selectmen, in its capacity as the Town's Licensing Board, were
held during the year on complaints of violations.
The Conservation Committee, to its credit, has been active in acquiring lands for conservation purposes, a prac-
which will prove to be of inestimable value to the future welfare of the Town and its inhabitants. This has resulted,
nevertheless, in a considerable amount of legal work in connection with acquiring the land. The trend toward "out-
door activities and recreation" precipitated some controversy among various groups when it appeared that our ponds
and lakes could not accommodate the various recreation preferences of all the users, atone and the same time. While
the Town does not "own" these bodies of water, it has had to become involved to a limited extent because of the
interest of Lunenburg citizens. There are still some problems in this area which have not yet been resolved.
For some time now, the City of Leominster has been engaged in expanding its sewerage system. A "leg" of this
system passes through a portion of Lunenburg's Whalom area. By virtue of an Agreement between Lunenburg and
Leominster, which was authorized by the Legislature, many Lunenburg residents are tied into the system. Lunenburg
pays a fee for each connection and then collects from the users. Because of the expansion and ensuing increase in
costs to Leominster, the Leominster Sewer Commission has been negotiating with Lunenburg for a new Agreement.
To protect its interests and upgrade the administrative handling in Lunenburg, additional Legislative help was sought
and obtained. We have not, as yet, reached complete agreement with Leominster.
A great number of applications for permits of one kind or another relating to the Zoning Code were processed by
the Town Clerk's office and the Selectmen during the year. On several occasions, it was necessary to give opinions
or rulings as to the legal effect of the Code's provisionsor the subject matter of some of the applications. Substantially,
all were resolved without litigation, if not to the satisfaction of all concerned. Two cases, however, ended up in
Superior Court. One case is pending. The other was disposed of in November, 1964.
In conclusion, we would once again borrow from the Bureau of Government Research publication, which reflects
our own thinking and, we hope, our practice. "No city solicitor or town counsel should be put in a position where
he is obliged to make policy if it can be avoided. Not because he might not be entirely qualified to make policy
decisions, but because once he gets into policy-making he tends to lose his effectiveness as a legal advisor."

PERSONNEL STUDY COMMITTEE
Calvin K. Sholl, Chairman
Howard A. Hamel
Stanley H. Howard
Walter B. Keeler
Phillips F. Lewis
The Personnel Study Committee completed its assignment this year with the submission of a final report in the form
of a Salary Administration Plan By-law which will be acted upon at a special Town Meeting which will be held in
February, 1965.
The original study by Sumner D. Charm & Associates of Boston in December, 1963 was used to formulate an over-
all Salary Administration Plan for Town employees. In preparing the Plan the Committee attempted to meet four
objectives:
1. Provide realistic wage scales for Town employees based upon wages paid by towns of similar size in
northern Worcester County.
2. Provide a codified system of fringe benefits which would help protect the interests of the Town employees
and provide an incentive for continuous employment.
3. Provide a Salary Plan which could be easily administered while encouraging more efficient administration
of Town departments.
4. Provide a plan which struck a balance between the needs of the Town employees and the ability of the
Town to pay.
The Committee was unanimous in its opinion that the proposed Salary Plan, would not solve all problems in the
operation of the Town employee group but the Plan would provide, for the first time, a workable system of ground
rules for the Town administrators and the Town employees. Under the proposed Plan the Town employee group could
function and grow in an orderly manner.
The Committee wishes to express their appreciation to the many Town employees and other townspeople who
assisted in this work.
America today stands poised on a pinnacle of wealth and power, yet we
live in a land of vanishing beauty, of increasing ugliness, of shrinking
open space, and of an over-all environment that is diminished daily by
pollution and noise and blight. This, in brief, is the quiet conversation
crisis of the 1960's.

Rodney A. Karlson, Chairman
William A. Allen
Norman C. Cross, Secretary
Mendall W. Colburn
Almon O. Ruggles
Andrew M. Bonnallie
John E. Rafferty
The Industrial Development Study Committee, in the course of its meetings over the year, consulted with members
of the State Department of Commerce, private consultants and others regarding the benefits to and reasons for the Town
having an Industrial Development Commission.
The Committee was unanimous in its decision to recommend that Lunenburg establish an Industrial Development
Commission for the following reasons: 1) Business looks for an Industrial Development Commission when looking for
sites and feels more welcome knowing a Commission exists; 2) Such a group could be very helpful in the long range
planning for the Town; 3) It is important to have a group that is well informed about the area and its potential; 4)
An Industrial Development Commission can help control the type of industry coming into Town; 5) Such a group could
be ready and able to take advantage of any large changes in the area, such as North-South highways or N.A.S.A.
The Committee recognizes that even with minimal areas and facilities to work with, thought and planning would
make Lunenburg better equipped for commercial expansion and help with a broader tax base. The Committee recog-
nizes that there are unlimited fields for the Industrial Development Commission to work in, but priority should be given
real estate, water supply, contacts and advertising.

Harold D. Harley
This committee has determined that the problems involved in finding space, either in existing buildings, or by
construction of new buildings in other areas, are such that a continuing study must be made. Immediate, although
admittedly temporary, recommendations are that changes in the lower rear of the Town Hall be made to accomodate
the welfare department, selectmen's quarters, and additional space for the town offices. It is also recommended,
again on a temporary basis, that certain interior changes be made on the second floor of the fire station to provide
additional space for the Police Department. It is hoped that through a continuing committee of this type, adequate
facilities can be recommended for the highway and other departments, allof which have outgrown their present quarters
due to the continued growth of the Town.
REGIONAL VOCATIONAL SCHOOL DISTRICT PLANNING COMMITTEE
Orren F. Bradstreet, Chairman
F. Clifton Miller
John C. Shields
The Lunenburg Regional Vocational School District Planning Committee was authorized by unanimous vote at the
Annual Town Meeting on March 7, 1964. The committee met with Mr. Ralph Brown, Superintendent of Schools, to
analyze local needs for vocational education. It was agreed that a survey should be conducted in the schools and this
is now being accomplished with the cooperation of the administration and staff of the schools. Legislation to authorize
a regional school planning board to investigate a vocational school was delayed because two cities were involved and
special legislation was, therefore, required.
In the interim, the State Division of Vocational Education with the able assistance of the Fitchburg Junior Chamber
of Commerce worked on a survey for the area communities involved. The survey was issued just prior to the November
10, 1964 organizational meeting. A copy of the survey is at theRitter Memorial Library for the public to read and study.
Three Planning meetings have been held and progress is being made. It is quite evident that the area is in dire
need of a regional vocational -technical school. This is based on national, state, and local studies. An Agreement
which is in the process of being written will be presented to each community for their consideration and approval if
they choose to take part in the regional district school. Included with the Agreement will be enrollment date, cost
data, geographic location of the school and approval by state agencies. Informational hearings for the public will
be set up. Target date is set for November 1965 for the establishment of the District.

POLICE
Sherwood G. Winchester, Chief
Frederick B. Hobbs, Sergeant
Roger A. Fitzgerald, Patrolman
Charles 1. Hannula, Jr., Patrolman
In 1964 we experienced many of the usual police problems, with many added new
ones. As in other years motor vehicle violations topped the list, and due to the increase
in complaints a second cruiser was requested for this purpose.
During the year we added a fourth patrolman, Charles 1. Hannula, Jr., andmuch of
his time will be spent on motor vehicle work. Mr. Hannula has spent weeks at the
State Police Academy and graduated December 11, 1964 with high honors. This gives
Lunenburg three Officers who have completed this school. Problems with loitering teen-
agers in the Whalom area grew to new proportions in 1964, and consumed much time and
money. For policing this area additional funds for 1965 are being requested.
For a second time additional office space has been requested. Aswe continue to
grow more work area is needed. We find it impossible to expand our file systemor desk
space in the present office. We need additional space for confidential interviews and
interrogation of routine police business.

Members of the Fire Department, Town Officials and Citizens alike were saddened by the resignation on April 1st

The Fire Department responded to 196 calls in 1964, an increase of 32 over the previous high of 164 calls in 1963.
Of the 196 calls 12 were building, 83 brush, 12 car or truck, 10 mutual aid, 4 rescue, 31 town dump, 15 service
calls, 11 resuscitator, 8 electrical, 5 accident and 5 false alarms. No fires of major proportions occurred during
the year. The drought was a prime factor in the large number of brush and grass fires andalso accounted for the un-
usually large number of fires at the town dump, ten of which extended into the woods, the remainder involving wetting
down or stand by service. Many of our brush and grass fires resulted from burning without a permit. Townspeople are
reminded that permits must be obtained from the Fire Department before outside burning is commenced. Incinerators
in bad repair or without covers also contributed to the excessive number of grass and brush fires. In September seven
Firemen attended a fire school held by the Meadowood Fire Department in Fitzwilliam, New Hampshire. The school
proved to be of value to both old and new members, as all phases of fire fighting and rescue work were covered. The
Fire Department held regular meetings every other Monday night with an average attendance of twenty-four men.
Educational movies were shown and outside were conducted. Street and hydrant location, pre-planned attack and
use of equipment were among the subjects discussed.
An inter-com system and wind velocity and direction gauge were purchased with company funds for use at the
Fire Station. The Company also purchased jackets for the Red Sox, the Little League Team which it sponsors, and
which won the 1964 championship. $328.00 was donated to charitable causes.

commenced to be answered from the Fire Station twenty-four hours per day. Previous to this, night calls were answered
in private homes from midnight on. These changes, which were authorized and approved by the Board of Selectmen
and Finance Committee, accounted for a $1,059 overdraft in the radio watch account. An increase in the Chief's
salary and additional compensation paid for care of the Station largely accounted for an overdraft of $1,304.33 in the
regular Fire budget. Chief Deming has requested that an appropriation be made at the annual meeting towards the
purchase of a tank truck to replace a 1948 Model (#4) which, because it contains a 1,200 gallon tank and prearranged
hose lines, is used for both building and brush fires and is particularly useful in outlying districts when water is in
particularly short supply. The Chiefhas expressed the opinion that the 1948 Model truck is no longer safe or dependable
for fire fighting. Other special purchases recommended in 1965 are additional radio equipment and two Scott Air Paks.
Funds have also been requested for painting the interior of the upstairs of the fire station.

TREE WARDEN
Richard W. Brown, Tree Warden
Moth Superintendent
Scientists agree that in all probability fresh water will be the first of his natural resources that man will exhaust.
Yet we tend to overlook the important part that trees play in conserving this very vital commodity. The wind and the
sun's heat have a great effect on vaporation. Trees slow down the speed of the wind and protect the earth's surface
from the sun. With its blanket of humus and its protection from chilling winds the forest soil is guarded against freezing
in the winter while the branches shade the snow against rapid melting. The soil is, therefore, always ready to receive
the water from the slowly melting snow while the surface of the hard, frozen fields nearby sheds the snow water like a
roof, as it floods off under the warmth of the spring sun.
So, in addition to beautifying our countryside, building timber, and providing environment for many plants and
animals, the forest also builds a reservoir to catch and store the huge amount of water it must have for growth. From
this reservoir springs and brooks carry life-giving water to land outside the forest.
In spite of these facts, in 1964, our Town saw fit to budget a niggardly $4,250 out of atotal budget of $1,327,000
for the use of the Tree Warden in replacing and eliminating dead and dying trees and in combatting the deadly insects
which are ravaging our shade trees and forests. It is hoped that a new awareness regarding the need for conservation
will cause our Citizenry to call for and promote an extensive program for the conservation of the trees in our Town.
Interested Townspeople are urged to express their feelings regarding this matter to the Board of Selectmen.

CONSERVATION -- TOWN FOREST
Harold D. Harley, Chairman

The efforts of the Conservation Commission and the Town Forest Committee, during the year of 1964, have been
mainly directed to preparing the Henry E. Cowdrey Reservation for an area that will be used by the citizens of
Lunenburg for recreational, educational and aesthetic purposes. Trails have been cut along both sides of the brook.
The old wagon and sled roads that were used more than half a century ago to haul out timber to the Gus Taylor saw-
mill off Mulpus Road have been plainly marked (this mill was located behind the present Frost home). The ruts worn
by the horses' feet, thesled runners and the wagon wheels can still be plainly seen although some trees, nearly a foot
in diameter, have since grown up in the center of the roads. The forests are beautiful and grand. The entire area,
especially the swamp area, abounds with wild life. The wild flowers and shrubs are delicately beautiful. Mulpus
Brook, which flows through the center of this two hundred andten acre area from end to end, for a distance of nearly
two miles, is a wonderful reality in scenic beauty.
One of the most interesting features of the "Cowdrey Lot" is a colony of beavers. These animals have built a
series of dams two hundred feet in length and in some places three feet high which have flooded forty acres of swamp
land with many million gallons of water. These dams, although nearly fifteen hundred feet from Massachusetts Avenue,
have pushed water back through the Flercum Brook under Massachusetts Avenue and along Elmwood Road, thus providing
water during a very dry 1964 for several wells which were dry during the summer of 1963. Several bridges will be
built across the brook for both hikers and horseback riders, and many attractive hiking trails and bridle paths and
picnic areas will be developed in the near future.
The purpose of conservation in Lunenburg is to set aside land for the benefit and use of the citizens of the Town,
to protect and preserve the natural resources and to enhance the beauty, the health, the well being and the wealth
of the community. Natural resources are the basic wealth of a country, and without them there would be neither
wealth nor country, and this would be just another uninhabited planet. The population of the world is increasing at
such an alarming rate that experts in this field foresee the time when there will not be enough natural resourses to
support the population. It is, therefore, for the benefit of all people, not only future but present generations, that we
strive to protect, preserve and increase our natural resources.
It was agreedwith the former owners of the Cowdrey Lot that there would be no hunting or trapping on this area.
It also seems to be the best plan for an area that will be used so much, by so many people, for so many different
purposes. It is hoped that every citizen of the town will help carry out this agreement. Some years ago the Town
voted to prohibit hunting in Lunenburg without a permit from the landowner. The Conservation Commission plans, in
the future, to open up to the hunters large portions of other areas.
One hundred and fifteen acres of land were acquired for conservation in 1964 and the Conservation Commission
plans to acquire more in 1965. The Commonwealth, under a "Self-Help Program", reimburses the Town fifty per cent
of the cost of land which has been approved by the Department of Natural Resources as suitable land for conservation.
In October Lunenburg had the good fortune of having two representatives of the Massachusetts Department of
Natural Resources spend a whole day in our Town for the purpose of examining conservation areas. Both men agreed
that nowhere in Massachusetts, including the State Parks, is there a more desirable area for conservation than the
Cowdrey Lot. The two visitors were Robert Hebb, Regional Forest and Park Supervisor for Worcester County, and
Egbert Hans, prominent planner for the Department of Natural Resources. During the past four years Mr. Hebb has
been a wonderful advisor to our Town's Conservation Program. Mr. Hans, who died in December, was rated very
highly in the conservation world. His enthusiasm, his talent and his energy knew no bounds. He was an inspiration
to everyone with whom he came in contact and leaves a great vacancy in the world of Natural Resources.

The total Nashoba budget for 1964 was $93,782, the total cost to the towns being $82,717, and to Lunenburg in particular
$10,060.50. The previous year's total budget was $77,624, the difference being due partly to a 10% salary increase and partly
to program expansion. The salary increase was prompted by a 20% increase for professional personnel and 10% for others in the
State Department of Public Health, Nashoba's chief competitor for staff recruitment. Nashoba salaries are still substantially below
those paid for comparable State posts. Expansion of the Well-Child Conferences and Dental Health Services accounted for the remainder
of the increase.
The Nashoba program places particular emphasis on Child Health protection. The Well-Child Conference offers health super-
vision for well pre-school children, primarily those whose health would not otherwise be supervised. The policy initiated last year
of emphasizing guidance for the mother in emotional and behavior problems of her child was continued. More time per child was
allowed in scheduling appointments so as to give adequate time for this guidance as well as for immunizations and physical examin-
ations. Despite this increased time the average time per child is still less than that recommended by child health authorities. The
aim of the Well-Child Conference is to prevent difficulties, both physical and emotional, and to promote the well being of children.
In Lunenburg 176 children made 339 visits at 23 conferences.
Nashoba services available to schools include the following: 1) Immunizations, 2) Tuberculin Testing, 3) Dental Prophylaxis,
4) Dental Health Program, 5) Consultation service and educational program for school nursing staff, 6) Periodic inspections of
school buildings and cafeterias, 7) Medical social service, 8) Epidemicological investigations and 9) Interpretation of public health
laws and regulations.
It is worth recording that there was not a single case of polio in the Commonwealth during 1964. This is a tribute to the ef-
ficacy of the polio immunization campaign. Let US hope that polio now joins smallpox as a disease that does notoccur in this
State. To make this true it is essential that people receive immunization as recommended. To forget immunization is to bring back
forgotten scourges. This is well recognized and is the basis of the Federal and State Vaccination Assistance Program which started in
1964 in two of our towns; the program will be extended to all other Nashoba towns during 1965. Lunenburg residents received im-
munization series and boosters as follows: Diphtheria -550; Smallpox -480; Polio -452.
There were 101 tuberculosis cases on the Nashoba register at the end of the year, consisting of 17 active and 84 inactive cases.
Tuberculin testing is today the first means of detection of tuberculosis. This procedure is carriedout in the Well-Child Conference and
is offered to first graders in school, to school personnel for certification and to selected cases at the Chest Clinic in Ayer. We are
grateful to the Middlesex and Worcester Health Associations for the tuberculin testing of our school children in 1964. Two hundred
eighty-two tuberculin tests were made in Lunenburg during 1964. Children with positive tests are today being given antituberculosis
drugs for periods of, usually, 18 months to prevent the germs from multiplying in the body. In 1964, Nashoba had five such children
on this preventive drug treatment. We changed over, during the year, to the Tine Tuberculin Test. This does away with the need
for syringes and needles, is better accepted by children and is less time consuming in mass programs. Arrangements were made with
Worcester County Health Association to mass X-ray six Nashoba towns during 1964. A total of 1,400 X-rays were done. No active
tuberculosis cases were found. The Chest Clinic at Ayer, asatellite of the Middlesex County Sanatorium, X-rayed 121 former tuber-
culosis patients or contacts at six sessions held at the Nashoba Community Hospital. We are proud to report that Nashoba was awarded
a Certificate for "outstanding service against tuberculosis and other respiratory disorders" by the Middlesex County Health Association.
The dental health program is carried out in the schools. Nashoba's dental hygienists clean the teeth and apply flouride solution
so as to make them more resistant to caries. A careful inspection of the teeth is done and, if necessary, a note is sent home to advise
parents to take their children to their family dentist. The aim of the program is to offer this prophylaxis at three-year intervals,
usually in the 2nd, 5th and 8th grades. During the school year 1963-64, grades 2, 5 and 8 in Lunenburg were offered dental prophy-
laxis. Of the 452 children receiving care, 188 or 42% were referred to their dentists. The dental program includes health education.

WELFARE
Gladys M. Goodale, Director
The specific function of the Welfare Department is to process applications for the various categories of assistance
and to determine initial and continuing eligibility. The Welfare office is also available to assist any person in meet-
ing a social need, by consultation or by referral to other social agencies.
The programs of assistance are Aid to Families with Dependent Children, General Relief, Disability Assistance,
Old Age Assistance and Medical Aid for the Aged. The cost of these programs is shared by Federal, State and Local
Governments. Want, or need, is determined in Massachusetts by computing budgets according to the State Standards
of Assistance. Each budget is figured with regard to the individual need and the resources of the applicant. Assistance
notonly refers to money payments to recipients but also payments to doctors, drugstores, hospitals, nursing homes and
others supplying individuals with medical or remedial care.
The Massachusetts Medical Aid for the Aged program was established in October of 1960. Under this plan medical
care only is provided for persons 65 years of age or over whose income and resources are insufficient to meet the cost
of medical services. This medical care may be provided in the home or in a recognized medical facility. During the
past year twenty-nine persons received medical assistance on this program. At the present time fourteen persons are
in Nursing Homes. Although the majority of these men and women receive a Federal Old Age and Survivors Insurance
check each month, this income is not enough to cover their living expenses and their medical needs.
New emphasis in our State and Federal Programs is on retraining and rehabilitation so that many more persons,
especially young adults, can become self-supporting. In our area a cooperative youth program has been instituted.
Commencing in January, courses in auto mechanics, paint spraying, machinery, cooking, "clerking", and clerical
work will be held in Fitchburg and Leominster for young men and women who are unemployable they lack skills.
Individual instruction according to needwill be emphasized and other courses will be added as the need arises. Any-
one desiring more information regarding this program is urged to contact either Mrs. Goodale, Mrs. Harley at the Jr.-
Sr. High School, or the Employment Office in Fitchburg.

There are two distinct functions carried out by this Department, Veterans' Services and Veterans' Benefits.
Among the many and varied services available free of charge to a Veteran and his family are information regard-

The bigstory of 1964 was the completion of the addition to the Library. The new wing was finished and occupied
in March, but the renovations to the existing building and the outside work were not finished for another two months.
Due to the drought, the planting around the outside; being donated by the Women's Club, will have to be done this year.
The trustees were well pleased with the design and imagination of Dr. Arland Dirlam, the architect, and also were
satisfied with the job performed by the contractor, the Crowley Realty Corporation, and from the compliments received,
we feel that the townspeople agree. One of the reasons for the addition was to provide adequate facilities for the
elementary school age children, and the librarians report that this age is making greatly increased use of the library,
and do come in and sit and read or look at books, which they were never able to do before.
A year ago the Trustees reported that, due to the desire of many people to participate in the expansion program,
money for furnishing the new addition would be raised by private subscription. To that end, a committee was formed,
headed by Mrs. Ralph S. Foster, and a campaign for funds which included donations by the school children was under-
taken, and a total of $6733.78 wasraised. This amount covered the costs of the furnishings. A list of all contributors
was included among the papers placed in the corner stone.
The laying of the corner stone and the dedication of the new library were combined in exercises held June 5,
1964, after which an open house was held. Dr. Arland Dirlam was the principal speaker. On October 15th the
librarians also held an open house and reception for the teachers, which was very well attended.
Despite the dislocations caused by construction during the first part of the year, circulation again reached a new
high, totaling 51,217. Abreakdown of this total circulation figure is as follows: Adult - 17, 101; Children - 32,351;
Periodicals - 1,765. With 1965 being the first full year of the operation of the enlarged library, indications are that
we will greatly increase this figure during that year.
Two hundred-seventy adult books and six hundred thirty-five children's books were added by purchase, and one
hundred-seven volumes were given by the following persons: Mrs. J. Simonds Hurd, Mrs. Louis Vitelli, Mrs.
Laurence Ebersole, Mrs. Donald Cragin, Miss Vivian Keene, Miss Betty Winchester, Mrs. James Mann, Mrs. Bigelow
Crocker, Jr., Herbert Sargent, Mrs. William Allen, The Four Corner Club in memory of Mrs. John Kenney, Mrs.
Charles Hillman, Mrs. Paul Ellis, Miss Minnie Martin, Walter White, Henry Pope, Miss Hazel Lloyd, Mrs. Blair
MacLean, James Jameson, Robert Marr, Mrs. G. Barry Whitcomb, The Lunenburg Teachers Association, Mrs. Rad-
cliffe P. Cheesman, and Mrs. Kathryn Wight.
In line with the policy of removing outworn and outdated books, one hundred seventy-eight adults' and one
hundred fifty-four children's books were discarded.
To keep the list of borrowers up to date, new cards should be issued periodically. On June 19, 1964 such a
system was started, and by January 7th two thousand one hundred eighty-nine cards had been issued.
Lunenburg is part of the Regional Library System. Many books which would not have been available to the Town
otherwise, have been borrowed under the Regional Plan. Any Lunenburg borrower may obtain a regional card which
will enable him to borrow books in Fitchburg and surrounding communities.
Fines in the amount of $946.91 were collected and turned over to the Town Treasurer.
