"THE BUCK STOPS HERE"
For several weeks, there was a sign hanging on the front of the Selectmen's table proclaiming that "The Buck
Stops Herel ". While this sign was posted in jest, it is an indication of the number and variety of problems that come
to the Board of Selectmen for resolution.
Who is "right" when someone shoots a neighbor's dog for killing his ducks? What can be done about a street
drainage problem so severe that a mother is afraid to have her child cross the icy street? What kind of lighting is best
for the Center of Town?
While there is no question about "where the buck stops", the major effort for gathering background information,
recommending courses of action and implementing decisions rests with Town employees, the many Town Committee
members, and with the Secretary to the Board of Selectmen. The complete activities of Town Departments and Com-
mittees are described elsewhere in this Report. However, a few highlights are mentioned herewith:
(1) Dick Brown has retired as our Highway Superintendent as of the end of 1972. Dick served the Town for 23
years including 8 years as Superintendent. The Board of Selectmen wish to publicly acknowledge the contribution that
Dick has made to the Town over the years and to wish him well in his retirement.
-4-
===PAGE 7===
(2) Continued progress is being made in the handling ofsolid waste disposal within the Town of Lunenburg. The
Municipal Refuse Disposal Areahas operated under a controlled program with specific hours and with an Attendant for
approximately 2 years. While our disposal operation does not meet all of the State's environmental statures, we do
comply with many parts of it including the "no burning" provision. The Refuse Disposal Planning Committee isactively
working with the State and Regional authorities to the end that Lunenburg will participate in the best overall solution
to this problem.
(3) During 1972, the State Legislature enacted legislation which limited the size of a voting district. As a
result, Lunenburg has been divided into two voting precincts. In order to minimize the expense of the extra election
officials to cover the two precincts, the Massachusetts Selectmen's Association (at the request of the Lunenburg Board
of Selectmen) has introduced a bill in the Legislature to allow one set of election officials to handle the returns from
more than one precinct.
(4) The Board of Selectmen as the Local Licensing Authority held nine hearings during the year. These hearings
were held relative to applications submitted for renewal of licenses and also applications for issuance of initial li-
censes for the sale of various types of alcoholic beverages. Of the nine hearings conducted, three were for violation
of M.G.L.A. Chapter 138, Section 34 (sale and/or delivery of alcoholic beverages to a person under twenty-one.)
(5) This was the second year that the Town has participated in the Federal Emergency Employment Act of 1971.
As of December 31, 1972 a total of four personnel (two regular Police Officers, one Attendant at the Municipal Refuse
Disposal Area and one Police/Fire Radio Dispatcher) are employed under this Act and this has amounted to approxi-
mately $26,485.73 in federal funds under this program during 1972.
(6) Rick Eley has been Secretary to the Board of Selectmen for five years. It is fitting that we recognizehis
significant contribution to the office of the Board of Selectmen in our Town Report. People who deal with the Board
of Selectmen frequently realize that Rick is one of the main reasons the office operates smoothly and efficiently. In
the complicated areas of Zoning, Licensing and Board of Health interpretations, Rick is able to give on the spot guid-
ance so that red tape is minimized.
General: One of the most important duties of the Board of Selectmen is to appoint members to the various Town
Committees. There are approximately 40 Town Committees involving over 100 members. During 1972, approximately
95 appointments and/or re-appointments were made by the Board of Selectmen. The Town has been very fortunate that
many interested, capable and reliable townspeople are now serving. However, with ever-increasing demands being
made upon our time-whether it be regular work or volunteer-it becomes more difficult to find dedicated people who are
willing and able to serve. If you are interested, have the time, and particularly if you have some expertise in some
Town area, please let your Selectmen know about it.
In the administration of "The Buck Stops Here", we can use all the help we can get.
Richard J. Eley, Board Secretary

Board of Assessors
The past year brought several personnel changes to the Board of Assessors. Mr. Leroy Allen, a member of the
Board for seven years, did not seek re-election. Mr. William Reed was elected to fill this office. Also, the clerk
for the Boardof Assessors for the past thirteen years, Mrs. Kathleen Deming, retired in July and Mrs. Donna LeCuyer
was hired to fill this vacancy.
During the year, Mr. John F. Nicoll, Chairman of the Board of Assessors, attended the Massachusetts Assessors'
School at the University of Massachusetts in Amherst. All Board members attended a school for assessors at Quinsiga-
mond Community College in Worcester. This school was sponsored by the Department of Corporations and Taxation
and attendance was mandatory by State Law for all assessors. Mrs. LeCuyer attended several meetings designed to
assist her in her regular duties and also in the problems dealing with the new eighteen month fiscal year cycle.
In July, the Board of Assessors accompanied by Town Counsel, Robert J. Larkin, appeared before the Appellate
Tax Board in Boston in an attempt to have the equalized valuation of the Town of Lunenburg lowered. Although un-
successful in this attempt, valuable experience was gained in the preparation and presentation of such a case before
the Appellate Tax Board.
The Assessors have attempted to maintain the program which was established when revaluation was accompi ished
two years ago. They have been reviewing properties, maintaining sales records and have held extra meetings in order
to keep the program up-to-date. The Assessors' regular meeting night was changed from Wednesday to Monday evening
thus giving the Board the opportunity to readily obtain or exchange information with the Board of Selectmen and Town
Counsel who also meet on Monday evenings.
Although building permits are required for all new constructionand/or alterations, the Assessors discovered sev-
eral violations and turned these names over to the Board of Selectmen for their attention and action. Lunenburg con-
tinues to grow with 121 building permitsbeing issued in 1972. This construction increased our total valuation for Real
Estate to $49,847,600.00 for 1972. Personal property valuation amounted to $2,180,490.00 for a total valuation for
the Town of Lunenburg of $52,028,090.00. This is an increase of $849, 140.00 over 1971. In spite of the increase in
taxable property, increased expenditures for town services has resulted in the tax rate going from $33.00 in 1970 to
$46.00 in 1972. The Board of Assessors reminds all taxpayers that only a joint effort by all Town officialsand realistic
spending at the town meetings can make our tax rate remain somewhat stable.

Town Clerk, Tax Collector, Treasurer
This has been an especially busy year for the Town Clerk's office with two town
meetings, two primaries and the State and Presidential Elections. Over 900 new
voters were registered. Preparation for the transition to a Fiscal Year involved a
considerable amount of time, thought and effort on the part of the Treasurer's office
as did the Federal Revenue Sharing Program, both of which are discussed below.
In the Tax Office expressions of concern regarding burgeoning property tax
bills have become more and more frequent. In a poll conducted for the U.S. Advisory
Commission on Intergovernmental Relations, a sampling of Americans were asked,
"What is the worst tax of all Forty-five percent answered, "The Property Tax".
Although this comes as no surprise to local, state and federal officials, to date gov-
ernment at all levels has not come to grips with the problem and there seems to be no
Mary L. Padula
solution in sight. Meanwhile, many taxpayers are running out of money and patience,
and one group, the one which is certainly the hardest hit - the aging and the elderly-
is also running out of time and simply cannot wait very much longer. It is a fact that in Massachusetts homeowners
70 years of age or over receive an exemption of $350. However, most people are forced to retire at age 65 and even
with the $350 exemption, in our Town the majority of our aging homeowners are still faced with a tax bill ranging from
$300 to $900 while many of their total incomes are in the $3000 to $5000 bracket. This means that they are still paying
anywhere from 10% to 30% of their total income in local property taxes.
An affluent society can have no excuse for putting this type of tremendous burden on these very low income house-
holders in order to finance its schools and local government, nor is there any excuse for driving them from the homes
in which they planned to live out their years. In several states they use a system pioneered by Wisconsin, which pro-
vides that taxes in excess of a predetermined percentage of total income be abated. Massachusetts should adopt such
a system pending a more widespread solution to its property tax dilemma.
In 1969, the State Legislature passed what has come to be known as "The Fiscal Cycle Bill", which has far-reaching
effects on every city and town in the Commonwealth. The original Bill was so poorly conceived that in 1969 and again
in 1970 the Legislature postponed action on it. In 1971, 23 pages of corrections were drafted, and the Bill was once
more placed before the Legislature, whereupon in spite of protests by the State Division of Accounts, who were much
concerned over the errors, omissions and inconsistencies of even the corrected version, it was passed. Further amendments
and corrections are being worked on even as the Bill goes into effect.
One of the main features of this legislation is to effect twice-a-year ling of Real Estate Taxes. In the 18 month
transitional year, two part bills will be mailed in June for the entire 18 month period, but only 2/3 of the bill, or 12
months worth, will become overdue on November 1. The last 1/3 will become overdue on April I (to be changed to
May 1). In subsequent years 12 month bills will be mailed in June, with 1/2 becoming overdue on November 1 and
1/2 becoming overdue on May 1. This means that the banks will be paying over tax monies twice a year, in October
and April. The net effect on the taxpayer is that he will be paying the tax for the first half of a calendar year six
months earlier than heretofore. The advantage to the Town which this "speedup" represents is difficult to evaluate in
advance. Theoretically the extra payment will save the Town money, because we will not have to borrow as much in
anticipation of revenue, and therefore will pay out less interest. However, the increased costs of extra clerical services
involved in the duplicate billing process will probably equal or even exceed the savings on interest.
On October 20, President Nixon signed into law a five-year Revenue Sharing Program which entitles communit
to quarterly payments from the Federal Government. Generally, there are few strings attached to the expenditure of
revenue sharing funds. Units of local government have the discretion to use their revenue sharing payments for broad
categories of priority expenditures described in the Actand on any lawful ordinary and necessary capital expenditures.
Federal grants received in the past have always been for very specific purposes and closely regulated by the State and
Federal governments. The Revenue Sharing Program reflects the belief of the present Administration that the govern-
ment closest to the people can best solve many of the pressing domestic problems that confront our society. Revenue
sharing funds amounting to $40,141 were received by our community in 1972. It is anticipated that we will receive
approximately $100,000 in the next fiscal year.
Fees received by the Town Clerk in 1972 amounted to $2283.00.

At last year's Town Meeting the Town voted
to increase the minimum lot size to 40,000 square
feet. The proponents of this amendment felt that this
was necessary in order to insure an orderly growth
in the town. The main reasons for this were retention
of aesthetic values, need for conservation of natural
resources, concern for proper sewage disposal, and
for water supply.
The Planning Board, working with developers
having either single lots or subdivisions, has at-

as its overall guide.

row type subdivision fashion. The Board, in working
with them, reduced the number of potential lots to
198, and in doing so, provided approximately 20
acres of untouched open space surrounding the lots
which were finally laid out in a "cluster zone"
design. This subdivision was subsequently disap-
proved, primarily because of potential sewage dis-
posal problems.

of last year's zoning amendment, the Board re-

Park Commission
Recreation wise -- this has been a banner year for the people of Lunenburg.
Participation in Lunenburg's recreational activities increased in 1972. There are now 125 people taking part
in junior basketball -- the most ever. The number of people participating in junior hockey tripled in 1972 andwe
now have seven teams. Wednesday night "after High School basketball gym" has been organized into a five team,
eleven game league and offers more wintertime recreation for Lunenburg's young men. Girls' softball, which ap-
peared to be a lost cause a year ago, picked up momentum under the direction of "Chuck" Winneberg this past sum-
mer and hopefully an area league will get under way in 1973. Tennis participation tripled in attendance this year
and now includes tournaments with players from other towns.
The Park Commission also supports teenage (non-team playing) basketball for both boys and girls; free skating
at Marshall Pond and Lake Whalom; winter tennis; adult physical education; A.A.U. track meets; men's softball and
golf; scotch foursome golf league; Pop Warner football; Little League baseball and morning baseball and teenage
summer basketball. Our summer recreational activities included the Arts & Crafts program at the Center Park and
Wallis Park; a swimming program in conjunction with the Lunenburg Red Cross; a Babe Ruth baseball league and
summer trips including trips to the New England Aquarium and Willard Brook. A Muscular Dystrophy Fair was held
again this year and proved very successful.
The Park Commission is constantly striving to maintain and improve Park facilities and equipment. At Marshall
Park the fence at the tennis courts was repaired, and runways were installed for long jump, pole vault and high jump.
Work done at the Town Beach this year included the replacement of two doors and painting of the building and the
fence. Also painted was the storage building at Marshall Park and the office on West Street which has also been
renovated and now houses the Park equipment previously stored outside. With the help of many Hockey Booster Club
members, the lights at the Town Beach are being repaired so that townspeople may enjoy skating at night. Your
Park Commission is working on a hockey rink and have a possible site which is large enough for the hockey rink,
softball field and tennis courts. Plans for this project are still in the early stages.
The future of Lunenburg recreation calls for a full-time Recreational Director. This person would have the time
to look into the many government-financed programs which would give Lunenburg better facilities for less money
plus a better organized recreational program.

Library Trustees
The Ritter Memorial Library is considered to be the cultural center of the Town and complements and supplements
the activities of the schools and the Historical Society. In addition to the regular book lending program, a substantial
broadened scope of activity was implemented during 1972.
A Sunday night film program during the winter featured American as well as foreign films for high school and
other adult audiences. A "Friends of the Library" group has been organized to sponsor exhibits and speakers for in-
terested people; including an exhibit of some of the products of the Lunenburg Arts & Crafts Center.
The Story Hour for pre-school children has continued to be very successful. Mrs. Emily Foster and Mrs. Gloria
O'Brien have been sharing the reading duties. After the reading, the children sing songs or do pantomime related to
the stories that they have just heard. During the summer a five-week film program for children was also well attended.
In making use of the Regional Lending Service, phonograph records were introduced as a new item at the library.
new feature was very successful, and hopefull the library will be able to supplement the record coll ection themselves
during the coming years.
A collection of books continues to be maintained the Pearl Brook Housing Centerand is changed periodically.
The seven third grade classes from the Annex use the library weekly. The library filled 85 inter-library requests this
year and lent 285 films to patrons. Purchased this year were 530 adult books and 258 children's books. A total of
$998.73 in fines was collected during 1972 and turned over to the Town.
The lending statistics for 1972 are: Adult Books 19, 160, Children's Books 27,595, Magazines 1, 838, and
Records 116, for a total circulation figure of 48,709.
Books received by the library as gifts came from the following people: Mr. John Lemay, Mrs. William Harris,
Miss Nancy Damon, Mr. Donald Cragin, Mrs. Raymond Little, Mr. Richard Storrs, Mrs. Eric Smith, Mrs. Seymour
Schnuer, Mrs. Bigelow Crocker, Jr., Misses Hazel & Minnie Martin, Mrs. John Boursy, Mr. Carleton Mott, Mr. &
Mrs. Albert Moisan, Mrs. Ruth Proctor, Mrs. John Pieksen, Mr. Harry Tozer and Mr. Wesley Fisher. The Memorial
Fund gave the library two excellent reference works: the 21 volume New Cambridge Modern History and the 5 volume
Praeger Encyclopedia of Art. Both the Woman's Club and the American Association of University Women gave gifts
of books to the library.
The library isopen from 10:00 A.M. to 9:00 P.M. Monday through Thursday, 10:00 A.M. to 5:00P .M. Fridays,
and 1:00 P.M. to 5:00 P.M. on Saturdays.

Personnel Committee
Since the Annual Town Meeting of 1972 the tempo of Personnel Committee activity has increased a great deal.
Beginning in April Ct. substantial amount of time has been spent negotiating an agreement with Local 353 of the Inter-
national Brotherhood of Police Officers which now represents Lunenburg's Police Department.
In December Mrs. Doris Hartz was hired to increase the efficiency of the Committee by taking minutes of meetings,
keeping records, handling correspondence, etc., thereby allowing members of the Personnel Committee to spend more
time reviewing job descriptions, negotiating, updating the Salary Administration Plan, and carrying out many other
activities to keep Lunenburg up-to-date in its employee relations for the benefit of the employees and the townspeople.
Pay scales and other benefits of surrounding communities comparable in size to Lunenburg have again been studied
and have been a helpful guide in revising Lunenburg's Classification and Salary Schedule and Salary Administration
Plan. Some reduction in the rate of inflation is reported by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and this has been consid-

Ellen Kemp and Mr. Robert J. Blanchflower.

Conservation Commission & Town Forest Committee
Henry David Thoreau wrote: "It would be worthwhile if in each town there were a committee appointed to see
that the beauty of the town received no detriment". It seems fitting that the State of Massachusetts was the first in
the nation to follow the sage advice of one of its foremost naturalists by creating in 1957 the Conservation Commissions.
Your local committees have attempted over the past ten years to preserve and protect some of Lunenburg's
natural areas. In 1972 the Legislature further broadened the responsibilities of the local Conservation Commissions
by directing them to implement the Wetlands Protection Act.
This law states that no person shall remove,
fill, dredge or alter any bank, beach, marsh, meadow or swamp bordering on any existing creek, river, stream,
pond or lake without first having a hearing with the Conservation Commission. Your Commission had three such
hearings this year.
Increasingly the community is becoming more involved in the cause of conservation of land in Lunenburg. The
local 4-H Clubs were awarded a $100 Reader's Digest Grant for a conservation proposal. The further development
of Clark's Hill Reservation as a bird sanctuary was their chosen project. These ambitious youth planted fifty autumn
olives and fifty tartarian honeysuckle; cleared the trail to the top of the hill; erected birdhouses; provided an addi-
tional picnic table for the property; and made a sign for the area. The Commission is grateful for their help and
enthusiasm. Likewise, the Lunenburg Memorial Fund has continued its support of our program by contributing fruit
trees for the sanctuary as a further inducement to wildlife.
Two additional pieces of were added to the green areas. Both were gifts Carl and Irene Proctor's twelve
acre tract of land on Elmwood Road and Misses Bertha and Fernal Brown's three acre tract off Northfield Road. Also,
a Memorial Fund in Irving Kimball's memory was established by his family and friends for improvements to the
Cowdrey Nature Center.
A Natural Resource Inventory Report, prepared by the Worcester County Conservation District, was completed
in 1972. The purpose of the report was to make an assessment of the Town's natural resources and to identify the
opportunities for natural resource development and preservation consistent with'the Town's objectives. Copies of this
report are available for the asking.
An additional feature to our activities this year has been the "Naturalist-At-Large Program" conceived and
executed by Douglas S. Fleming, a member of the Commission and a biology-geology teacher at the High School.
One Sunday a month, Doug takes townspeople by foot, snowshoe, or crosscountry ski on an interpretative through
different town-owned Conservation lands. The response to this program has been enthusiastic, with an average of
thirty-five persons participating each month.
The Conservation Commission, with your support, will continue in its efforts for the preservation and wise use
of open space.
Douglas Fleming of the Conservation Commission conducts one of his monthly Naturalist-At-Large tours in Proctor Park.

Refuse Disposal Planning Committee
With the ban on open burning and the strict enforcement of State laws concerning solid waste disposal, it has
become evident that a fresh look must be taken of our Municipal Refuse Disposal Area. This Committee continues to
explore the possibility of joining with neighboring towns in a regional endeavor which has been unsuccessful to date.
However, in the meantime, an engineering firm has been engaged to evaluate Lunenburg's present disposal
facility and to suggest alternate sites if it should prove that the present site is unacceptable. It is expected that this
study will be completed in 1973.

During the past year there were several inquiries made by concerns interested in our two largest tracts of com-
mercially zoned property; the Aracomsite on Chase Road (Route 13) in the north end and the site on Shirley-Leominster
Road in the south end. All interest immediately ceased as soon as these concerns found out that there is no water
available at these sites and no immediate plans to provide any. In all cases the interested parties were not heavy
process users but only wanted water to feed sprinklers for fire protection.
The Development & Industrial Commission has been aware of this problem for some time and has tried to convey
this message to the Town as well as the members of the Water District. During last year's Town Meeting the voters
voiced their unanimous approval for what the Development & Industrial Commission was trying to accomplish-that is,
to bring small, clean, light industry into the Town, to be located in industrial parks away from the population centers,
and to be buffered by large tracts of conservation property so that growth of industries could be controlled and, if need
be, limited. The motivation behind this plan was to provide a broader tax base whereby the overburdened taxpayer
might find some relief.
However, it has become clear that the Town, the Development & Industrial Commission, the voters, and the
taxpayers, will have little to sayabout industry so long as the water users control expansion of the Water District. The
problem of expanding the District is a perplexing one since under present conditions the cost to do this must be totally
borne by the existing users. It does not seem fair for the Town's taxpayers to expect the water users to foot the bill for
potential industrial growth and neither does it seem fair for the water users to restrain a broadening of the tax base.
As one possible solution to this dilemma, the Development & Industrial Commission together with the Water
District Commissioners is investigating potential sources of federal funding that will allow a major expansion of the
District without overburdening the users. In conjunction with the Montachusett Regional Planning Commission, tech-
niques for requesting funds are being pursued for the following: a. Additional well and pumping facilities; b. In-
creased storage capacity; and C. Transmission piping to both the north and south of town. This sounds like a reason-
able plan since we understand that a Water District can legally request funds in the same way as a town can.
Assuming a Water District can legally obtain government funds, the Water District users would first have to vote
to request these funds before any action is taken. Should this occur and should funds be made available, the water
users again would have to vote to accept the total plan and their share of the expenses could range from 15% to 50%
of the total cost. Based upon these uncertainties, the Development & Industrial Commission recommends a Water Study
Committee be formed to review the Town's role in assuming the duties of the Water District. Until such time as the
Water District or the Town can provide water for industrial use, the Development & Industrial Commission will continue
its rather passive role in Town government.

Around 1875, in a small province in the northeast
part of France, a young boy on his way to school was
savagely attacked and bitten by a wild dog. A man work-
ing nearby rushed to the boy's aid and was able to free
him. The boy ran home in a state of shock and told his
parents of the dreadful and frightening incident. He was
taken to the family physician who adminstered the boy's
wounds and then told his parents "this is all I can do".
During this period of medical infancy not much hope
lay ahead for the survival of a person who had been
bitten by an animal thought to be rabid.
This occurred about 100 years ago when the over
abundance of rabid and feral (wild) dogs was the major
cause of the dread disease "hydrophobia". The now
famous Louis Pasteur worked endlessly until he found the
antidote which allowed control of this disease.
One hundred years later, we can control the
disease but the problem of wild dogs is still with US.
Wild dogs, in general, are forced to spend practically
all of their time in search of food. This search continues
to cause the loss of cherished pets, destruction of private

Highway Department
What have highways done for you lately? It so happens that there is a long road of answers to that question.
We take highways for granted, just as we do so many other necessities, until we are deprived of them. The most vocif-
erous advocate of good roads isn't the motorist happily cruising 70 mph on a new interstate expressway--it's the poor
motorist already late for work who gets stuck in a traffic jam on Main Street. Only then the horns of anguish heard
throughout the land. The American desire for mobility has helped knit the country together, dissolving both distance
and differences. Its two principal by-products the motor vehicle and the highways have contributed immeasurably
to our high standard of living and to the variety of ways we can enjoy the amenities of life.
What is the cost? For an average of 30 cents a day we have the down payment on a ticket anywhere in this
beautiful country, with our family and on our own schedule, stopping whenever we want, perhaps towing a camper or
boat with us, seeing and doing things that most people only read or dreamed about a few years ago. Is it too big a
price to pay? Can weafford to abandon or neglect our highways? In the absence of any acceptable alternatives to the
motor vehicle in the foreseeable future, the answer has to be no.

Police Department
Traffic accidents were responsible for a bleak year in Lunenburg in 1972. An unprecedented six fatal accidents,
coupled with a twenty per cent increase in total accidents in one calendar year, dictate the way for strict enforce-
ment and the establishment of safety drives and programs during 1973. In 1972 there was a significant reduction in
the number of house breaks. Citizens should be commended for their cooperation and continued support in reporting
suspicious motor vehicles and persons around private homes and businesses.
Training and schooling of regular officers contined with Patrolmen August N. Misner, Alphonse J. Baron and
Steven G. Whitney completing basic training schools as required by law. Plans call for Patrolmen Michael A.
B ottalico and Richard A. Lambert, Jr. to undergo similar training in the coming year. Sergeants Roger A. Fitzgerald
and Hector J. Morin attended training sessions on crime scene search techniques. Sergeant Fitzgeraldnow spends
most of histime as court officer andnewly appointed Sergeant Morin has the authority to supervise regular and reserve
officers working between 6:00 P.M. and 2:00 A.M.
The Town now has three police cruisers and the Federal Communications Commission has granted an additional
police frequency that will relieve traffice on the overcrowded existing local government frequency and should prove
of value during times of emergency situations. Three dual frequency radios for the cruisers and two walkie-talkies
were purchased in 1972. Requested communications equipment for the 1973 program should adequately take care of
the police department needs for the foreseeable future.
Pavement marking equipment was obtained through Governor Sargent's Highway Safety Bureau under 100 per
cent reimbursable funds. Purchase of a "Breatholizer" is not recommended until the department has more adequate
facilities. It is hoped that all fficers will undergo training to become quali to administer the so-called "Breatholizer
Test". Recognition should be given to the Exchange Club for the purchase of anti-litter signs that will be erected in
suitable locations in the spring.
The Board of Selectmen, on recommendation from the Building Committee, has filed a "letter of intent" with
the Massachusetts Civil Defense Agency signifying that consideration is being given to erecting a new municipal
building that will encompass a fully federally approved Emergency Operating Center and Police Station. The Emer-
gency Operating Center, except during periods of local or national emergencies, would be utilized by the Police
Department and funds under a 50 per cent reimbursement factor are available, if approved, for the portion of the
building and equipment considered as "EOC".
An adequate police facility for Lunenburg is needed in the interest of the public. The present quarters do not
provide adequate space for efficient operation. There is a lack of privacy for the Chief of Police and officers con-
ducting investigations. There is a lack of provisions for security for the storage of evidence and for garaging of
vehicles and there are no detention facilities.
As the Town grows, so do the demands for service. Increased manpower, equipment, caseloads and the need
for more sophisticated and complex court preparation is hampered by the physical set up of the police department.
It is recommended that a police facility which can achieve these results be made available.

Tree Warden
The oak leaf skeletonizer was on the decrease this year. This pest becomes a problem only if a homeowner has
oak trees on his property. It can be controlled by spraying about June 1st when most of the eggs have hatched and
worms can be seen
During 1972, 178 diseased trees were cut down, chipped and buried. The Department of Natural Resources gave
three days of sampling elm trees amounting to $150.00, and six days of removal work, amounting to $600.00. Forty-
seven trees were tagged and samples sent to the Shade Tree Laboratory, University of Massachusetts, for culture. A
total of $6,916.93 was spent under the Dutch Elm disease account, of which $260.35 was reimbursed by the County.
Fifteen Maples and five Mountain Ash were purchased with Tree Warden funds to replace diseased trees. A total
of $3,730.00 was spent under this account.

Report of State Audit
Mr. Gordon A. McGill, Director of Accounts
Department of Corporations and Taxation
Boston, Massachusetts
Sir:
In accordance with your instructions, I have made an audit of the books and accounts of the town of Lunenburg
for the period from August 1, 1968, the date of the previous audit, to June 17, 1972, and report thereon as follows:
The financial transactions, as recorded on the books of the several departments collecting money for the town
or committing bills for collection, were examined, checked, and verified by comparison with the books and records of
the town treasurer and the town accounting officer.
The books and accounts of the town accounting officer were examined and checked. The ledgers were analyzed,
the recorded receipts being compared with the treasurer's books and with the records of the departments collecting money
for the town, while the payments, as entered, were checked with the treasury warrants. The appropriation accounts
were checked with the town clerk's records of financial votes passed by the town meetings and with the finance com-
mittee's authorizations of transfers from the reserve fund. The necessary audit adjustments were made, and a balance
sheet, a copy of which is appended to this report, was prepared showing the financial condition of the town on June 17,
1972.
The appended balance sheet shows overdrafts in two appropriation accounts totaling $14,758.81, which over-
drafts are contrary to the provisions of General Laws, Chapter 44, Section 31 because written approval from the State
Director of Accounts was not requested and received before such overdrafts were made for snow removal.
The books and accounts of the town treasurer were examined and checked in detail. The recorded receipts were
analyzed and compared with the records of the departments making payments to the treasurer and with other sources from
which money was paid into the town treasury, while the disbursements were checked with the warrants approved by the
selectmen and with the accounting officer's books.
The cash book additions were verified, and the treasurer's cash balance on June 17, 1972 was proved by the
reconciliation of the bank balances with statements furnished by the banks of deposits, by examination of the savings
bank books, and by actual count of the cash in the office.
The maturing debt and interest payments were proved with the amounts falling due and were checked with the
cancelled securities on file. The outstanding coupons on June 17, 1972 were listed and reconciled with the balance
in the bond and coupon account, as shown by a statement received from the bank of deposit.
The records of payroll deductions for Federal and State taxes, the retirement systems, credit union, the United
Fund, group insurance, teachers' insurance, teachers' association union dues, and for school employees' annuities
were examined and checked, the deductions were footed, the payments to the proper agencies were verified, and the
balances on hand were reconciled with the controlling ledger accounts.
The records of tax titles and tax possessions held by the town were examined and checked. The transfers from
the tax levies to the tax title account were compared with the collector's books, the reported redemptions were checked
with the treasurer's recorded receipts, the foreclosures were verified, and the tax titles and tax possessions on hand were
listed, reconciled with the accounting officer's ledger controls, and checked with the records at the Registry of Deeds.
The savings bank books and securities representing the investments of the several trust and investment funds in the
custody of the town treasurer were examined and listed, the income being proved and all transactions and balances
being verified.
The books and accounts of the tax collector were examined and checked in detail. The tax, excise, and assess-
ment accounts outstanding at the time of the previous audit, as well as all subsequent commitments, were audited and
proved. The recorded collections were compared with the payments to the treasurer, as shown by the treasurer's and
the accounting officer's books; the abatements, as entered, were checked with the assessors' records of abatements
granted; the transfers to the tax title account were proved; and the outstanding accounts were listed and reconciled
with the controlling accounts in the accounting officer's ledger.
-39-
===PAGE 42===
Further verification of the outstanding tax and excise accounts was made by mailing notices to a number of per-
sons whose names appeared on the books as owing money to the town, the replies received thereto indicating that the
accounts, as listed, are correct.
The records of deferred street assessments were examined. The payments in advance were compared with the
treasurer's recorded receipts, and the amounts added to the tax levies were verified. The financial records of the town
clerk were examined and checked. The collections on account of dog and sporting licenses issued, and from miscel-
laneous charges, were verified and compared with the payments to the Division of Fisheries and Game and to the town
treasurer.
The records of departmental cash collections by the board of selectmen, the sealer of weights and measures, and
the inspector of gas piping and gas appliances, as well as by the police, health, public welfare, school, library, and
cemetery departments, and by all other departments in which money was collected for the town, were examined and
checked. The payments to the treasurer were verified and the cash on hand in the several departments was proved by
actual count.
The surety bonds of the officials required by law to furnish them for the faithful performance of their dutieswere
examined and found to be in proper form. Appended to this report, in addition to the balance sheet, are tables showing
reconciliations of the several cash accounts, summaries of the tax, excise, assessment, special tax, tax title, and
departmental accounts, together with schedules showing the condition and transactions of the trust and investment funds.
During the progress of the audit, cooperation was extended by the several town officials, for which, onbehalf
of my assistants and for myself, I wish to express my appreciation.
Respectfully submitted,
