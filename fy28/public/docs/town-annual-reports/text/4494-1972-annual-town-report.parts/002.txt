The construction timetable for the school opening was hampered by weather, equipment delays, sub-contractors
not meeting scheduled completion dates, etc. Hence, the "1971 Interim Plan" for "start-up" was finalized on January
3, 1972 when all of the students at the Fitchburg Vocational School Annex (phased out), the Practical Nursing Pro-
gram at "Burbank" and the Rehabilitation Nursing Assistant Program at Rutland Heights Hospital were brought together
under "Monty Tech's" roof.
Since then our students have been installing machines, erecting partitions and fabricating furnishings, thus
completing many structural tasks not included in the general contract. In addition, dozens of jobs have been completed
for the cities and towns in our regional district. These include rebuilding the nurses' dorm at Burbank into a Mental
Health Clinic, building a forester's home at the Boy Scout Camp in Gardner, repairing the bandstand in Ashby, printing
over 150 different forms for various branchesof municipalities, rebuilding trucks, backhoes, tractors for several civic
agencies, manufacturing components for Water Departments, and many other projects. These tasks were performed as
a learning experience in the pursuit of courses of study and, at the same time, saving thousands of dollars for the com-
munities in our region.
On February 27, 1972, an Open House was held to show the public our new school. An estimated 4,000 to
5,000 people came to see our building and to participate in the ceremonies with our legislators, State Department
of Education officials and community leaders. The October 1, 1972 census showed that 889 students were enrolled,
with a waiting list of 277. Of these 889 students, 741 were in grades 9-12 and 148 were post high school students
in the health fields, food trades, machine shop, design, and computer fields. Several were former "drop outs"
occupying available seats since our Junior and Senior classes have not reached their peak enrollment.
Adult Evening Education programs started with an enrollment of 175 in March of 1972 for six courses. In the
September enrollment, 350 registered for 21 courses with a waiting list of 150. The evening adult programs have
developed an additional dimension to the usual job preparation and trade extension programs. Representatives of
local industries have made many requests for short unit courses to up-date specific skills such as pneumatic and fluid
power control techniques, mig, tig and heliarc welding, machine and electronic mensuration and testing methods,
heating and air conditioning controls, oil burner service, oil burner service certification, etc. Since the evening
adult programs can include all of the 25 skilled areas taught during the day, a request for greater facility utilization
will shortly be considered by the School Committee.
To service additional students on the secondary level, "reach-out" and "spin-off" programs are in the process
of being developed with the sending schools in our district. In addition, a summer program is being planned for those
students who are Seniors and wish to develop job entry skills during a six-week program. Several avenues for inter-
relationship are being explored with Mount Wachusett Community College. These will make it possible for upward
progression as well as receiving course credits in some of the post-graduate courses toward an Associate's Degree.
Our student body has been developing a closer identification to "Monty Tech" as evidenced by the many extra-
curricular activities. Twenty-four clubs and organizations have been established varying from 70 students par-
ticipating in a Chess Club, Outing Club, Drama, School Newspaper, Yearbook, Student Government, and a Horse
Show Club. The Horse Riding Club organized a Class C Show exhibiting over 125 horses involving almost every seg-
ment of the school and raised over $500 toward the electric scoreboard in the gym. This year plans are being made
to sponsor a Class A Show.
Our first commencement graduated former students of the Fitchburg Vocational School (who became Seniors at
"Monty Tech") from our one - year programs and the graduating class consisted of 23 undergraduates and 88
graduates from our Dental Assistant, Dental Lab Technician, Medical Assistant, Medical Lab Assistant, Practical
Nurse, Rehabilitation Nursing Assistant, Geriatric Aide, and Nurse Aide Programs. To date, 400 have graduated from
these para-professional programs and almost all are working in their field of training.
As this report is being written, the School Committee is working to adopt the 18-month Operating and Capital
Budget. Every effort will be made to complete budget considerations at an early date. The budget determination could
not commence until instruction and directives were formulated by the various State Agencies concerned with the 18-
month transition regulations.

It is a privilege to submit my Third Annual Report as Superintendent of Lunenburg Public Schools. This is
the 103rd such report preserved in the Town records and covers the year ending December 31, 1972.
LOOKING BACK ON 1972 - THE SCHOOL YEAR IN CAPSULE
Lunenburg Schools experienced a relatively smooth year in 1972. Negotiations with the teachers' associ-
ation and non-teaching groups proceeded without undue conflict and although school costs continued to climb a
School Budget of $2,069,069. passed without opposition from the voters at the Annual Town Meeting in March
Mrs. Marjorie Stillman was returned by the voters to the School Committee after an absence of six years and Mr.
Roger Raboin was also elected to a 3-year term. These members replaced Mrs. Jean Larkin and Mr. Lou Forgues
both of whom completed full 3-year terms and did not choose to run for re-election.
School enrollment continued to remain quite stable with our 2226 students on October 1st representing an
increase of 13 students over 1971 figures. Anticipating a predicted decline in elementary enrollment last Spring,
one teacher was transferred to the Middle School to fill a vacancy thereby resulting in a cutback of one 4th grade
teacher. Surprisingly, however, the decline in enrollment did not materialize and instead we added more students
in our middle grades--the grades where we are currently experiencing an enrollment bulge.
The school year was highlighted last Spring by a well-attended Open Forum concerning smoking in the
High School. Parents and students discussed the pro's and con's of establishing an outside smoking lounge at Lunen-
burg High School. Both parents, the Administration, and the School Committee agreed that smoking should not be
condoned in the High School under any circumstances since it is a health problem and should be discouraged by
school authorities. The end result of the open forum and the collective study of the smoking problem was:
(1) a more realistic discipline policy was established for students found smoking in lavatories
(2) greater parent involvement and responsibility were called for on individual smoking
problems, and
(3) a mass clean-up campaign of the High School lavatories and a general appeal to
the student body were initiated

FIVE YEAR GOALS FOR LUNENBURG SCHOOLS
Since 1970, school administrators in cooperation with their faculties, have been asked to develop yearly
Master plans. These plans simply represent a respective school's goals for the year or stated another way, their
targets for improvement.
These plans are incorporated into an annual
master plan for the school system prepared by the
Superintendent and presented to the School Committee
and the Community for their approval, and hopefully
financial endorsement. Yearly master plans, however,
must point to some long-range direction for Lunen -
burg Schools over a period of years. Parents, students,
and teachers may not totally agree with the stated
long-range goals and may want to change or modify
them; however, at least some planning has been done
(planning which should be scrutinized), some points
of departure and hopeful arrival have been articulated,
and a means for future evaluation has been provided.
With these thoughts in mind, the following list repre-
sents 5-year Goals for Lunenburg Schools accompanied
by the more specific 18-month Master Plan Goals.

SCHOOL COMMITTEE ACTIVITIES
As of this writing, a busy Lunenburg School Committee finds itself involved in contract negotiations with
teachers, custodians, and cafeteria workers. Although a professional negotiator represents the Committee in con-
tract discussions with the teachers' association, all committee members sit as a body in all negotiation sessions. The
Committee does all its own negotiating, however, with the AFL-CIO Cafeteria and Custodial Union representatives.
At this point, the chances for contract settlements with all groups prior to Town Meeting are rather slim.
In addition to the time-consuming business of negotiations, the School Committee has been busy working
on its 18-month (January 1, 1973 - June 30, 1974) School Budget. 18-Month budgets are being developed in
municipalities throughout the State this year in order
to provide for a transfer from a calendar budget year
to a July to June fiscal budget year.
Concerned about possible reduced State re-
imbursements and the effect of the 18-month School
Budget on the tax rate, some budget cut-backs have
been made by the School Committee this year in the
expense and capital outlay accounts. These reduc -
tions, however, are found in less than 10% the total
operating budget since over 90% of the school budget
includes salaries and fixed costs such as transporta -
tion, heat, and utilities.
To conclude, being a School Committee
member in Lunenburg is long, hard and many times
complex work. It is estimated that a School Com-
mittee member spends approximately 320 hours per
year (or 40 work days) on School Committee business
with most of this time being spent in late evening
meetings. Considering the personal and professional
obligations these members have on the outside, Lun-
enburg owes its School Committee adebt of gratitude
for their dedication and sheer perseverance.

MRS. GRACE L. TAYLOR
1957 - 1972
Mrs. Taylor has taught twenty-two years with fifteen
of them being in Lunenburg. Mrs. Taylor graduated from
Fitchburg Teachers' College in 1928 and then took a teach -
ing assignment on Long Island where she taught grades six,
seven, and eight in one room. In 1929, an inspector arrived
to view her 55 children from wall-to-wall. He asked her if
they were having an assembly. She retorted that these child -
ren were hers all day long and that she had trouble telling
where one grade began and another ended. Mrs. Taylor had
had enough of Long Island. The following year--her third
year of teaching--she taught 5th and 6th grade math in a
suburb of Philadelphia where teaching conditions were improved.
Upon moving to Fitchburg, Mass. in 1957, Mrs. Taylor
was urged by a friend late in that summer to go back to teach-
ing. Fortunately for Lunenburg, she was hired as a 6th grade
teacher by Supt. Billings, two days before school opened that
September.
During her 15 years in Lunenburg, Mrs. Taylor moved from the Thomas C. Passios Elementary School to the Jr.
High School and back again. Then, after the Middle School was completed, she moved to new quarters once more.
Mrs. Taylor was a dedicated teacher in every sense of the word. Students and teachers alike developed a
keen sense of respect for her. Whenever a fellow teacher suggested a new and sound way of dealing with social stud-
ies or science, Grace's answer was always, "Yes--what have we got to lose!" She pulled her share of the load
coming in some days with bandaged leg and all. When kidded by the other teachers she always had a quick and
witty retort. Many children when quizzed about Mrs. Taylor will attest to the fact that she enjoyed children, loved
her subject, and inspired a love of knowledge in them.
We all wish her years and years of richly deserved happiness and leisure.

Excerpts from Report of Superintendent of Schools:
"We believe the Town of Lunenburg is fortunate in having such a good teaching staff and urge the citizens to
cooperate with them in their many difficult problems We have been very fortunate during the past year in having
student instructors from the Fitchburg State Teachers' College to handle our shop classes. Excellent work has been
accomplished, real enthusiasm shown by the students, and shop facilities have been improved under the guidance of
these men. In March and April, 1947, the Boston University Cooperative Guidance and Testing Service adminis -
tered two sets of tests to the pupils in the Jr.-Sr. High. The first series, for grades 8 and 9, tested mental ability,
reading comprehension, English and Math fundamentals. The second, for grade 11, was for occupational interest and
aptitude, but included also mental ability and reading comprehension Early in the year alterations were made at
the Brooks House to accoramodate Grade, 6 in that building, Grade 3 was moved to the second floor in the Center
School, and a combined first and second grade class was formed.
