JOSEPH F. BILOTTA
1940 - 2005
This year the Town Report is dedicated to one of the Town's
beloved Selectmen who passed away last year and who is sadly
missed. Joe served the Town of Lunenburg as a member of the
Board of Selectmen for 18 years, serving as Chairman for 14
consecutive years. He served on the Eagle House Building
Committee, the Public Safety Building Committee, the CAFO
Screening Committee and the First and Second Charter
Commissions. He was instrumental in the Inter-municipal Sewer
Agreements between the Town and the Cities of Fitchburg and
Leominster. Joe was known as the "Mayor of Lake Shirley" and
prior to the acceptance of the Charter, on many occasions
fashioned himself as the "Mayor of Lunenburg", a role that he
fulfilled with dedication and loyalty to the Town. Joe was a one of a kind politician, a true Democrat, a person who is
sadly missed by all who knew and worked with him.
"Joe Bilotta was Joe Bilotta and he presented himself as a very unique individual. I have noted many times previously
that to know Joe was to know at least five different people. He was a family man, a caring man, a community man, a
politician and man of purpose and dedication. He was tough but once you learned to work with him and he respected
you, consensus could be brought forth. I am grateful that there was a Joe Bilotta to work with in my years of service in
this community. We had many issues but at the end of the day, no matter how he expressed himself, I knew that I was
working with a person who cared about his community" Marion Benson
LAWRENCE T. PATON
1934- 2005
Larry was' a lifelong resident of Lunenburg, growing up on
Paton's Farm at the corner of Route 2A and Chestnut Street. He
graduated from Lunenburg High School in 1952 and embarked on
an impressive lifetime of service to the Town. His first job was on
the Lunenburg Fire Department at the age of 16. He served on the
Department for 49 years with the last 15 years as the Assistant
Chief. He was a founding member of the Fire Medical Technicians
certified by the National Registry of Emergency Medical Services.
He responded to approximately 15,000 emergency calls over his
career, Impacting families not only in Lunenburg but also in all
the surrounding towns. He retired from the Fire Department in 1999. In the words of retired Fire Chief James Deming,
"it is unlikely that Larry's level of activity on the Lunenburg Fire Department will ever be repeated".
Larry worked as custodian in the Lunenburg School System for 40 years, spanning from 1952 to 1994, with a two-year
break in service while he served in the US Marine Corp where he attained the rank of Corporal. Upon Larry's retirement
from the school system the senior class honored him by being named Grand Marshall of the homecoming parade.
Although Larry's love for the Town of Lunenburg was paramount, it was exceeded only by the love he had for his
family. Among the family's favorite memories that live on today are yearly vacations at Cape Cod, Fourth of July
cookouts and Christmas Eve family gatherings. Larry will be best remembered for his generous, kind and humble
character that never waived throughout his entire life.

BOARD OF SELECTMEN / SEWER COMMISSIONERS
The unexpected passing of Chairman Joseph Bilotta resulted in a reorganization of the Board, and a reassignment of
responsibilities. Robert H. Bowen was elected Chairman, and Steven M. deBettencourt continued as Vice-chair for the remainder of
his term. The members of the Board were variously assigned positions on other Town committees. Initially, David Rodgers and
Robert Bowen served on the Charter Review Committee and continued to advise the Finance Department in its preparation of the
budget until the Annual Town Meeting. Following the Annual Election in May, Thomas A. Alonzo and Chairman Bowen were elected
by the Board to serve on the Charter Review Committee. This committee has been reviewing the current Home Rule Charter and it
is anticipated that recommendations will be submitted to the town for the next Annual Town Meeting. Daniel W. Cronin was
nominated Vice-Chairman, Steve deBettencourt was nominated Clerk for the Board and both members continue to serve in their
capacity on the Library Building Committee and Capital Planning Committee.
The search for the CAFO continued with Steven M. deBettencourt and Daniel W. Cronin serving as Board of Selectmen
representatives on the CAFO Screening Committee. With the assistance of Bennett Yarger Associates of Scituate, MA, hired as
consultant, the Committee submitted recommendations to the Board of Selectmen and accordingly, Daniel M. Keyes was hired as
Chief Administrative Financial Officer on June 20, 2005.
The Phase 1, Contract II, including Electric Avenue, West, Elm and Massachusetts Avenue Sewer projects continued throughout the
year and are nearing completion with final paving on Massachusetts Avenue anticipated in the Spring of 2006. Upon completion of
the final paving and repairs to personal properties the board will be assessing the final actual betterments to all property owners
along the sewered routes. With the near completion of the sewer project, the town Is realizing a growth in the number of
development applications and residential construction. As the Town is mandated by the State to maintain a percentage of
affordable housing units, the Board has been working together with the Planning Board on attaining a 40R Smart Growth District
within its borders in order to direct this type of development to more desirable areas. Another option at managing growth due to
the installation of sewers would be to adopt a Sewer Overlay District Bylaw which the board will also be working with Chief
Administrative Financial Officer and the Planning Board on.
This year has been a year of excitement and new beginnings with the openings of the new Primary School, formally dedicated in
October 2005. Member Thomas A. Alonzo replaced Chairman Bilotta in his capacity as Board of Selectmen representative on the
School Building Committee. Within three months time the Lunenburg Public Lunenburg Public Library opened their doors on
December 5, 2005 and member Daniel W. Cronin served as Chairman to the Lunenburg Public Library Building Committee. As a
result of these openings the town is now faced with the prospect of what to do with the vacant buildings and a number of options
have been proposed by a number of study committees that have reported back to the Selectmen. Various proposals have been
explored, from raising the old fire/police station, refurbishing the former Ritter Memorial Library into administrative office space,
selling the building and/or land on the former police chief annex, to selling the closed primary school. At this point in time the one
agreed upon course of action is to refurbish the library building into town office space. A decision on what to do with the
remaining buildings is imminent, as it is cost prohibitive particularly for heating fuel and insurance purposes to maintain these
vacant buildings.
The board extends a special "thank you" again this year to the Lunenburg / Townsend Rotary Club for decorating the Gazebo at
Christmas and the Turkey Hill Gardeners for the seasonal Town Hall decorations and flowers in the upper common. We would also
like to acknowledge the generous donations that were received and utilized so that the Town could continue with the traditional
Band Concerts on the Common and any donations to this cause would be greatly appreciated.
Your Board has enjoyed working in service to the Town, working with each other and with you the citizens. The Selectmen would
also like to acknowledge the individuals who work so hard for the Town and those who volunteer their time and commitment by
serving on the various Boards, Commissions and Committees within the Town.
Please feel free to direct any calls to our office staff, Daniel M. Keyes, Chief Administrative Financial Officer, Laura Williams, Chief
of Staff / Chief Administrative Assistant or Linda Douglas, Administrative Assistant @ 978-582-4144. If they cannot assist you, they
will forward your call to the appropriate authorities. Our office hours are Monday, Wednesday and Thursday 8:00 AM to 4:00 PM,
Tuesday 8:00 AM to 6:30 PM and Friday 8:00 AM to 12:30 PM.

CAPITAL PLANNING
The Capital Planning Committee, following the Charter direction, continues to present FY Plans and Capital Program Plans. To
clarify, the FY Capital Plan determines the needs of the governmental departments for the coming year. The Capital Program Plan
depicts the long-range needs of the Town, such as new buildings or major building renovations. The cost of the Capital Program
Items has a higher cost value. All Capital Expenditures have the same Criteria for prioritizing: Mandates, Safety, Need, Crisis, Cost
Effectiveness and Ability to Fund.
At 2005 Annual Town Meeting, for FY 2006 Capital Plan, the Town allocated capital spending for $540,443.00 with an additional
Capital Program spending of $300,000.00, through borrowing, for the Fire Department's ladder truck. This truck was purchased
second hand, in excellent condition, thus fulfilling the Criteria of Safety, Need and Cost Effectiveness.
The FY 2006 Plan included two 6-wheel dump trucks w/plow and a 1T dump truck w/plow for the Public Works Dept. at $225,000.00.
The Fire Dept. was listed at $35,000.00 for hose and appliance, fire fighting gear and replacement of ice/water rescue boat. Also
included for the Fire Dept. was the 4th payment for an ambulance at $28,400.00. The Police Dept. was listed at a marked cruiser at
$34,649.00 and radar unit at $2,500.00. In the School Dept. a floor scrubber, replacement of flooring, blackboards, furniture as well
as parking lot repairs and snow guard and gutters totaled $140,000.00. The Technology Dept. annual Town replacement of
technical equipment and infrastructure software upgrade totaled $66,632.20. Also on the Plan were lease payments for Police Dept.
and Board of Selectmen copiers for $8,261.36.
The past years of the Capital Program Plans for building construction sees all three major buildings, the Primary School, Public
Safety Facility and the Public Library in operation. Basic studies have been done on a re-use plan for the vacant buildings left by
the new construction. The Capital Planning Committee hopes that the next year will see final plans created to carry out the basic
plan of need by re-designing and renovating the vacant center buildings for re-use such as governmental offices. Further, Capital
Program planning is being anticipated on Town-wide owned buildings and parcels.
This past year brought a new Chief Administrator and Financial Officer to our Town, Mr. Daniel Keyes. The Committee will be
working with Mr. Keyes in continuing to define policies and procedures to forward and to streamline Capital Planning efforts. As
we progress, we need to keep in mind the purpose of reasonable and continuous capital spending. This statement has been
expressed many times and is still important. This Committee, for years, has referred to the "big man-little man" analogy, which
simply stated, means we cannot lose sight that Town spending must include a continuous balance of capital spending. The
expenditures through FY Plans are the tools to provide services for safety and protection, the management of government and the
means to keep facilities and grounds in good condition. This is cost effective because, understandably, the alternative becomes a
crisis that is more costly in the end. Capital Program Expenditures keep the community current to the needs of the citizens.
Each year it is more difficult to fulfill the Criteria of Ability to Fund. The Committee's 2005 efforts for the FY2007 Plan were
prioritized at $630,086.36. To date, the available funds from revenue are $370,761.00. The Capital Planning Committee takes this
into serious consideration; however, cutting FY Plans to the bare bones is not cost effective. Also, there are times in Capital
Program Expenditures that the burden to the taxpayer is outweighed by the need of the Town. The community should stay alert to
the need.
This past year has brought some changes to the Committee. Ernest Hunter, Member-at-Large has resigned. Ernie has given many
years of service on different Boards and Committees and we thank him. Elaine Murphy, a former representative from the Finance
Committee has been appointed to replace Ernie Hunter as Member-at-Large. David Matthews has been assigned to the Committee
as the representative from the Finance Committee. The Committee will continue to work with all Boards, Commissions and
Committees with the planning of Capital Expenditures that fit the Criteria.

CENTRAL MASS MOSQUITO CONTROL
The Central Massachusetts Mosquito Control Project (the project) currently provides its services to 36 cities and towns
throughout Middlesex and Worcester Counties. The Project's headquarters is located at 111 Otis Street, Northboro MA. Tours of
the headquarters or visits to field work sites may be arranged by calling the office in advance. Please call 508-393-3055 during
business hours for more information. The Project practices Integrated Mosquito Management (IMM), blending state of the art
methods and techniques with expertise, experience and scientific research to provide our member communities with
environmentally sound and cost effective mosquito control.
During 2004 the Project received seven thousand and eighty-five (7,085)) requests for service from town residents and officials.
A total of over six thousand (6,000) pounds of Btl (Bacillus thuringlensis israelensis) was applied by helicopter in two towns,
Chelmsford and Billerica, and seven thousand two hundred and ninety-nine (7,299) pounds by hand throughout our service area
were applied to area wetlands to reduce the emergence of adult mosquitoes. This represents over two thousand and sixty
(2,060) acres of wetland that was treated with this mosquito-specific bacterium, significantly reducing adult mosquito
populations in these areas. Thirty-two thousand four hundred and forty- four (32,444)) catch basins were treated with larvicidal
product to control the mosquitoes that seek out these cool dark wet areas to breed, including the Culex mosquito, a major target
for West Nile virus transmission. Seven thousand seven hundred thirty-seven (7,737) culverts were cleaned in an attempt to
eliminate unnecessary standing water and reduce mosquito breeding. This work was done in conjunction with cleaning, clearing
and digging of one hundred sixty-eight thousand three hundred fifty-two (168,352) feet of streams, brook and ditches. This
represents almost thirty-two (32) miles of waterways which were cleaned and improved by project personnel in 2005.
The Mosquito Awareness Program which we offer to elementary schools and other civic organizations in our district has become
very popular. Project staff meets with students, teachers or concerned residents to discuss mosquito biology, mosquito habitat,
and control procedures. Much of the presentation is directed toward what children and their families can do to prevent
mosquitoes from breeding around their homes. Slides, videos, coloring books and other handouts make this an interesting
program. This program is tailored to meet the needs of the specific audience. One thousand six hundred and nineteen (1,619)
students attended these programs.
As part of our effort to reduce the need for pesticides we continue to expand our wetlands restoration program. By cleaning
clogged and overgrown waterways, mosquito breeding can be reduced and drainage areas are restored to historic conditions.
Bti mosquito larvicide is used to treat areas where mosquito larvae are found. We routinely check known breeding sites kept in
our data base, but also encourage the public to notify us of any areas they suspect could breed mosquitoes. Our field crews will
investigate all such requests and treat the area only if surveillance gathered at the time shows an imminent threat of mosquito
emergence. Our goal is to manage all mosquito problems with education, wetlands restoration or larviciding, but we recognize
that there are times when adult mosquito spraying is the only viable solution. In such cases specific areas are treated with either
hand-held or pickup truck mounted sprayers if surveillance gathered at the time exceeds a pre-determined threshold to warrant
an application. This program is offered on a request-only basis, and the exclusion process allows residents and/or town officials
to exclude areas under their control from this or any part of our program. The Project's surveillance program monitors adult
mosquito and larval populations density, and is the backbone for prescribing various control techniques. Specialized mosquito
traps are deployed throughout the Project's service area to sample for mosquitoes that may be transmitting mosquito-borne
diseases. In conjunction with the Mass. Dept. of Public Health we sample in areas suspected of harboring WNV and other
viruses. One thousand one hundred fifty-three (1,153) pools (collections) of mosquitoes totaling eleven thousand nine hundred
twenty-eight (11,928) specimens were tested for mosquito-borne viruses this year, and two (2) pools of Cs. Melanura, in
Westborough were confirmed to be infected with the EEE virus. Six (6) pools of West Nile Virus were confirmed, four (4) in
Westborough (3 Culex and 1 Cs melanura) and one each in Holliston and Wilminton, both Culex species. MDPH identified EEE in
Holliston but subsequent surveillance did not confirm any additional virus isolates. No human or horse cases were identified
with WNV or EEE in 2005 in our service area.
Educational pamphlets are available to anyone interested in learning about mosquito control and services provided by the
Project, and these items are routinely stocked in member Town/City Hall and Libraries. Display boards with information on our
program are rotated through area Town Halls throughout the year. We also have a website, www.cmmcp.org that has extensive
Information on mosquito biology, our control procedures, etc. This website has become a model for other Mosquito Projects
and has been widely used throughout our service area and beyond.
We would like to thank you for your support during 2005 and we look forward to helping you and your community with its
mosquito problems in 2006 and beyond.

BOARD OF HEALTH
George Emond, Chairman
Increased activity for new construction caused partially by the recently installed and expanded sewer system in the Town has
caused a significant increase in demands on our office which is required to review all construction projects, even those which
are being designed to connect to sewer systems. Our Special Town Meeting last November authorized a Comprehensive
Wastewater Management study to determine the geological makeup of the town's soil, our groundwater supply and other factors.
Municipal sewer systems simplify the process of constructing both Individual homes and multi-unit development projects, but in
many situations are not the only solution to wastewater management. Sewers can have a deleterious effect upon water supply
since it effectively removes water from the supply chain and sends it out of town. As demand exceeds the ability of the aquifer to
recharge itself, supply diminishes, and lack of water can become a problem. We urge all citizens to carefully examine any
proposals that come before you relating to sewage disposal and select the one that bests suits the situation, protects the
environment and our quality of life.

approved under 40B submittals during a calendar year, the Town can delay any new 40B submittals for one year. If 54 units are

The Planning Board has been working on achieving a 40R status for a Smart Growth District. The Board's interest in securing
this status for the Town is because 40R development is more advantageous in mandated development. It allows the Town to
become the makers of its own destiny. The Town can place development in strategic areas, have input in the design, and gain
infrastructure. Most importantly, the Town can move forward in meeting mandated count of affordable component levied by the
State. A 40R development, If rental, can be counted one for one of the State's affordable mandate. Therefore, the Town will be
better able to manage growth within the 40R framework. There is also financial assistance according to a scale of density.
Further, the State passed Section 40S of the MGL which is assistance in the education of students within a 40R project. Working

CHARTER REVIEW COMMITTEE REPORT
Lunenburg's Town Charter requires that the Charter undergo periodic reviews, to be performed by a Charter Review Committee,
at least every ten years. In the summer of 2004, the Board of Selectmen authorized such a committee to be convened. Our first
meeting was held on September 7, 2004 and the committee was comprised of nine members from various town boards and
committees as well as two citizens-at-large, as detailed in the Charter.
Our charge was to methodically review the entire Charter, discuss each article section by section and, when appropriately
agreed and voted upon, propose amendments that would clarify, improve or simply add to, the existing foundation document of
our town government. Over the next nineteen months, in more than thirty-three open meetings and two public hearings, the
committee has met to confront that challenge. Every aspect of every facet of the document was discussed and debated,
sometimes at great length over several meetings. No topic was off-limits for discussion. Throughout the process, input and
comment was sought and encouraged from the CAFO, town boards, committees, department heads and employees, as well as
the public.
We are now completing our work and are prepared to submit to this year's Town Meeting our recommendations for approval,
both at Town Meeting and at the ballot box afterward. The complete document detailing the proposed revisions Is available to
the public at Town Hall and at the Lunenburg Public Library. It is our hope to have it posted on the town's website shortly.
Please be advised that as an appointed body, there are limits as to what types of changes or additions we could entertain and
ultimately propose. Our review committee should not be confused with an elected Charter Commission, which has broader
powers. Fortunately, none of the amendments proposed by this committee are outside the bounds of our recommendation
authority or Town Meetings adoption authority.
We are recommending many changes. In fact, there are more than can be listed in this summary report. However, the majority
are simply "housekeeping" changes such as spelling and grammatical corrections, stylistic changes and reordering of
paragraphs. In our opinion and intent, they do not substantively change any position, process or power already given by the
existing Charter.
Of the more substantial changes, we will highlight what we believe to be the most important:
The CAFO will be renamed to Town Manager. The current title is thought to be confusing and ill-understood. We think
the new title is more universally known and would facilitate the search for candidates whenever the position may
become vacant.
Town Manager will no longer be required to be, or to become, a resident of Lunenburg.
The restriction on applicants to the Town Manager position who have held appointed or elective town office has been
eased.
A Sewer Commission will be created. This five-seat body will be comprised of both elected and appointed members.
This commission will be taking over the sewer responsibilities currently being held by the Board of Selectman.
There will be a mandated Director of Municipal Finance who will report directly to the Town Manager. Currently, the
CAFO holds that title. While the CAFO/Town Manager will not lose any power or authority in that arena, it was thought
best to designate someone in charge of that department for the sake of continuity and smooth transition during
changes in the Town Manager occupancy. This position may be filled with someone already employed by the Town and
needn't be an entirely new person or position.
Three of the members of the Finance Committee will be elected.
All members of the Board of Health will be elected. (currently one is appointed)
We encourage everyone to obtain a copy of the proposed changes and read them thoroughly in preparation of the Annual Town
Meeting. We anticipate there will be much discussion on these proposals during the meeting.

SPECIAL SERVICES
During the 2004-2005 school year, the Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District provided special education
support services to over three hundred students. In September 2005, the Support Services Department is working with
approximately 230 students.
The revised law, Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA-04), took effect July 1, 2005. It provides for more services to
students transitioning from Monty Tech to careers, college, or further training. Since the intent of this law is to educate as many
students as possible in the general education classrooms, the staff at Monty Tech has embarked on a major shift in how we
teach and support students with disabilities. The major focus of special education services will be inclusive education using
universal design to ensure that every student with a disability is education in the "least restrictive environment."
The student support services at Monty Tech are available to all students. We have a full-time school nurse with a part-time
assistant who administers medications, performs state mandated health screening tests, and provides, when necessary, health
information to the student's IEP team meeting. We have a full-time school social worker who is able to participate on teams and
assist students who have needs concerning finances, family issues, homelessness, maternity, health issues, and proper food
and clothing. Our students have access to the services of a full-time adjustment counselor and a part-time school psychologist.
Both of these people are available for scheduled counseling sessions and mental health emergency treatment or crisis
intervention. We have a part-time speech pathologist who gives therapy to students with disabilities and consults with all
teachers in the building.
The Special Education Program at Monty Tech is reviewed every May in accordance with regulatory requirements. The results of
this evaluation are used to improve the special education procedures and programs at Monty Tech. The Parent Advisory Council
participates in this review and evaluation.
The Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District adheres to federal and state law regarding confidentiality of
personally identifiable student information.

TECHNOLOGY
Monty Tech continues to provide technology resources, which exceed both state averages and benchmarks. However, as noted
in the last report, economic conditions have resulted in a decrease in our ability to keep all workstations at the desired level.
As networking standards continue to evolve, we also face the challenge of upgrading the infrastructure of the network. Many
areas of the building are still operating on wiring which is not rated to handle the traffic capacities of the equipment connected to
it. All existing fiber optic backbones have been upgraded to gigabit Ethernet, and we are attempting to install gigabit fiber to all
individual and instructional areas that contain 12 or more computers. There remains, however, a large number of category 3
copper data wire connections that must be upgraded to category 5.
Although wireless laptops remain popular, the administration has decided to limit expansion in this area, dropping the priority
given in previous years to laptops.
Overall, the most significant challenges center around support and maintenance of the network. Our use of the internet
averages 98.8% of the bandwidth available on our two T1 connections. Monitoring this traffic as well as responding to virus
attacks, spam, and content filtering issues, as well as switch lockups, requires a considerable time commitment on a daily basis.
As reported for the last several years, the Information Technology Help Desk provides great system support for troubleshooting,
repair and maintenance for the vast majority of the workstations at Monty Tech. They responded to over 2,000 trouble requests
last year. Comcast recently installed a fiber connection to the public access studios in Fitchburg, which provides the capability
to originate high quality TV programming from our TV studio and adjacent auditorium.

TURKEY HILL MIDDLE SCHOOL
Principal: Keith Hochstein
2005-2006 was a memorable, distinguished and celebratory year for Turkey Hill Middle School. Each year the Massachusetts
Department of Education selects a very small number of schools across the state that have exceeded expectations for MCAS
tests for several years in a row. Each of these schools receives the designation as a Compass School. It is called the Compass
School Award because the winning schools use strategies that are innovative, successful and can serve as guides for other
schools hence, "Compass Schools". This year only eight schools from all the public schools in the state were chosen to win
this prestigious award, and there was only one middle school chosen. That school was our Turkey HIII Middle Schooll
This award serves as a barometer of several characteristics. It reflects the excellence of our entire staff, the dedication of our
sensational students and the deep support that the Town of Lunenburg and all our parents have for public education. It is an
award that is also shared by the entire public school system especially TC Passios School. MCAS are cumulative tests so
success at one grade is the result of the work of all the preceding grades.
There are many ways to judge MCAS results, some of which can become tedious. There is one way, however, that clearly
demonstrates why THMS won the Compass Award and that also shows the academic excellence of our school. The Department
of Education compiles the percentage of all students who receive an "Advanced" or "Proficient" score on all the various tests
given. They also provide data that compares your school to all of the other schools in the state in terms of percentage of
students receiving "Advanced" or "Proficient". For instance, there were 580 schools that gave the 6th grade math test. Out of
this number, THMS finished the 45th highest in terms of the percentage of students in "Advanced" or "Proficient" or the 92nd
percentile. in 7th grade, ELA THMS finished 79th out of 458 schools that have 7th grade or 83rd percentile. In 8th grade math we
finished 63rd out of 456 schools or the 86th percentile and in 8th grade science we finished 42nd out of 456 schools or the 91st
percentile. These are outstanding results and results of which the entire Town of Lunenburg can be proud. In addition, THMS
has been attaining results similar to these for several years, and this is why we won the Compass School Award.
The DOE also asks potential winners to describe in detail why these above expectation scores were obtained, and they visit
schools to speak to students, parents and teachers. When the DOE announced that we had won this award, they, in part,
attributed our selection to students who were extremely proud of their school, parents who were complimentary and enthusiastic
about THMS and teachers who successfully practiced the strategies that were described in our application. We attributed our
success to the Results model of teaching, our Understanding by Design approach, and our Circle of Success beliefs. Underlying
all of these is an attitude of collegiality and caring shared by the entire staff. Teachers work together to share best practices,
and they care about the children in our school.
Last September a number of schools officials went to a wonderful reception at the State House where Governor Romney and
Commissioner Driscoll officially proclaimed Turkey Hill Middle School as one of the eight schools this year to win the Compass
School Awardl
Turkey Hill Middle School has always believed that we need to also meet the affective side of children's needs. To that end, we
again set and achieved goals that would meet the social/personal side of our students. We took a major step with our athletic
program by expanding from cheerleading, field hockey and football to also include girls and boys basketball. This year we had
our basketball team play a fourteen game schedule and enjoy all of the benefits of playing sports and representing their school.
All five of these programs are paid for by our own fundraising. Through fees and fundraising, we have five interscholastic sports
programs that enable students to develop skills like teamwork, dedication and just plain having fun.
In addition, THMS continued to develop the concept of Student Led Conferences that strives to increase the levels of
responsibility and student awareness around their own learning. THMS further refined our new Homework Policy by surveying
all our parents to determine what is and what is not working in order to both decrease the stress of homework on parents and
students and increase the academic benefits of doing schoolwork at home. THMS held a series of Parent Forums this year to
increase the levels of communication between school and home. The hope was to allow time for parents to address any
concerns and to ask any questions they might have. These proved worthwhile and will be continued.
Our Mission calls for the schools to develop life-long learners. We believe that in order to do this our school also must be
constantly improving, constantly learning and constantly enhancing the school environment to provide a better education for all
our students. We believe we are doing this, and we hope the community agrees.

Cynthia Daukantas, Principal
July 1 and the move into the new Primary School was both exciting and exhausting. Preparing the building for four hundred
little faces in September proved a daunting task. Hundreds upon hundreds of boxes, equipment, and supplies needed to be
unpacked and stored. Many parents visited the school during the closing weeks of the summer to prepare their child for a
successful entrance to the new school year.
The Lunenburg Primary School continues to strive toward educating the total child. We utilize a variety of instructional methods
and modalities in our approach to meet the individual needs of every child. We believe that the success of our programs is
dependent upon a healthy and productive partnership between home and school. Toward that end, parents must play an active
role in many of the daily programs and routines of their child's educational program.
With an enrollment of four hundred two students, class sizes range from eighteen to twenty-two. Because research indicates
that class sizes of twenty and under in the early years provide lasting educational benefits to young children, we strive to keep
our kindergarten through grade 2 classes smaller.

to provide the funding for kindergarten and first grade children to receive instruction for the month of July. Mrs. Lisa Stone,

efforts, we will be able to offer support to both kindergarten and grade one children this summer. Through the support of
parents and to meet the needs of parents, two full-day kindergarten programs were created this fall. We are excited about
offering a third full-day program to kindergarteners, and look forward to when we can offer this program to all five year olds.

SPECIAL SERVICES DEPARTMENT - SPED
Elaine E. Blaisdell, Student Services Coordinator
Our mission of continuous improvement for ALL students is our primary focus in the district. The staff in the Lunenburg Public
Schools takes pride in their ability to provide equal access to the curriculum for all students. Administrators, regular and special
educators, and parents collaboratively work to develop Individualized Educational Programs for students. The plans developed
are rigorous and provide the necessary specialized instruction for students to be successful in the least restrictive environment.
Currently there are two hundred and sixty-four (264) students with disabilities receiving some form of special education or
related service in Lunenburg on an individual educational plan. This represents 14% of our total student population. This
percentage is consistent with the national average and less than the state average of 15.9%. Of the total number of students
receiving services, eight students received services outside the district during the 2005/2006 school year. Of the eight, students,
seven are in day placements and one is in a residential placement. The breakdown of disability categories is as follows; Autism
or PDD - 3.78%; Communication Disorder и 14.39%; Developmental Delays 9.09%; Emotional Disabilities 2.27%; Health
Disabilities - 6.43%; Intellectual Disabilities 6.06%; Multiple Disabilities 1.13%; Neurological Disabilities- 1.13%; Physical
Impairments - 1.13%; Sensory/Vision Impairment/Blind <1% and Specific Learning Disabilities - 25.37%. In Lunenburg, fifty-five
(55) students identified with a disability receive accommodations via a 504 plan. Most of these students have health disabilities
that require some accommodations in the classroom.
Special Needs students reached the expected progress level to achieve adequate yearly progress this year. The staff worked
hard last year to meet this goal and continues to focus efforts this year on our students using MCAS and district assessments.
We continue to identify areas of deficit and provide the necessary instruction to remediate the area of need. We accomplish this
through our schools' results teams, at Individual Educational Team meetings, and through the teachers' efforts in the classroom.
At individual team meetings parents and teachers engage in active discussions about the best method to address Individual
deficit areas. In some cases the student receives a double Language Arts or Math class that provides the student with needed
additional instructional time in an area of identified weakness.
For many years regular and special educators have worked together to meet the needs of small groups of students in the
classroom. This year these Instructional teams have been formalized and expanded upon. Regular educators and special
educators are working together as a team to meet the needs of all students in the classroom. The district has committed
resources and time to developing effective Instructional teaming efforts across the district. The Instructional teams established
across the district are discovering the benefits of the co-teaching concept. At the primary school teams are working together at
the 2ⁿᵈ grade level providing flexible grouping to strengthen reading. At the fourth grade level Instructional teams are working
together to differentiate the reading program. Students are provided with novel choices and the regular education staff has
teamed up with special educators and Title One staff to provide novels on varying ability levels. At THMS the 7th grade Red Team
has teamed up in the areas of math and is providing an individualized vocabulary program for students who need it. At the high
school two special needs teachers have teamed up to provide appropriate instruction for students with significant disabilities.
These are just a few of the teams that are working together to meet the needs of all of our students in Lunenburg, including
those with and without disabilities.
The Parent Advisory Council of Special Achievers in Lunenburg (PACSAL) has been an active support group for parents.
Meetings have been scheduled for the last Tuesday of each month, at 7:00PM, in the Lunenburg High School Teacher Resource
Center. With the completion of the new library, the group will be moving into a meeting area there. The executive board is made
up of Co-Chair: Kelly Harvey, Co-Chair: Deborah Carter, Treasurer: Kelly Moriera, and Secretary: Sandra Osborne. At the
PACSAL meetings they have established a short business meeting and provided speakers on various special education topics
for parents in the district. Presentation topics at meetings this year have covered Learning Disabilities, Autism, Responsive
Classroom, Best Buddies, Parent Rights and Communication Disorders to name a few. The PACSAL has provided information
on disabilities and their organization participates in various town functions such as the Town Fair and the Multicultural Festival.
In summary, The Lunenburg Public Schools takes great pride in its ability to provide a variety of special needs services to meet
the varying needs of our students. We have an experienced hard working staff that works together with parents to provide these
necessary services. Our parents continue to be equal partners in our efforts to develop challenging and focused educational
programs for their children. We are committed to the Lunenburg Public Schools mission to prepare our students for lifelong
learning and responsible community membership. This is a total community effort and we encourage ALL Lunenburg residents
to join with us to create positive experiences and futures for our children.

Karen Martin, Director
During this past year, the Instructional Services Office continued to focus on three areas critical to the District's mission: (a)
curriculum development, (b) assessment, and (c) continuous improvement for ALL. Following is a summary of activities and
results in each of these areas.
Curriculum Development: The School Committee formally adopted the History/Social Studies curriculum in September.
Curriculum adoption monies purchased needed materials and resources for social studies and English/Language Arts. In
January, the Instructional Technology Taskforce began the process of developing a curriculum to address instructional and

Social, Emotional Learning Taskforce began the process of developing a curriculum to address the social and emotional skills
needed to be a lifelong learner and for responsible community membership. Our district continues to annually review and refine
curriculum to improve student performance.

of 10th grade students met the required benchmark for graduation in English/Language Arts. Overall, 91% (128) of 10th grade
students passed both required tests.

CONTINOUS IMPROVEMENT FOR ALL: The No Child Left Behind legislation provides an opportunity to carefully examine
performance results for all students. Every district in the nation received Annual Yearly Progress results for this mid-cycle. The
Lunenburg Public Schools met AYP goals in the aggregate (all students) and for all subgroups. This data helps guide the school
councils, administrative team, and Results teams to plan for appropriate interventions, programming, and professional
development.
School structures and collaboration focused on student learning ensure all students improve. The Lunenburg Public Schools
has Results Teams, Understanding-by Design training, a comprehensive professional development program, and new teacher
induction program to support student learning.
Results Teams meet one time per month during faculty meeting time. The teams use data to set goals, plan teaching strategies,
and assess the results. This process ensures a regular review of student performance and effective teaching strategies.
This year grade level teams are developing common UBD assessments for English/Language Arts. This process focuses on
developing assessments to improve student performance. Through ongoing analysis of student results, refinement of units, and
increased awareness of effective assessments and instructional practices, student performance will improve. The UBD units
provide additional measures in non- MCAS subjects and provide additional curriculum materials for new teachers.
Ongoing and sustained professional development continues in a range of areas. This year graduate level courses were offered
in Understanding by Design, Curriculum Implementation, Five Essential Elements of Reading plus 6-Traits Writing, Adolescent
Literacy - Continued, Instructional Teaming, and courses for new and veteran teachers on classroom management and skillful
teaching.
This year Title I services include a part-time math specialist and an extended-day program. Our Title I tutors provided additional
classroom support for our Title I students in ELA and math, and the extended day program provides additional time on learning
for Title I students in math.
Our new teacher Induction program provides ongoing support for new teachers to the district. New teachers participate in a two-
day orientation, have a trained mentor, and receive peer coaching during a two-year process. New teachers participate in
professional development focused on skills and strategies needed for beginning teachers.
SUMMARY: The Instructional Services Office has a strong partnership with the individual schools, the administrative team,
faculty, and community. The combined and integrated efforts of all parties ensure continued focus on improving student
performance and strive to provide teachers, administrators, students, and the community with the necessary data, educational
research, professional development, support, and resources needed to improve student performance.

DIRECTOR OF FACILITIES AND GROUNDS - SCHOOLS
John Londa, Director
The opening of the completed Lunenburg Primary School led to the reorganization of the school district over the
summer months of 2005. The new building was occupied in June 2005. We continue to work closely with the
General Contractor and his subcontractors on punch-list items and uncovering break-in issues with the building.
The old Primary School was emptied and returned to Town control.
The focus of the school maintenance effort for FY06 continues to be improvements to the School Department's
compliance with the American Disabilities Act (ADA). At the Passios Elementary School, door hardware changes
to convert from doorknobs to door levers were completed. Conversion of hardware at Turkey Hill Middle School
is expected to be completed by the end of the fiscal year. Installation of wheelchair ramps on the sidewalks at the
Elementary School and Middle School also remains to be completed.
The town approved $120,000 in funding at the Fall Special Town meeting for development of a long-range
facilities plan that studies the impact of town growth and conditions of school facilities. The study will focus on
the feasibility of renovations to Lunenburg High School but will look at the entire main campus facilities.
Architectural firms will be solicited in the April timeframe to begin to study this issue in June 2006 through March
2007.
Utility costs for electricity and natural gas rose to unexpectedly high levels reflecting the impacts of Hurricane
Katrina on the Gulf Coast of the US. Conservation measures implemented included reminder signs to shut off
lights that are not needed, installation on timers on most outside lighting, and eliminating lighting on vending
machines. The school department and selected town facilities are looking to enter into contracts with third party
suppliers for electricity and natural gas.
Capital improvements for FY06 included $12,500 in replacement whiteboards for Lunenburg High School and
three classroom sets of furniture at the schools on the central campus. Installation of snow guards and gutter
repairs for the high school gym and auditorium remain to be completed this fiscal year.
The Lunenburg Track and Field Athletic Association continued work on a soccer and track facility between the
High School and the Middle School. The installation of Astroturf-like playing surface for the field was completed
and the facility opened to the public.

rules noted by the Moderator. At 9:20 A.M. the Annual Town Meeting was adjourned to take action on the Special Town Meeting.

4.13. Smart Growth Overlay District
4.13.1. Purpose
4.13.1.1. The purpose of the Smart Growth Overlay District to advance the objectives of Chapter 40R for affordable housing as
follows:
a) Emphasize mixing land uses;
b) Increase the availability of affordable housing by creating a range of housing opportunities in neighborhoods;
c) Take advantage of compact design;
d) Foster distinctive and attractive communities;
e) Preserve open space, farmland, natural beauty and critical environmental areas;
f) Strengthen existing communities;
g) Provide a variety of transportation choices;
h) Make development decisions predictable, fair and cost effective and
i) Encourage community and stakeholder collaboration in development decisions
4.13.2. Location
4.13.2.1. The Smart Growth Overlay District includes the land formerly used as Drive-in Theaters on Electric Avenue and
Summer Street shown on Assessor's map Number 82, Parcel Number 20 and on Assessor's map Number 81, Parcel 907, and
land formally used with structure of Primary School shown on Assessor's map Number 63, Parcel 142.
4.13.3. Permitted Uses
In a Smart Growth Overlay District all uses permitted in the underlying districts and as provided in the underlying permitted
districts shall be permitted subject to the restrictions and provisions of the underlying districts.
4.13.4. Use permissible subject to Development Plan Review
a) Single family, two family houses, town houses and garden apartments
b) Recreation facilities for the use of the residents for the Smart Growth Overlay District
4.13.5. Conditions of Use
4.13.5.1. A development in the Smart Growth Overlay District is subject to the following;
a) Twenty (20) percent of the units shall be designated affordable for a period of thirty (30) years and shall be defined in
Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD) Regulations under M.G.L., Ch. 40R.
b) The provisions of Section 8.4. Development Plan Review.
4.13.5.2. Density and Dimensional Requirements
a) Irrespective of the requirements of the underlying District, eight (8) dwelling units per acre for single family units andtwelve
(12) units per acre for two family units are permitted.
b) No building or structures other than fences shall be erected closer than forty (40) feet from a street line or lot line.
c) Irrespective of all other dimensional requirements there shall be twenty-five (25) feet between all structures.
4.13.5.3. Mix of Dwelling Units
If there is more than one type of dwelling unit, no one type shall be less that 10% of the total units.
4.13.5.4. Phased Growth
The provisions of Section 4.11. Phased Growth shall not apply to the Smart Growth Overlay Districts; or take any other action
relative thereto. ARTICLE 44 was PASSED OVER
