In Memoriam 
 
Marion M. Benson, 83  
Marion passed away on December 27, 2015 in Leominster. She was very active in the Town of 
Lunenburg where she lived for 40 plus years. She was an active volunteer in both church & civic  
groups, including the School Committee. After her return to Lunenburg in 1981, she began  
volunteering on the Planning Board, where she quickly became Chairperson. She volunteered for  
two decades on the Board at which time the position evolved into full-time Planning Director; a  
position she held until 2014. In May 2015, her many years of service were recognized at Annual  
Town Meeting by a Ritter Building conference room being named in her honor.  
 
 
August “Gus” S. Misner, 78 
Gus passed away on July 15, 2015 in Togus, Maine following a long battle with cancer. 
He was born in Fitchburg on December 17, 1936 where he attended school and played 
football. He served in the U.S. Army from 1955-1964, was stationed in the Arctic and was 
an aide to President Eisenhower. He later became a police officer in Lunenburg for several 
years until ill health forced his retirement.  
 
 
               Senator Mary Padula, 89 
       Mary Louise Padula, nee Harley, passed away on October 29, 2015. Born in Fitchburg and 
       nearly a lifetime resident of Lunenburg, Mary served as Town Clerk, Tax Collector and  
       Treasurer for 30 years. Upon leaving the Town Hall, she represented the Second Worcester     
       and Middlesex District in the Massachusetts Senate for 8 years, and as part of Governor         
       Weld’s cabinet, served as Secretary of Housing & Community Development for 5 years.       
       She served as President of the Massachusetts Treasurer and Tax Collector Association and  
       as Assistant Minority Leader in the Senate and on many boards and committees. She was  
       recognized by Fitchburg State College as one of the 100 citizens who made the greatest  
       difference in North Central Massachusetts in the last 100 years.  
 
 
 
David M. Shea, 67 
David Shea passed away on August 8, 2015 after a courageous battle with cancer. David 
was an atmospheric scientist with an environmental consulting company for 37 years. 
He was a longtime resident of Lunenburg. David was an original founder of the 
Lunenburg Recycling Commission and a member of the Board of Health for 30 years. 
He was a passionate golfer who enjoyed playing as well as organizing an annual golf 
tournament. David was a soccer coach and a high school soccer referee for many years. 
David was known for his great sense of humor and his unending energy. He seemed to  
                            know everyone and was referred to many times as the “Mayor of Lunenburg.”

LUNENBURG 
Lunenburg was settled in 1718 and incorporated in 1728. It  occupies a  land area of 
approximately 26.4 square miles. Lunenburg is located in north central Massachusetts 
approximately 43 miles from Boston.  It is bordered on the west by the Town of Ashby and the 
City of Fitchburg, on the south by the Town of Lancas ter and the City of Leominster, on the east 
by the Town of Shirley, and on the north by the Town of Townsend. 
 
Local legislative decisions are made by an open town meeting consisting o f all registered voters 
in the t own. Subject to the legislative decision s made by town meeti ng, the affairs of the t own 
are generally administered by a five member Board of S electmen and a  Town Manager. Local 
school affairs are administered by an elected seven member School Committee while local taxes 
are assessed by a three m ember Board of A ssessors, all are appointed on an at -large basis for 
staggered three-year terms. 
 
Lunenburg is a semi-rural community located in northern Worcester County.  Three water bodies 
account for somewhat of a clustered approach to the t own's histo rical development.  Lake 
Whalom and the former Whalom Amusement Park delineated the Whalom District from the 
remainder of Lunenburg.  This district was developed as a housing base for  many factory 
workers working in the mills and factories of Leominster an d Fitchburg.   In many ways the 
characteristics of the Whalom District more closely reflect characteristics of these cities than of 
the rest of Lunenb urg. The Hickory Hills area of t own, around Hickory Hills Lake, was 
developed as a private community compri sed largely of vacation cottages.  The third body of 
water is Shirley Re servoir, a public water body also developed with mainly vacation cottages 
that have now been modified to year -round residences. Commercial and industrial development 
is permitted in several areas of town, primarily along the two major arteries (Rout es 2A and 13), 
and the t own's peripheries , due largely to the close proximity of Leominster's and Fitchburg's 
sewer and water systems.  Although many farms and orchards had slowly developed in to single 
family housing, farming continues o n in Lunenburg.  At the Annual Town Meeting on May 4 th, 
2013, the Town voted to establish an Agricultural Commission to represent, preserve, and 
enhance farming in the community and to administer the “Right to Farm” bylaw.

REPORT OF THE TOWN MANAGER 
 
The Town continued to make important progress in 2015 in many areas, including:  School Building; 
Building Reuse; Increased Public Transportation; and updating the Pavement Management Plan.    The MS/ 
HS building is well under construction and set to open in September 2016.  Fifty-eight (58%) of the 
project’s eligible costs will be paid through a grant from the MA School Building Authority.   As of 
December 31, 2015, approximately $30M of the Town’s total share had been permanently financed in order 
to take best advantage of the low interest rates.   
 
The Board of Selectmen (BOS) appointed Building Reuse Committee continued its work throughout 2015.  
In the summer, the Committee issued a Request for Proposals for a Building Reuse a nd Space Needs Study.  
Town's goal for the Project was to assess the existing conditions of four (4) buildings owned by the Town 
and determine the most cost effective building(s) to accommodate the Town's current and anticipated space 
needs for Town administrative operations currently housed at Town Hall and remote sites.  The building(s) 
that are determined not to be advantageous to the Town may be deemed as “surplus property“ and disposed 
of in accordance with M.G.L. c. 30B§16.  The Assessment and Space Needs study include the following 
four (4) buildings:  Town Hall; Ritter Memorial Building: Thomas C. Passios Elementary; and the Brooks 
House.  In late summer, the Town hired Tappé & Associates, the same firm serving as Architect on the MS/ 
HS School Project, to complete this study.  The results of the study were presented to the BOS in 2016.  
 
The BOS worked diligently with the Department of Public Works to update its existing Pavement 
Management Plan.  The updated plan built upon the foundation of the existing plan but included additional 
evaluation criteria.  The original plan was developed in 2009 and funding decisions from that point up until 
FY16 were based upon that plan.  Beginning in FY2017, all funding decisions will be based upon the 
revised plan which established a PCI, or Pavement Condition Index, for each roadway and a Prioritize List 
which takes into account both the functional classification of the roadway and the needed repair.  The 
Board further determined that a PCI of 70 would be considered a generally acceptable pavement condition 
and worked on determining the best funding mechanism for the PMP Program to insure that maintenance 
needs would be addressed uniformly across all roadway classifications.  To that end, the Board agreed to 
put forth a debt exemption question to the voters, seeking an additional $4M in tax dollars to complete the 
large backlog of needed maintenance & repair work.  The work would be completed over a period of 3 – 4 
years and would be financed over a period of at least twice as long.  Thereafter, the Town would endeavor 
to include additional funding within the Operating Budget to provide for a sufficient level of funding to 
maintain roads at a PCI of 70. 
 
The Town also continued to work on its largest and most comprehensive roadway project, the 
redevelopment of Summer Street, a project slated to begin in fall of 2016.  In 2014, the Town committed 
$230,312 from Chapter 90 Funding to fund the Town’s portion of the 75% design.  The project is expected 
to cost about $7M, all of which will be funded though the Regional Transportation Improvement Plan 
(TIP), which is a grant of State & Federal funds.  The project was approved for TIP funding for FFY2017. 
 
One of the most exciting projects which continued into 2015 was the implementation of enhanced public 
transportation within Lunenburg.  The Town pays both an annual assessment to the MART and to the 
MBTA.  The assessment to MART pays for local/ regional transportation.  The assessment to the MBTA 
pays for access to the commuter rail system in Fitchburg and Leominster.  The Town learned through 
MART that there is a provision in State Law that would allow the Town to use its MBTA Assessment to 
purchase additional local transportation, at no additional cost to the taxpayer.  Accor dingly, the Town 
undertook a review of residents’ transportation needs and began working with MART to provide additional 
local transportation.  The additional services began in spring 2015, though ridership was low.  The Town 
was able to revise its transportation offerings to include limited service along the full length of Route 2A, 
from the town center to the Shirley line.  The Town will continue to monitor ridership and make 
adjustments as needed to best service the public. 
 
I would be remiss if I did not take this opportunity to thank all of the people in this town who give of their 
time and many talents to make Lunenburg a better place.  Thank you all for your dedicated service.

BOARD OF ASSESSORS 
      
 
The Board of Assessors saw its first transition in a number of years.  Elected to the Board twenty-one years ago, 
Christopher Comeau chose not to seek re-election.  Chris was a reliable and respected member who could be 
counted on to support the office in its long-standing mission to improve the office, render fair tax assessments, and 
treat the taxpaying public well.  Never shying away from offering opinions over the Town’s financial state, he 
sought to find balance between financial prudence and supporting worthwhile projects. 
 
Replacing Mr. Comeau on the Board is Richard Letarte, a prominent accountant and businessman.   Mr. Letarte 
brings considerable experience and time to work on special assignments. 
 
The Assessors’ administrative staff, Louise Paquette and Sheila Craigen, along with the professional assessment 
staff of Regional Resource Group, Inc. (RRG) led by Harald Scheid round out the office.  RRG veteran assessor 
Rebecca Boucher replaced George Bourgault as Associate Regional Assessor. 
 
The office anticipates a busy 2016 calendar year.  An improving economy will see the construction of more homes 
and apartment dwellings.

Agricultural Commission 
 
 The Agricultural Commission was created in 2013 with the aim of encouraging the continued 
pursuit of agriculture in Lunenburg, promoting agriculturally-based economic opportunities, providing an 
opportunity for mediation and negotiation of farming issues, educating the community and advocating for 
the continued understanding of the importance of locally produced food and farm products.   
              
            With this continued understanding and the continued support of the community, we can work 
toward the preservation of prime agricultural lands, which serve to beautify Lunenburg visually and 
maintain the rural charm wherein Lunenburg’s appeal lies.   
 
            The Agricultural Commission has had the opportunity to mediate several issues concerning 
farming and has tried through education and investigation to help the parties involved reach equitable 
arrangements within the existing laws and protections.   
 
            We have provided information to prospective buyers of agricultural property in town as well as 
providing guidance to residents preparing to go before town commissions.  We are currently adding links 
to information and services from the Agricultural Commission website.  With the strong support of the 
Board of Selectmen, the Agricultural Commission was able to substantially expand the Lunenburg 
Community Farmers’ Market, offering a wider variety of vendors, products and musical entertainment 
from the local area.   
 
            The Agricultural Commission would like to express heartfelt thanks to the Farmers’ Market site 
management team, to the participating vendors, the entertainers (usually paid in produce!) and especially 
to the members of the community who have shown a strong and consistent support of the market and the 
local farmers who depend on community patronage.

BUILDING DEPARTMENT 
 
The Building Departmen t is located  on the second floor of the  Ritter Memorial Building, 960 Massachusetts 
Avenue, Lunenburg, MA .  Office hours for the Building Department are M onday and Wednesday 8:00 A.M. to 
4:00 P.M., Tues day and Thursday 8:00 A.M. to 6:00 P.M. The Building Department  is closed on Fridays.  
Michael J. Sauvageau serves as the Building Commissioner , John Morreale serves as the  Assistant Building 
Inspector and Lisa Normandin is the department Administrative Assistant.   
 
The Building Department now processes all Wiring, Plumbing and Gas permits, a duty that was formerly done in 
the Office of the Town Clerk.   
 
Building permits for 2015 decreased slightly from 481 in 2014 to 452 for 2015 but still reflects increased 
construction activity since the tail end of the economic and housing downturn a couple of years back. Solar 
permits remain steady as does new single family homes and townhouses. The Emerald Place project should be 
completed this year. Several large projects are in the reviewing stage or have been approved and are anticipated to 
begin construction soon. The new high school is progressing well and it appears it will be completed on or before 
schedule. 
 
The 2009 International Building Code for residential and commercial construction is the current building code 
and it is anticipated that a new Ninth Edition Code (2015 International Building Code) will be in effect by June of 
this year. 
 
Residents and developers are reminded that Lunenburg is a “Stretch Code Community” which has specific 
code requirements in addition to the current energy code requirements. 
 
Carbon monoxide detectors are required by law to be installed in every home regardless of the age of the 
dwelling.  Also, all swimming pools are required to obtain a building permit and an electrical permit.  Inflatable 
pools that do not have code required fencing around them do not meet the building code and should be removed.   
 
Residents should be aware that inflatable swimming pools do not meet code compliance  requirements.  P lease 
contact our office to determine what steps need to take place for compliance and safety.  
 
Residents should be aware that Massachusetts Law requires that all persons, partnerships and corporations who 
bid or perform residential contracting (reconstruction, alterations, additions etc.) for work exc eeding $1000.00 be 
registered with the Commonwealth.  This office will enforce this provision, however you should ask your 
contractor about his/her registration.  Please contact this office with any questions about this requirement or call 
for any information regarding zoning requirements or building code issues.   
 
Michael J. Sauvageau 
Building Commissioner & Zoning Official

Conservation Commission 
 
The Town of Lunenburg Conservation Commission is a seven member board appointed to 3-year terms 
by the Board of Selectmen, whose duties are codified under the Conservation Commission Act M.G.L. 
Chapter 40, § 8C. The Commission enforces the Massachusetts Wetland Protection Act M.G.L. Chapter 
131, § 40 and Article XXII of the local bylaws for the protection of wetlands. 
  
The year 2015 was one of a very busy and active agenda containing both public hearings and meetings for 
a wide range of projects from commercial development, subdivisions and small single family upkeep 
projects. The Conservation Commission worked to spell out goals of preparing to use the year 2016 to 
revise its regulations to provide consistency with the original by-law and to find those sections that may 
be out of date and need to be modified. 
 
The Commission held numerous inspections with its agent on weekends prior to meetings to familiarize 
themselves with the upcoming proposals that would appear on their agenda. The Commission is working 
with the Town on familiarizing itself with the upcoming storm water mandates from the EPA and 
working towards compliance both by procedure and with any necessary codification. 
 
The Conservation Commission in cooperation with the Open Space Committee and the Lunenburg Snow 
Riders Club has closed out a grant that obtained the former Lane property as Open Space and has worked 
to place an adequate and American Disabilities Act- compliant parking area that would blend in with the 
trail heads on Holman Street. The Snow Riders continue to obtain grant monies to continue to improve 
trail access to be enjoyed by all. The Conservation Commission held public recreational walks through its 
open space parcels and plans to continue to do so.

PAVEMENT MANAGEMENT  
 
The u pdated Pavement Management Plan , inclusive of an electronic database  and a revised 
Town-wide pavement defect analysis. For a second time, a complete survey of every road was completed. 
The amount of new paving does not keep up with the seasonal deterioration factor, and as such the 
backlog in excess of nine million dollars. The program remains underfunded.  
Road Projects typically extend over two construction seasons and  overlap from one fiscal year to 
the next. During this year the following roads were addressed either totally or in part: Howard St., 
Reservoir Rd., Goodrich St., and smaller sections of Arbor, Page, and Kilburn Sts.   
Documentation provided through a “pot hole” log  ( a daily record of road work specific to 
patching and filling of pot holes) proved that one third of all the available work days during the season are 
dedicated to patching and other temporary repairs to similar roadway failures. As defects are identified we 
respond to every location in a timely manner.  This has proven to be as less than effective  method of road 
repair. New pot holes appear each spring particularly after a severe winter, and often staff addresses  the 
same areas year after year.  
The seasonal crack sealing program has been effective in some locations however this application 
is designed as a temporary solution with an effective life span of around five years. Many streets that have 
been “sealed” are well beyond that time frame. This process involves the installation of rubberized asphalt 
under high pressure to cracks or separations in the p avement. The Pavement Management Plan identifies 
specific areas where this method is the best option.   This application does not take the place of curb to 
curb paving, only postpones it and is not applicable in areas of more serious defects. 
The revised Pavement plan, no matter how detailed, is of little value without an identified source of 
funding. Lack of revenue continues to be the major detriment to improving the overall condition of our

ENVIRONMENTAL 
 
Storm Water 
 
For yet another year  the Environmental Protection Agency has not  set the requirements for the 
next phase of storm water regulation and permits. As we aw ait the potential of what will be an  unfunded 
Federal mandate the Town remains in compliance with current protocol.  
The DPW , as a result of limited funding  is forced to become reactionary to roadway drainage 
issues. A capital program in the 1990’s earmarked $20,000.00 annually tow ard rehabilitation/installation 
of piping and catch basins. The elimination of this dedicated funding placed the financial burden upon the 
overall General Highway maintenance allocatio n effectively reducing drainage repairs. In anticipation of 
the new regulations the Town Manager has re -established a budget line within t he DPW dedicated to 
storm water, a necessary first step.  
As roadways are scheduled for pavement, drainage is surveyed and necessary repairs are 
addressed. Little or no preventative maintenance, short of  public safety issues in the form of flooding or 
structural failure, street sweeping and  catch basin cleaning can be  initiated due t o a lack of available 
resources and the absences of a Town wide drainage survey. New storm water regulations will likely 
force the Town to become more proactive albeit with no identified source of funding. At the conclusion of 
winter and again in the late fall drainage structures in close proximity to open water are cleaned. Other 
structures throughout the Town are addressed as time and resources allow on an intermittent schedule. 
  
Waste Water 
 
The DPW continues as the caretak er of the entire sewer system. In an effort to become more 
efficient the Sewer Commission has outsourced much of the day to day inspections and preventative 
maintenance of the overall wastewater system inclusive of 10 pump stations.  Business management and 
oversight of the subcontractor remain the responsibility of the DPW.   
 
Tree Removal   
 
While it may seem difficult to believe, the infamous “Ice Storm of ‘08” continues to detrimentally 
affect trees in the Town right -of-way. As presented to the Board of Selectman during the initial recovery 
process, the “heartwood” of a large volume of trees was affected. Previously unnoticed damage together 
with natural att rition of every species  creates a backlog of tree felling  and pruning that far exceeds the 
allotted budget. The department is unable to fulfill each resident request and must prioriti ze work based 
upon the calculated degree of danger to residents and prope rty. A more efficient approach would involve 
a survey of all Town owned trees with a generated list of those requiring attention. Until a plan is in place, 
the DPW will act in a reactionary mode. Al l work aside from brush removal, chipping, and the felling  of 
small diameter trees is outsourced due to the necessity of a bucket truck and a licensed professional. Each 
spring DPW staff initiates the activity of removing and disposing of woody debris within the right-of-way 
and a Town-wide program of curbside mowing. 
 
Landfill 
 
The “capped” landfill off Young’s Rd. remains  in compliance with  the inspection protocol 
mandated by  the Division of Enviro nmental Protection.  The site is monitored  regularly for excessive 
methane gas as well as the possibility of groundwater contamination.  
The frequency of the testing was reduced through a special permit acquired as a result of 
consistently favorable results.  The landfill site including the  perimeter fencing is inspected on a regular 
schedule by DPW personnel.

Solid Waste 
 
Public works monitors, orders, stores, and distributes trash bags in order to facilitate the curbside

Lake Shirley Dam 
 
In the early 1990’s, with the structure in need of repair/rebuilding The Lake Shirley Association turned 
over ownership of the dam to the Town. After completion of a total  reconstruction, responsibilities for 
maintenance, monitoring, and record keeping was completed solely by a small number of volunteers from 
the Lake Association. In 2004-2005 custodianship of the dam, compliance with State dam regulations, the 
establishment of an operations manual as well as an emergency action plan was placed under the umbrella 
of the Department of Public Works. As with the storm water program and other EPA / DEP mandates 
compliance with current and future regulations is critical. We rely on the expertise and support of a 
professional consultant to assist with predetermined mandates. The most recent inspection resulted in a 
positive report.   
 
Winter Operations 
 
Snow and ice present a major challenge to our Town. The safety of the general public depends on 
the ability of the DPW to maintain our roadways in a safe condition despite the severity or the duration of 
snow and ice events. To that end we develop a winter operations procedu re based upon a detailed  plan 
that must be flexible due to the unpredictability of each storm. A consistently high level of service is 
expected and must be delivered to every part of Town in a timely manner.  
Departmental preparedness begins with successful implementation of the capital plan.  Financial 
support is necessary to provide effective fleet rotation insuring minimal interruption of services due to 
breakdowns and  repairs. Many of the front line vehicles are well beyond the optimum time for 
replacement. I n the fall, salt and sand are delivered to the Chase Rd. facility . Lunenburg is in a 
consortium with 14 other communities in an effort to procure the lowest possible price for salt. 
Individually the Town also solicits bids for road sand. From late summer to early fall vehicle maintenance 
staff inspects and prepares the trucks, sanders and plows for winter op erations. During the construction 
season road crews address right -of-way issues, overhanging branches, and pavement defects in order to 
eliminate obstacles to plowing. Each division within the DPW is involved in winter operations. Typically 
DPW staff combines with 18-20 subcontractors to provide service.  
Generally it is not the amount of snow but the duration of the event that determines the overall 
cost. For example, a fast moving snow storm of 6-8 inches will cost significantly less than a long duration 
freezing rain event even though the accumulation is much less. Historically, the department operates in a 
deficit due to a yearly allocation that is below the cost of an average winter.

Lunenburg Public Library  
The Lunenburg Library is alive and well.  Since last May we have seen lots of changes. Karen Kemp, 
Children’s Librarian for 29 years, retired.  She is currently enjoying her new life which gives her more 
time for her art (she is a painter) and travel. Kate McCarron who served in this role for more than 24 years 
retired in March.  She is enjoying her time with her new grandson.  We were able to hire a new Young 
Adult Librarian, Nicole Piermarini.  Nicole has a Master’s Degree in Information Science and lots of 
experience in the Young Adult realm.  She herself is a Dr. Who fan and is an avid gamer.  Debra Laffond 
joined us in September to become our new Children’s Librarian. Debra filled this role in Pelham for 
almost 10 years.  She was named Children’s Librarian of the Year in New Hampshire in 2013 for her 
exceptional work.  The staff and Library Trustees are thrilled to have Nicole and Debra here at the LPL.  
Sandy Altucher, our first ever Digital Services Librarian, left in late fall.  Sandy’s very personable style 
and technical expertise is sorely missed.  We also hired two new pages, Sage Scorse and Robin Morris, 
they have become trusted assets in the Library. 
The Board of Trustees said goodbye to Jeanne Raboin and Joanne McQuaid.  We thank them for their 
time and energy in helping us. Kiirja Paananen, Donna Saiia and Susan Visser were welcomed to the 
Board. 
The Library building celebrated its 10th anniversary in December.  Lots of folks that were intimately 
involved with the fundraising and the building of this Library returned to help us celebrate – even the 
architect, J. Robert Stewart stopped by.  It was a wonderful heartfelt gathering.    
Changes to the building this year included a new burner (which the staff really enjoyed this winter) and 
the migration of exterior lighting to LED, a more environmentally efficient option.  Our thanks to the 
Lunenburg’s Green Community Task Force for the funding and the help in getting this done.  We are 
hoping to receive a grant to allow us to migrate the interior lights to LED as well.   We also painted the 
café area and the Teen Room.  We feel very lucky that we have the Friends organization, the Board of 
Trustees and the DPW who really take a lot of pride in how the Library looks and operates.   
In terms of technology we have upgraded our Wi-Fi network and added a general Facebook page for a 
total of three Facebook pages.  We have also added three new EReaders and tablets that can be borrowed.  
The Library now has a “real” telescope that can also be borrowed, as well as a volleyball kit that has 
everything in it if you would like to play.   
One thing we have focused on this year is more community outreach.  We know that in order for the 
Library to maintain its position in Lunenburg we have to provide relevant resources to the community – 
whether that be programs or library materials.  We are trying hard to connect with the different 
community organizations and assist them in whatever way we can. A couple of examples are the 
Lunenburg Historical Society, who is now having their meetings here at the Library with the Library 
helping them to advertise; we also have reached out to area preschools and they are now coming to the 
Library on a weekly basis; and our YA Librarian and Brianna Lively at the High School have joined 
forces and resources to share an Anime Club. 
We have really increased our programs this year – in all areas.  We certainly spanned a broad range of 
interests from a Quidditch match in the main room of the Library to Ukulele lessons to an exhibition of 
hundreds of fossils and so much more. 
The staff and Trustees of the Lunenburg Library would like to thank all of our hard working volunteers, 
they are part of the fabric of the Library and they make so many things possible: Bea Adams, Sandy 
Flanagan, Barbara Fleming, Doug Fleming, Ruth Hamilton, Carol Gaudet, Karen Godin, and Ann Smith.

Thoughts from Nicole Piermarini, Young Adult (YA) Librarian: 
A new chapter started in the teen room this year, beginning with the retirement of Kate McCarron after 24 
years of service.  And while she will be dearly missed, we are very excited to start this new chapter and 
add to what Kate began. 
Upon entering the teen room, you will notice some changes and a new layout. The computers all have 
new adjustable chairs, suited for the high counters.  There is a graffiti bulletin board in the corner hangout 
area, complete with chalk markers. Also, there is a new gaming center right as you walk into the room.  
We purchased a Wii U console and games to create a kit that teens can borrow at the main desk and play 
in house on the new television.  The new layout has attracted new teens to the Library and it is now one of 
THE places to be after school. 
The space isn’t the only part of the room to get a face lift.  We are slowly examining the collection, 
removing damaged books, updating and replacing them as necessary in an effort to ensure we have the 
most up-to-date and responsive collection possible.  The manga collection has undergone a massive 
overhaul in an effort to create space and to make it more current.  Series are being completed and new 
popular ones are being added.  In the fiction collection, we are relabeling our series to include order 
number in an effort to make them easier to find.  As each section is completed, it has been given new 
signage to draw attention and interest.   
2015 was a year in transition and we will continue to examine, update, and strive to make the teen room 
the best, most responsive space it can be. 
Thoughts from Debra Laffond, Children’s Librarian 
The year 2015 saw the end of an era in the Lunenburg Public Library- beloved Children’s Librarian 
Karen Kemp retired after 29 years.  While she will be missed, we were very excited to make some

SENIOR PROPERTY TAX WORK-OFF PROGRAM 
Est. 1998 
CALANDAR YEAR JANUARY 1, 2015 – NOVEMBER 1, 2015  
 
“A unique approach to balance the social & financial needs of senior homeowners and local governments 
of Massachusetts” Archhana Prakash, Post Doctoral Fellow of Population Studies at the Center of 
University of Michigan 
The Property Tax Work-Off Committee is a 5 member committee appointed by the Lunenburg Board of 
Selectmen. 
 
The Lunenburg Property Tax Work-Off Program offers income eligible senior citizens who are age 60 
plus an opportunity to receive up to $1,000 off their property tax bill in the calendar year rather than the 
fiscal year.  Participants worked in various town departments to earn the tax abatement doing general 
office duties, custodial, library shelving, and  kitchen assistance. 
For each hour worked a person received $10 and worked up to 100 hours between January 1, 2015 and 
November 1, 2015 to receive the abatement.  Any hours worked beyond that were considered volunteer 
hours. 
There were nine Lunenburg residents who participated between January 1, 2015 and November 1, 2015.  
These individuals as a whole accumulated 865.5 hours towards their abatements. 
Eligibility Requirements: 
 Applicants must be 60 years of age or older 
 Own and occupy a home in Lunenburg as their primary residence for at least one year 
 Receive a property tax bill in their name or their spouse’s name who is also 60 years of age or 
older 
 Meet the income guidelines of :  
One person household - $40,000 / Two person household - $60,000 
 All new applicants are required to have a CORI background check and must re- apply and be 
accepted annually for participation in the program. 
Earnings are exempt from state taxes only.  Earnings are subject to federal and FICA tax withholdings 
and are also subject to OBRA if participants are not receiving a pension from either Worcester Regional 
Retirement or Massachusetts State Teachers Retirement.

SEWER COMMISSION 
 
The five member Sewer Commission was originally established at the Special Town Meeting on May 6, 2006.  
At the first Sewer Commission meeting after the May 2014 Town election, when the Commission elects the 
officers for  the next year, Carl Luck was elected to remain as Chairman, Dave MacDonald to serve as Vice-
Chair and Mike Nault to serve as Clerk.  Other members on the Commission were Butch Bilotta and Troy 
Daniels, who was elected to the Sewer Commission in the May 2014 Town Election as a write-in candidate to 
fill the vacancy left by Jay Simeone who did not run for re-election. The Sewer Commission bears the 
responsibility of overseeing the Town’s sewer system, planning for the future, and developing and implementing 
rules, regulations and policies.  The Department of Public Works manages and maintains the operational side of 
the system.  Under the Town’s Charter, responsibility for the day-to day operation, care and maintenance of the 
sanitary sewers are under the supervision of the Town Manager.   The Town of Lunenburg does not operate a 
sewer treatment plant. Approximately 81% of Lunenburg sewer is discharged to the City of Leominster, and the 
other 19% is discharged to the City of Fitchburg.    
  
There are currently 764 properties connected to the Lunenburg system and another 378 that have access to Town 
Sewer but are not yet connected. During FY15, 36 new accounts connected to Town Sewer.  The Commission 
billed out $715,400 in quarterly sewer bills, and had $41,759 of prior year delinquencies added to the owners’ 
property tax bills for non-payment.   Nine properties with access to sewer were assessed privilege fees that had 
not originally been assessed betterments.  Privilege fees help to pay down the debt owed by the Town on the 
original sewer project.  
 
In FY15, it was required by the State Department of Revenue that the Sewer Enterprise and the Sewer 
Betterment Funds be combined for a consolidated statement, and for the first time, the Commission had to 
project the year-to-year income over expense as pertains to betterment income versus debt service.  With these 
revisions to the budget, it was necessary to request a transfer from Retained Earnings for $77,401.80 (Article 17, 
May 2015 Town Meeting) to cover both general expenses and debt service for the next fiscal year.  The goal of 
the Sewer Commission is to maintain a balanced budget going forward and to build up the Retained Earnings so 
that it may be used for major capital projects to maintain and improve the operating efficiency of the system.    
 
In August 2013, the Sewer Commission began contracting with Small Water Systems Services of Littleton 
(SWSS) for the Operations and Management of the 10 pumping stations and sewer infrastructure. The Sewer 
Commission has been very satisfied with the personnel, the work and the arrangement with SWSS, renewing the 
contract again for FY15.  Since the start of the contract, additional responsibilities have been added to the SWSS 
contract, including installing and monitoring the SCADA system at the Leominster Road station in March 2015 
and taking on the maintenance of the 10 generators housed in our pumping stations.   
 
The Sewer Commission continued work on preparing for the major sewer project in the Pratt Street/Lakeview 
Ave sections of town, using the State Revolving Fund (SRF) program throughout FY15, to connect 80 plus 
homes in areas of need as determined by the Comprehensive Wastewater Management Plan to public sewer. 
Forty-two of these homes were in close proximity to Whalom Lake and were of concern to the homeowners, 
Board of Health and environmentalists.  The proposed sewer extensions had attained a satisfactory rating by the 
State to be assured of an SRF loan and the Commission presented the project at the spring Town Meeting in 
May 2014, obtaining approval to move the project forward.  The Commission worked continuously with the 
engineering firm, Wright Pierce, throughout the year on design and engineering.  In the spring of 2015, the 
Commission put the project out to bid and in May 2015 awarded the construction bid to CJP Construction out of 
Millis, MA, with construction to commence in the summer of 2015.
