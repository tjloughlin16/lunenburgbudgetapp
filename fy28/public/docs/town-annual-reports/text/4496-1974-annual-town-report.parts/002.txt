IN MEMORY OF
Rosaire Chapdelaine was not only very well
known in the area as a farmer and business-
man, but also as an individual who possessed
a keen awareness and respect for his fellow-
man. Undoubtedly "Rosaire" will best be
remembered by many for his attendance and
active participation in the deliberations at
Town Meetings.
ROSAIRE CHAPDELAINE
1905 - 1974
IN MEMORY OF
William A. Mason, Jr. served as Chairman and
member of the Lunenburg Planning Board for ten
years and was the first Chairman of the Monta-
chusett Regional Planning Commission. He was
a graduate of Bentley School of Accounting, a
Navy Veteran of World War II and a member
of many civic and professional organizations in
the area. Prior to retirement "Bill" was the
proprietor of a Mobile Home Park in Lunenburg.
WILLIAM A. MASON, JR.
1912 - 1974

SUMMARY - The proposed constitutional amendment, if approved, would provide for a census in the year 1975 and every
tenth year thereafter of the inhabitants of each city and town as a basis for determining the representative, senatorial and councillor
districts for the ten year period beginning with the first Wednesday in the fourth January following the taking of the census, pro-

QUESTION NO. 3 - PROPOSED AMENDMENT TO THE CONSTITUTION
Do you approve of the adoption of an amendment to the constitution summarized below, which was approved by the General Court
in a joint session of the two branches held June 7, 1972, received 250 votes in the affirmative and 3 in the negative, and in a
joint session of the two branches held June 6, 1973, received 253 votes in the affirmative and 0 in the negative?
SUMMARY The proposed constitutional amendment would repeal Section 2 of Article 46 of the Articles of Amendments to
the Constitution of the Commonwealth (the "anti-aid amendment", so-called), and replace it with a new Section 2, for the purpose
of allowing grants in aid to private higher educational institutionsor to students, or parents or guardians of students, attending such
institutions.
The proposed amendment would delete the first clause of the present Section 2, which requires that all moneys raised by
taxation in the towns and cities for the support of public schools, and all moneys appropriated by the Commonwealth for the support
of common schools shall be applied to or expended in only those schools conducted according to law under the order and super-
intendence of the authorities of the town or city in which the money is expended. The effect of the deletion of the first clause of
Section 2 would be to remove the constitutional prohibition against the use of public moneys, which have been raised by taxation
or appropriated for support of public schools, for grants in aid to private higher educational institutions or to students, or parents
or guardians of students, attending such institutions.
The proposed amendment would also alter the second clause of the present Section 2 by removing the prohibition against a
grant, appropriation or use of public money or property or loan of public credit by the Commonwealth or any political subdivision
for the purpose of founding, maintaining or aiding any school or institution of learning, whether under public control or otherwise,
wherein any denominational doctrine is inculcated, or any other school or any college which is not publicly owned and under the
exclusive control, order and superintendence of public officers or public agents. In place of the foregoing, the proposed amend-
ment would prohibit the grant, appropriation or use of public money or property or loan of credit by the Commonwealth or any
political subdivision for the purpose of founding, maintaining or aiding any primary or secondary school which is not publicly owned
and under the exclusive control, order and superintendence of public officers or public agents.
The proposed amendment would also add to the present Section 2a provision that nothing therein shall be construed to pro-
hibit grants in aid to private higher educational institutions or to students, or parents, or guardians of students, attendingsuch
institutions.

QUESTION NO. 5 - LAW PROPOSED BY AN INITIATIVE PETITION
Do you approve of a law summarized below which was disapproved in the House of Representatives by a vote of 73 in the affirmative
and 150 in the negative and was disapproved in the Senate by a vote of 16 in the affirmative and 22 in the negative?
SUMMARY - Provisions of the act establish an independent Corrupt Practices Commission, with five members to be appointed
by the Governor to staggered five year terms. The Commissionh subpoena powers and is to investigate, by means of secret hear-
ings, candidates' compliance with all state and federal laws relating to political campaign contributions and expenditures and
corrupt practices. If probable cause is shown, the Commission is required to direct the Attorney General to take further action in
the proper form and is required to make public a report of such action. In cases involving the campaign practices of the Attorney
General himself, a special attorney is to be appointed. If no probable cause is shown, the Commission is to state so publicly.
Further, the proposed act extends the application of the current statute regarding the disclosure of campaign expenditures
and contributions, to all candidates for office above the town and city level, with the exception of President and Vice President
of the United States. The act requires all such candidates to receive and disburse all amounts greater than $25 by check, and makes
it a crime to knowingly receive cash payment from a candidate or his committee fora service costing more than $25. The actalso
requires candidates to designate a singlebank as depository of funds and as recordkeeper, with records of receipts and expenditures
to be open to public scrutiny.
Provisions of the act extend present campaign spending limits to cover all media expenses and require all media firms (in-
cluding television, radio, newspaper, billboard, magazine, advertising, public relations, printing, opinion polling, computer,
telephone, telegraph) to report the purchase of media services by candidates. Media expenses of candidates for the offices of
district attorney, clerk of court, register of probate and insolvency, register of deeds, county commissioner, county treasurer and
sheriff are limited to $.07 for each resident of the respective electorial district. Other provisions of the act require candidates,
upon official announcement or filing of nomination papers, to report all political receipts and expenditures since the date of the
last general election for the office sought, and count such expenditures toward spending limits. The act also limits candidates to
one political committee.
The act establishes a maximum penalty of one year imprisonment and/or $10,000 fine for individuals engaged in corrupt
practices, and a $50,000 fine for corporations so involved. In addition, the Attorney General is permitted to bring an action for
the removal of a candidate whose election was materially aided by corrupt practices on the part of the candidate or one acting in
his behalf, where such removal is otherwise authorized by law.

AN EDITORIAL FROM THE SUPERINTENDENT
OF SCHOOLS -
Last April, Lunenburg voters approved an opera-
ting school budget request of $2,573,377. This sum
WILLIAM R. HOLLAND
reflected a 12.4% increase over the previous year's
Superintendent of Schools
budget appropriation; a percentage increase which
also comes close to representing the average annual
school budget increase experienced in Lunenburg over the past five years.
As usual, over 80% of the budget increase was contributed to teacher salaries with the remain-
ing increases found in the hard-hit energy-related areas, particularly fuel oil and utilities.
Lunenburg, however, was fortunate last year when compared to budget increases in surrounding towns
since teachers had signed a two-year salary contract calling for an approximate 4% increase in
1974-75 and bus contractors were completing the third year of their bus contracts. These facts,
plus an unanticipated higher level of reimbursement for education and other sources from the State,
helped contribute to an approximate $8. decrease in the tax rate.
Unfortunately, keeping school costs down in FY-76 is an extremely difficult if not nearly im-
possible task. Increases in supplies, fuel oil and utilities continue to climb the inflationary
ladder. The awarding of new bus contracts and renegotiating of all employees contracts push costs
upward. New State laws like Chapter 766 (Special Education) and Chapter 622 (Equal Educational
Opportunity) also contribute to budget increases. These factors cause school officials to freeze
or cut present spending levels, eliminate any requests for needed new personnel, and abolish plans
for new programs or the major remodeling of buildings.
Although discouraging, these "belt tightening" measures are being taken in Lunenburg just like
they are being taken in other school districts throughout the country. In fact, in response to
rising unemployment and a suffering economy, local and state newspapers are carrying almost daily
summaries of school and municipal budgets that are being slashed. However, in spite of much
rhetoric in the press and many bold and genuine attempts to keep school costs down, factors beyond
the direct control of school, town, state, and yes, federal officials, continue to push costs
higher each year. With the annual cost-of-living spiraling upward in the vicinity of 12%, local
officials find themselves caught up in a real struggle to keep operating budgets at or below a 10
to 15% increase.
Meanwhile, because nearly 80% of the school budget and the school budget increase is in staff
salaries, school committee members in Lunenburg and elsewhere are spending an inordinate amount of
time in collective bargaining activities, particularly with teachers.
Lamenting the marked decline of candidates for school committee positions throughout the
State, a recent Worcester Evening Gazette editorial made this point:
"The job of school committee members has changed substantially in recent years. As
teachers have become organized in labor unions, school committee members everywhere have
found themselves less involved with educating children and more tied up with bargaining
about teachers rights and salaries.
"On the salary issue, school committees have found themselves largely in no-win
situations caught between a high pressure union and the paying public, damned if they
did and damned if they didn't.

Although rather blunt, this editorial does define the problem. On one hand, teachers and
other staff employees are anxious to realize salary adjustments in order to meet the very real in-
creases in their cost-of-living. On the other hand, taxpayers are resisting ever-rising increases
in local taxes because they too are suffering from rising costs as well as suddenly finding them-
selves with less financial security. Add to this problem the increasing demands being placed upon
your local school system to provide more and improved programs and services to its children while
at the same time keeping its budget in line, it is no wonder there is a dearth of candidates for
school committee positions.
Quite simply, since our enrollment in Lunenburg has and will continue to remain quite stable
at around 2200 pupils, the only real way to significantly hold down school costs is to sacrifice
some of the current quality of our present educational system. For example, we would definitely
have to limit the objectives of the public school to teaching the basic skills. In order to
eliminate staff, class sizes would have to be increased from our current average of 25 pupils to
35 pupils, all extra-curricular programs, particularly the athletic and music programs, would have
to be eliminated, professional staff important to but not directly involved with classroom instruc-
tion (administration, guidance, library, etc.) would have to be significantly reduced, high school
elective courses would have to be eliminated or drastically reduced, and current attempts to pro-
vide students with occupational training and exposure to the fine, performing, and domestic arts
would have to be significantly curtailed.
Even these actions may be difficult to accomplish, since by statute, school systems are re-
quired to provide students with certain required educational experiences. Non-compliance with
certain State laws could mean loss of state funds or civil court action brought against the school
system by parents. State laws and Board of Education regulations clearly define minimum curriculum
standards as well as a host of other required programs and services for youngsters. Some laws and
regulations are many times SO specific that even the number of required instructional hours, the
number and type of required instructional staff, and the number and type of student to be served
is delineated. To make this situation even more alarming in these times of economic stress, is
the tendency on the State level each year to pass more laws that mandate expensive programs with-
out giving any guarantee of State funding to help ease the financial impact of these new programs
locally. Clearly, the financial burden of these new State mandates rests on the sagging shoulders
of the local community.

Further, any attempts to keep school costs down cannot solely be a local activity but has to

PERSONNEL CHANGES:
1974, in Lunenburg Schools, was a year of new faces and several personnel changes. In April,
Carolyn Simsarian and John Ludwig were elected to the School Committee and John Davis was elected
Chairman of that group. Roger Raboin decided not to run for a second term in order to devote more
time to his own business. In his three years on the School Committee, Mr. Raboin took a deep inter-
est in all facets of the school system. His many contributions and his high level of commitment,
particularly in the time he devoted to his school committee functions will be sorely missed.
Several important administrative appointments were also made last Spring. Following the
retirement of Dick Dustin, Paul Ralston of Morristown, Vermont, was appointed as the new principal
of the Thomas Passios Elementary School. Mr. Ralston brought with him the recent experience of
four years as principal of an innovative elementary program in Morrisville. In his short tenure in

Lunenburg, Mr. Ralston has already demonstrated his abilities as a capable and knowledgeable
elementary school administrator.
In June, Richard Bishop and Thomas Starr, two middle school teachers, were appointed as admin-
istrative assistants to Mr. Barney at the Middle School. Filling a vacancy created by the resig-
nation of Robert Antonucci, who moved to Leominster as Assistant Superintendent of Schools, both
administrative assistants devote one-half of their time to administrative responsibilities and the
other half of their schedule to teaching responsibilities. This new administrative arrangement,
which was designed to provide qualified Lunenburg teachers with an opportunity for administrative
experience, has been working quite smoothly to date.
In other personnel actions, Jack White, Middle School Guidance Counselor, and Linda Howard,
Elementary School Guidance Counselor, both resigned to accept positions in other school systems--
positions which offered them professional advancement opportunities. Both counselors made signif-
icant contributions to Lunenburg Schools during their tenure in our school system.
In August, Bob Okerman replaced a capable Ray Lemieux as our Supervisor of Buildings &
Grounds. A talented "master of all trades", Mr. Okerman very quickly learned his new role and has
brought the school custodial and maintenance operations to new heights of efficiency resulting in
considerable savings to the Town.
And finally, Mrs. Doris Lahti, Head Town and School Nurse for the past twenty-one years,
announced her intention to retire in January, 1975. A tireless and dedicated worker, Mrs. Lahti is
a unique person who has contributed greatly to Lunenburg Schools and Lunenburg families over the
years. All staff members of the Lunenburg School System wish Doris much happiness in her retire-
ment.
CHAPTER 766 - THE NEW SPECIAL EDUCATION LAW:
In September, new regulations required Lunenburg to provide expanded educational services to
youngsters, aged 3 to 21, with special needs. The law also required local school systems to pick
up certain special education costs which were previously assumed directly by the State. Although
not providing for any new tax revenue to fund the new law, the State plans to reimburse local
cities and towns for a portion of their special education costs by use of new reimbursement
formulas. Mr. Burgess in his report provides details concerning our first year experiences with
the new law and its regulations.
Proceeding cautiously, Lunenburg provided for a $33,157. increase in its special education
budget last year to cover Chapter 766, a sum which included the hiring of one additional special
needs teacher. Because of the lack of inadequate special needs facilities in the elementary
school, voters in a special town meeting last Fall authorized the School Committee to spend
$48,000. in "up-front" Chapter 766 State Funds to construct a special needs resource classroom
addition to the Passios School. This addition, which is scheduled to be completed by September,
1975, will include two speech therapy rooms, a combined guidance/core evaluation team room, and
two distinct classroom areas for small groups of special needs students. Lunenburg is indeed for-
tunate to have such an addition approved and available for use in the near future.
Finally, our initial experiences with the law indicate a strong need for some increased staff-
ing if we are to meet the new demands being placed upon our present personnel. We purposely
delayed hiring extra special education staff last year since we wanted to gain experience with the
law and its detailed regulations. We now find that in the long run it may be more cost effective
to add selected staff in certain areas like speech and elementary guidance (e. g. psychological
testing) then to contract required services out to costly private clinics or consultants.

school budget or any budget for that matter.

TRANSPORTATION:
Each child in Lunenburg, living a mile or more
from school, is transported daily to classes--a sim-
ple statement of fact, one often taken for granted.
Yet, when considered in total, what did this really mean to the Town in FY-74? Specifically, it
meant that each school day, twenty buses, covering forty separate routes, travelled 726 miles to
carry 1862 kindergarten through grade twelve pupils (approximately 85% of the total school system
enrollment). In the course of the 1973-74 school year, 130,680 miles were logged for regular
transportation!
Student participation in academic field trips and athletic events consumed another 8000 miles
as pupils travelled to 238 events. And, special needs children being transported within the town
and out-of-town to such locations as Fitchburg, Leominster, and Worcester, required approximately
20,000 miles.
Total cost for all transportation services was $158,695.; approximately 76% of this cost was
reimbursed by the State to Lunenburg. Thus, the net cost of transportation was $38,500. in FY-74.
The very reasonable bids submitted by local contractors during the past several years for
transportation is responsible for keeping this cost in line. By coupling the cost factor with the
important human elements of proper pupil deportment, parent cooperation, and excellent bus
contractor and driver service, Lunenburg got superior "mileage" from its transportation service in
1974.

TRANSITIONAL CLASSES:
We have two transitional classes in the Middle School that have proven to be very worthwhile
for some students. Miss Mary Bouchard is the transitional class teacher in grade 5 and Mr. Robert
Saudelli works with the transitional group in grade 6.
A transitional class does not work with children with severe problems, but deals with children
who need more confidence or reinforcement in the basic skills. The classes are smaller than the
regular classes which enables the teacher to spend more time with a student on an individual basis.
The key to the transitional class is flexibility. For example, the transitional teachers are not
locked into a block of time or restricted to a 45-minute Language Arts class. Miss Bouchard and
Mr. Saudelli assess what skills are in need of reinforcement and have the opportunity to devcte
more time in areas where students need specialized assistance.
The students in our transitional classes are not isolated from the rest of the school program
such as Science, Social Studies, Industrial Arts, Homemaking, Physical Education, Art or Music.
They simply are given more individualized help in the areas of Language Arts and Mathematics with
the hope of improving their basic skills and increasing their self-confidence.
