Board of Selectmen
"Progressive Action"
NORMAND THOMPSON
RUDOLPH MASELLA
F. CLIFTON MILLER
Board of Selectmen: left to right - F. Clifton Miller, Chairman Rudolph Masella and Normand J. Thompson.
The year 1971 has been one of the most demanding ever encountered by the Board of Selectmen. Progressive
action which took place in 1971 will be reflected not only in 1972 but for many years to come. The Board of Select-
men is responsible for the operational as well as financial management of many Town departments. We are very for-
tunate in Lunenburg tohave cooperative, dedicated and responsible individuals not only as department heads but also
as employees working in these departments. This close-working relationship, thus assured in Lunenburg, is not always
true in towns our size.
Progress was made in two important categories this year--changes in our refuse disposal area and proposed re-
building of Route 2A necessitated by the high accident rate. One of the major responsibilities of the Board of Health
is the operation of the Municipal Refuse Disposal Area on Townsend Harbor Road. In order to comply with the "No
Burning" law of the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, a controlled disposal area was initiated by the Board of Health,
an Attendant was hired and specific hours were established. The current condition of the disposal area is testimony
to the success of the program. A Refuse Disposal Planning Committee was appointed in 1971 to study the critical
refuse disposal problem and it is hoped that a recommended long-range solution will be ready for submission to the
Town at the 1972 Town Meeting.
Anotherarea of prime concern to the Board of Selectmen is public safety. For many years the Board of Select-
men has been disturbed with the high accident rate and fatalities on Route 2A and has been urging the Department
of Public Works and State officials to take corrective action. All possible avenues to correct this situation have been
examined and with the continued increase in motor vehicle traffic, the problem continued to escalate. After many
meetings with the State Department of Public Works the only course of action left was the relocation and rebuilding
of Route 2A which is scheduled to start this spring.

The Board of Selectmen took the necessary action to become one of the first communities in the area to employ
persons under the provisions of the Emergency Employment Act of 1971. This Act provides that all wages and fringe
benefits which would normally be paid by a community are paid by the Federal Government. At present, two regular
policemen and the Attendant at the Municipal Refuse Disposal Area are employed under this program which, as of
December 31, 1971, has amounted to approximately $3,556.43 in federal funds for a two and one-half month period.
The Town is at present awaiting final approval to employ a Fire/Police Radio Dispatcher under this program, which,
if granted, would indicate that approximately $25,000.00 will be received by the Town for those employed under
the Emergency Employment Act during 1972.
Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors: John F. Nicoll, Clerk Kathleen M. Deming, Bernard J. Paton and Chairman Leroy Allen.
The year 1971 was the second year in which Lunenburg was at full-fair valuation. In 1970, under full-fair
valuation, the tax rate was $33.00 per thousand. Due to a decrease in state receipts and a substantial increase in
town appropriations, the tax rate for 1971 rose to $42.50, an increase of $9.50 per thousand. The local taxpayers
are reminded thatas we expand and increase local services, we must expect a comparable increase in the tax rate.
The Assessorshave made an attempt to correct any inequities or differences reported by the local taxpayers, Consider-
able time has been spent reviewing properties where there were questions and listing new buildings constructed in
town this past year.
The Assessors attended several State and County Association meetings to keep themselves informed on new
legislation. The State Tax Commissioner, recognizing the difficulties of part-time assessors, has determined that all
assessors in the State must attend a school set up by the Commissioner. A manual is alsobeing prepared for assessors
to assist them in making more uniform appraisals throughout the Commonwealth.
Mr. Chester J. Pliska, a member of the Board of Assessors for the last seventeen years, did not seekre-election
in 1971. The Assessors wish to extend their thanks to "Chet" for his many years of service to the community.

There is a limit to what is sane and responsible in Taxation, to the price
people are willing andable to pay. The majority believe that this limit has been
reached, for throughout the State and the Nation the cost of providing basic ser-
vices to the people has skyrocketed, forced upward by inflation, the rising expec-
tations of a growing population, and increased wage levels for public employees.
It is estimated that the cost of state and local services isannually increasing three
times as fast the income from existing state and local taxes. Unless substantial
relief, suchas revenue sharing with the Federal and State governments, materializes
in the near future, the only alternative to rising taxes would seem to be a change in
public expectations in such fields as education, and curtailment of basic services.
More than 80% of state and local revenue comes from sales, property and ex-
cise taxes. This year in Lunenburg 93.5% of all monies received came from state

and local taxes, 21.8% from the State, 64.5% from local property taxes, and 7.2%
from motor vehicle excise. It has been estimated that of the 53 billion dollar in-
crease in state-local tax yields from 1955 to 1969, less than 2/3 came from growth. In Lunenburg, an even smaller
portion, 42%, was attributable to growth, the balance being absorbed by existing property owners. In that period,
while our population increased 44%, the assessed value of all real and personal property increased 77%, property
taxes increased 317%, and the average homeowner found his tax bill to be 2 1/2 times what it was 15 years before.
From 1955 to 1969, state and local expenditures rose 244%, while Lunenburg's expenditures increased 337%.
Economists have estimated that during this period inflation accounted for 44% of the increase in state and local spend-
ing, rising work loads for 26%, and increased scope and quality of services for 30%. Exactly 70% of all amounts
spent for services in Lunenburg in 1971 was for school purposes. In Massachusetts, approximately 72% of school
costs are paid from property taxes, with State and Federal governments paying the remaining 28%. In 1960 Amer-
icans spent 25 billion on public education, and by 1970 that amount had doubled. In the same period, Lunenburg's
expenditures for education rose 21/2 times.

A comparison of total earnings records for the calendar years 1970 and 1971 reflects average increases for full
time municipal employees as follows: Non-School Administrative and Professional - 7.4%; Non-School Non-Admin-
istrative - 7.7%; School Teachers and Administrators - 13%; School Clerical - 9.3%; School Custodial - 10.6%.

our school teachers have the power of collective bargaining, they do not have the right to strike, and their wage

Planning Board
"Rules & Regulations" "
Amendments to the "Rules and Regulations Governing the Sub-division of Land" are planned to require utilities
to be placed underground in new sub-divisions. Increased fees for inspections will be required as well as certain
other charges that experience has proven to be desirable. A new issue of the Protective By-Law is being printed
incorporating all changes to date and arranged in a format which we believe will be more convenient to use.
Revisions to the Protective By-Law were enacted at the 1971 Town Meeting. These revisions require that pro-
visions for parking be included in plans submitted for building permits. Also, a section on definitions was added and
penalties specified for violations of the Protective By-Law.
The Board is concerned with potential sewerage problems and future supply of pure water for the Town as land
development continues. Steps are being taken to take advantage of monies that now appear to be available for a
comprehensive study of the sewerage needs of the Town. The development of land in Lunenburg has continued at an
accelerating pace in 1971. The second section of the Broadmeadow development is in progress and definitive plans
for three other sub-divisions have been received. A preliminary plan has been received for a 249 lot sub-division
in the Pleasant Street-Electric Avenue area. In addition, plans for about 30 lots involving division of parcels "not
requiring sub-division approval" have been signed by the Board as required by statute.
The Planning Board has had inquiries from two developers who wish to build multi-unit housing. The Board
participated in hearings before the Board of Appeals regarding an appeal involving low and moderate income housing
under the General Laws, Chapter 40B. With the advent of increased development in the Town, the Board will be
reviewing the present zoning and sub-division by-laws in an attempt to determine whether or not modification should
be made to the By-Law in order to provide for more orderly future growth, open space and retention of the pleasing
evironment of our Town. The Planning Board will welcome suggestions from the citizens of Lunenburg.

Tree Warden
During 1971, 152 diseased trees were cut down and burned. The Department of Natural Resources gave three
days of sampling amounting to $150.00, and ten days of work amounting to $1,000.00, to assist in detection and re-
moval of diseased trees. Approximately 27 samples were takenby the State and sent to the Shade Tree Laboratory,
University of Massachusetts, for culture. A total of $6,503.15, of which $324.15 was reimbursed by the County,
was spent under the Dutch Elm Disease Account.
Tent Caterpillars infest wild cherry, wild apple, old orchards and shag bark hickory trees and are seen in early
spring. They emerge from their tent and feed all over the tree.
Fall Webworms are similar in type and habits and also infest the same trees. These pests feed only in the con-
fines of their web. Neither does any damage to well cared for trees.
Fifteen Norway Maples were purchased to replace diseased trees with Tree Warden funds. A total of $3,677.00
was spent under this account.
The "Gypsy Moth "-When a single tree or a small number of trees is attacked by gypsy moths, a homeowner may
wish to apply the following techniques developed many years ago, but which can still be quite effective. 1. Tie
with heavy twine, a ring of burlap (about 1 foot wide) around the tree at about breast height. Let the top half of
the burlap hang down over the bottom half. During their later instars, the large caterpillars seek the burlap folds
for protection during the heat of the day. They can be collected daily and destroyed. 2. The egg masses can be
painted with creosote any time prior to hatching. This will effectively kill the eggs. However, caution should be
exercised to prevent exposure of the skin to this material as it is an established cancer producing agent. Creosote
will also kill one of the important gypsy moth egg parasites if it is present in the egg mass.

The year 1971 was a year of change for the library. For the first half of the year the library operated without
a Library Manager. Mrs. Ruth Proctor, Assistant Librarian, undertook the job of Acting Library Manager as well as
continuing her own work. During that time, the Library Trustees, unable to prevail upon Mrs. Proctor to assume the
job, looked for a new Library Manager. In September, Miss Jean Tenander was hired. Mrs. Proctor then resumed
her job as Assistant Librarian. Miss Tenander, who is from Fitchburg, has an M.L.S., an M.A. in Comparative Lit-
erature and a B.A. from the University of Michigan.
The second new staff member at the library is Mrs. Emily Foster, a Lunenburg resident, who began work in Jan-
vary and, since September, has been working in the children's section. Mrs. Foster is the person most frequently seen
at the check-out desk.
A filmprogram was started at the library in late December and, although just underway at this writing, hope-
fully it will prove to be a success. The films to be shown are both American and foreign and are selected either be-
cause they are artistic successes, or they represent a certain period in filmmaking, or because they are the works of
famous directors. The films are shown on Sunday evenings for adults and high school age people. A separate children's
film program may start sometime next year.

Total circulation figures increased from 43,275 to 50,063. The number of adult books circulated was 19,223,

In June, the library began to issue new cards to borrowers and to date has issued a total of 1,761 cards. Prior
to this 5,623 people were on file as card holders and users of the library. However, since the town population is
approximately 7,500 this suggested that virtually everyone in town had a library card. It was for this reason the
decision was made to issue new cards as a way of eliminating the hundreds of names of people who had left town,
died, or for some reason no longer made use of the library.
The number of books purchased this year suffered a marked reduction from last year but this is due solely to the
fact that because of the lack of staff, the library operated on a reduced book budget and the staff concentrated on
readers' services. Adult books purchased this year totaled 275 and children's books, 170. Ninety-seven adult books
and 160 children's books were discarded and are on sale at the library for 10 cents each.

Conservation Commission & Town Forest Committee
"Watch Dogs"
People of Lunenburg wake up and livel It may be later than you think. Your time may be running out.
The countdown mayhave already started. The fateful day may come when you and your children and your children's
children could wake up (if they wake up at all) without air to breathe, water to drink, or food to eat. Our natural
resources could be destroyed, our ecology wrecked and there may be no environment. We can pour millions of dol-
lars into educating our children to live in a world that in twenty years may find water unfit to drink and the oxygen
level too low for human survival.
Today's challenge is to find ways of living in our environment without destroying it. And to this end, your
committees have worked, acting as "watchdogs", protecting the wetlands for the recharging of our water supply,
acquiring areas of forest which not only produce oxygen and encourage wildlife, but also provide areas of undevel-
oped land for the recharging of the human spirit. Our aim is still to set aside at least 4,000 acres for conservation
purposes.
We are particularly grateful to the Lunenburg Memorial Fund for their part in making possible the purchase of
six picnic tables for use in the Ben Normand area, Cowdrey Nature Center and Clark's Hill Reservation. Their gift
also included an aluminum rowboat for use in the Mulpus Swamp, as well as the planting of forty-seven shrubs and
trees, to provide both food and shelter for animals, in the Clark's Hill Reservation.

Development & Industrial Commission
"Matters Of Factual Consideration"
The Development & Industrial Commission's activity during the past year was rather limited in scope, undoubtedly
affected by the poor economic conditions that prevailed in the area as evidenced by the business cut-backs in the
neighboring cities and the high unemployment levels.
When competing for new business, especially during bad times, one must realize how muchmore handicapped
the Town of Lunenburg is in relationship to the surrounding area. For example, we do not have an adequate water supply
or sewerage to support industry. Our only plentiful resource is land but even here we are disadvantaged since our
Town Protective By-laws do not include industrial zoning. In years past many inquiries were made concerning
plant sites, but when confronted with no industrial zoning, these companies located elsewhere.
This past year we did have an opportunity to improve our positic regarding water supply. However, the voters
of the Water District saw fit to vote down a proposal to erect a much needed standpipe. The Development & Industrial
Commission, working through the Overall Economic Development Program Committee for the Fitchburg-Leominster
Area, has made a request for federal funds so that water might be provided to the commercially zoned property on
Chase Road and on Leominster-Shirley Road.
Under the Public Works and Economic Development Act of 1965, an areamay request government help when an
area's unemployment level reaches a critical stage. Although the request is based upon future industrial use, obviously
the Town and individual homeowners would benefit should the water system be expanded. The Lunenburg Water District
Commissioners have assisted with and lent their support to this request.
The members of the Development & Industrial Commission feel some light industry is desirable and should be
included in the normal growth of the Town. The argument used to discourage industrial growth is that new business
creates new jobs, and new jobs mean new homes and families, and new homes and families mean increased school
population, which in turn means more taxes for everyone since the tax money gained from real estate may not be enough
to cover the increases to the school budget. This concept is basically sound, and industry if not properly planned for
could indeed adversely affect the tax rate. However, one fact remains crystal clear--if Lunenburg remains as it is,
the most desirable bedroom community in the area, then as our neighboring cities and towns develop their industry
we will acquire a disproportionate share of new homes and families without receiving a single tax dollar from
industry.
The Montachusett Regional Planning Commission in a recent report has made the following population projections
for the next ten years: Leominster up 17.3%, Lunenburg up 16.8%, Townsend up 16.5%, Westminster up 6.1%, and
Fitchburg and Shirley to drop slightly. This increase in Lunenburg represents 1,247 people and is slightly greater
than the 1,085 gain between 1960 and 1970. Already we can see a major housing development being planned for the
Pleasant Street area in the immediate future.
Other arguments focus on ecology. Some individuals feel industry is a noisy polluter and an eyesore If prop-
erly planned for and with today's rigid anti-pollution laws this is not the case. One need only comparea recently
built plant to those constructed several years ago to see the dramatic differences in layout and landscaping.
Industry per se is not a godsend. It cannot and will not significantly reduce taxes, but if properly planned
for, it can be a tremendous stabilizer and can become a decelerator for ever increasing taxes.
- 22
===PAGE 25===
For example, if one of our larger tracts of commercially zoned property were zoned industrial this in itself
should discourage construction of new homes in and around the area. Also, if planned properly, other large tracts of
land surrounding the property could be put into conservation so as to create a buffer between adjoining residential
areas and prevent the spread of industry into these more populated areas. Then, if water were tobe provided to this
site in sufficientquantity to just handle fire protection and limited process usage, we could have a fairly attractive
piece of real estate that could undoubtedly be developed as an industrial park for light industry. Through planning
and control of size, location and services, industrial development of this type can provide some form of tax relief.
The Development & Industrial Commission will put before the votersduring Town Meeting anarticle that is not
binding and asks for opinion regarding future industrial growth. The purpose of this article is strictly for guidance
not only to this Commission but to all elected and appointed officials of the Town of Lunenburg.
Should the thought of industrial growth, even though it maybe limited in scope, be repugnant to the voters of
Lunenburg, they must keep in mind the dilemma that faces them in the future. Taxes will continue to rise along with
new home construction. Ultimately we will be faced with the need for additional services that must accompany this
growth such as water, sewerage, fire and police protection, schools, roads and rubbish disposal, to name a few. If
industry is not to be included in this growth then alternate approaches must be found now if we are ever to control
the tax spiral.

During 1971 we conducted interviews with elected and appointed members of various Committees and Boards.
We discussed with them the operation of town government as viewed from their present positions with special emphasis
on their opinions of the strengths and weaknesses of the government as it presently exists in their own and other areas.
We also had the opportunity to read many articles and reference material concerning town government in general.
As a result of our studies and discussions, we submit the following recommendations:
1. Zoning By-Laws - The three areas of town business which are most time-consuming for the Selectmen are:
the Highway Department, Board of Health and administration of the zoning by-laws. We recommend that an
enforcement officer be appointed by the Selectmen to administer the zoning by-laws. He should be specifically
charged with reviewing and checking on all applications and permits issued for new buildings or alterations,
to make sure that the plans presented under the by-laws are adhered to. The Board of Assessors, the Board of
Health and the Planning Board must be kept appraised of all developments. Adequate remuneration for the time
involved should be offered. By relieving the Selectmen of this duty, we would expect stricter supervision over
the departments under their control. We would recommend that they spend more time in long-range plans for
the direction of town growth.

certainly resulted in a more orderly transaction of town business, as well as much saving of time for the Select-
men as individuals. We recommend that a Central Purchasing Division for all town departments be set upunder
his control for more economical and better supervised ordering and distribution of all necessary supplies. A
more thorough knowledge of all available State and Federal funds which might help the economy of the Town

"MRDA" Study Underway
The ban on open fires which became effective last year created a problem for Lunenburg as well as for most.
other towns in the Commonwealth. Our Municipal Refuse Disposal Area is destined to run out of space at an accel-

with Article 11 of the Annual Town Meeting on March 6, 1971 to solve this problem.

of this appropriation. We recommend that the Committee be continued for one more year.

Highway Department
Emphasis on Anti-Pollution Measures"
When one considers the investment that the American public has in highway facil-
ities and motor vehicles, obviously, it is imperative that they be of use to them during
any time of the year regardless of the weather. We cannot deny the motorist the use of
his vehicle or of the highway for even short periods of time because they are covered
with snow and ice.
To keep the roadways clear, we have placed a great deal of dependency on chem-
icals - both sodium chloride and calcium chloride. The use and demand of chemicals
has continued to grow year after year, and with environmental pollution being uppermost
in the minds of many Americans today, much criticism has been directed to the winter
maintenance men.
Because of this emphasis on anti-pollution measures, a report was prepared by the
Highway Research Board which defines the scope of the highway pollution problemre-
sulting from the use of highway road salts. This report states that not all salt in the en-
Richard W. Brown
vironment comes from that being applied to roads in the winter time. Approximately 35
Highway Superintendent

sea also pickup and deposit much chloride on the continental land mass near the oceans.
Other sources of chlorides which may cause pollution are surface runoff, industrial wastes and sewerage plants.
Chlorides in the environment are measured in parts per million (ppm). To get some idea of the relative size of one
part per million, one must think in terms of one inch in sixteen miles. Water containing as many as 2,000 ppm has
been used for human consumption without adverse effects on the human body.
There are several areas where highway road salts have been accused of creating pollution problems. Firstly,
fish have been killed in farm ponds and small lakes immediately adjacent to highways where salt has been used on
the highway. This is a legitimate problem which results from extremely high concentrations entering these bodies
without sufficient water from other sources to dilute the salt. It is claimed that deep wells supplying water for do-
mestic consumption have been polluted from highway road salts, even though the wells were located several hundred
yards from the highway. This claim is difficult to disprove and is usually untrue unless water-bearing shales or sand
lead directly from the roadway to the wells.
Most roadside vegetation can withstand concentrations of the chlorides up to 1, 200 ppm and a grass with a good
salt tolerance can resist chloride levels of up to 4,500 ppm. Some trees and bushes are sensitive to the presence of
even less than 2,000 ppmof salt, such as maples, certain poplars, larch, roses, multiflora rose and barberry. Trees
with a good tolerance for salt are the oaks, locusts and hawthornes, and will usually withstand up to 6,000 ppm of
salt in soil around them. The pollutant factor is not simply the amount of chloride present, but the amount of chem-
ical related to the amount of water present.
While there are several alternatives to the uses of chlorides on roads, none of them appear at this time to be
feasible, and we will have to depend upon the melting action of these chemicals for some time to come. The only
alternative to discontinuing the use of chlorides as de-icing agents would be to discontinue bare pavement maintenance
and allow snow to build up on the streets between each storm. However, the American public would not tolerate
a return to the conditions that prevailed 20 years ago. (Information in this report was adapted from paper present-
ed by R.N. Hughmanick at the 11th Annual North American Snow Conference.)
