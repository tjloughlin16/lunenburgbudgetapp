SECTION V-10. Off-Street Parking and Loading Areas.
A. Off-street parking and loading spaces shall be required as follows:
1. Dwellings: Two (2) parking spaces for each dwelling unit therein.
2. Place of assembly, including a funeral home: One (1) parking space for each three (3) seats therein or one (1) space
for each sixty (60) inches of bleachers or benches.
3. Schools: One (1) parking space for each classroom and office therein, plus one (1) parking space for each three (3)
seats in the auditorium thereof.
4. Hotels, motels and other places providing overnight accommodations: One (1) parking space for each room accom-
modation therein and adequate spaces for delivery vehicles.
5. Restaurants: One (1) parking space for each table or booth, and one (1) parking space for every two (2) counter
seats, and adequate spaces for delivery vehicles.
6. Other service and retail establishments: Parking areas, exclusive of driveways, shall be in a minimum ratio of
three (3) square feet of ground area to one (1) square foot of gross floor area, exclusive of storage areas, and adequate
loading spaces.
7. Offices and all other permitted non-residential structures, except agricultural: One (1) space for each one thousand
000) square feet of gross floor area, and loading space for all deliveries and shipping; however, the minimum parking
area shall be in a minimum ratio of one (1) square foot of ground area for each three (3) square feet of floor area.
B. Design of Off-Street Parking and Loading Spaces:
1. For all required off-street parking spaces, open or enclosed, each three hundred (300) square feet of net standing
and maneuvering area shall be considered one (1) space However, if such spaces are for a single or two family dwelling
or are located in a completely enclosed building, each two hundred and fifty (250) square feet of net standing and ma-
neuvering area shall be considered one (1) space. All required parking spaces shall be at least fifteen (15) feet from

SECTION I-4. DEFINITIONS
For the purposes of this by-law certain terms and words are herein defined as follows: Words used in the present tense include the
future; words in the singular number include the plural and words in the plural number include the singular; the word "shall" is
mandatory and not directory; the word "lot" includes the word "plot"; and the word "land" includes the words "marsh" and"water".
A. Building: A structure having a roof or cover for the shelter, housing or enclosure of persons, animals, or property.
B. Building Lot or Lot: A building lot is that area of land described on a site plan in an application for a building permit
or an application to the Board of Appeals for a permit or a variance, or otherwise defined as the area on which structure is to be
constructed or a use is to be conducted, and which is recorded by planor deed at the Worcester Northern District Registry of Deeds.
A building lot shall not include any part of a street. A corner lot shall be any lot abutting on two (2) or more streets at their
intersection.
C. Dwelling: Any building, or part thereof, used for habitation for one (1) or more persons, but not including commercial
accommodations for transient occupancy or trailers or mobile homes, however mounted.
D. Dwelling Unit: One (1) or more with cooking, living, sanitary and sleeping facilities arranged for the use of one
(1) or more persons living together as a single housekeeping unit, but not more than five (5) persons unrelatedby bloodor marriage.
E. Exterior Line: The dividing line between street and a lot and, in the case of a public way, the street line established
by the public authority laying out the way upon which the lot abuts.
F. Family: One (1) or more persons living together in one dwelling unit, but not including sororities, fraternities and other
communal arrangements.
G. Farm: A parcel of land ten (10) acres or more used for gain in the raising of agricultural products, livestock, poultry
and dairy products, or a parcel with a gross annual value of farm products raised in excess of four hundred dollars ($400.00) It
includes necessary farm structures and the storage of equipment used.
H. Frontage: The linear extent of a lot measured along street. The right-of-way from the intersection of one side lot to
the intersection of the other of the same lot.
1. Garage, Private: Coveredspace for the housing of motor vehicles, a part of or separate from a dwelling but not for the
rental of more than one (1) stall or for commercial repair or commercial storage.
J. Garage, Public: Any garage other than private garage, available to the public, operated by a public authority or for
gain, and which is used for storage, repair, rental, greasing, washing, servicing, adjusting or equipping of automobiles or other
motor vehicles, or supplying of gasoline or oil to motor vehicles or fuel to any kind of self-propelled vehicle.

"An Active Year"
The Lunenburg 4-H Clubs provided an active and stimulating year for the young people of our town. Many
individual and group activities were held.
Again this year the operating clubs were: "Mixed Dairy Club" led by Mrs. Yvonne Bourque, "The Kitchen
Kapers' 'Ied by Mrs. Barbara Raboin, "The Krazy Kitchen Kids led by Mrs. Ruth Anderson, "The Cookie Crumblers"
led by Mrs. Agnes Price, "The Pie Crust Kids" led by Mrs. Marcie Gauntlett and "The Bridle Path 4-H" led by Mrs.
Diane Moore and Mrs. Dixie Beck. The second half of this year showed a decline in three of these clubs.
The year began with individual club work. February brought on monthly bowling fun and our annual bus trip.
This year we went to see the U.S.S. Massachusetts Battleship and toured the Ann Dale Cookie Factory where 4-H
cookies are made. In March a Demonstration and exhibit night was held where area winners went on to county compet-
ition. The winners were Donna Camber, Ed Stockwell and Chris Currie. In April six leaders attended the Annual Leaders'
Banquet held at Camp Marshall. The Horse Club held a Dr. Wright Clinic, had mounted meetings, did barnchecks
and attended an all day clinic in Bolton.
A 4-H Family Picnic ended our official year but not our activities. We dug in and helped out on two clean-up
days. There really wasa lot of rubbish on our streets. The 2nd Annual 4-H Horse Show was held in October under
the direction of Mrs. Lois Camber and turned out to be a great success with Richard Bishop presenting the top 4-H
Awards to the high point winners. The Open Horse Show was held in October under the leadership of Mrs. Camber:
In July we were proud to represent Lunenburg in the Civic Day Parade. A float depicting the three active clubs in
town rode elegantly down Main Street in Fitchburg lowed by a group of horses proudly displaying the 4-H emblem and
colors. Our float took 3rd prize while three of the Horse Club members won trophies for their fine riding.
Approximately twenty-five members attended 4-H Camp this summer. Town camperships were awarded to: Deborah
Kokk, Carol Surrette, Paula Bourque, Patty Cutting and Karen Lindsay. Cookie Sale campership winners were: Donna
Camber, Ed Stockwell and Lisa Surrette. In September the newly elected officers took over. They are Mrs. Ruth
Anderson, Town Chairman; Mrs. Yvonne Bourque, Secretary; Mrs. Marcie Gauntlett, Treasurer. Trustees of the 4-H
funds are Richard L. Bishop and Raymond Proctor. Also in September we participated in the Grange Fair Horse Show
and dairy members exhibited in the dairy show. We also attended 4-H Day at the Eastern States Exposition. A Pot Luck
Supper washeld, slides were shown, and the trophies won at the parade were on display. The Dairy Club went on their
annual Dairy Tour. The Annual Awards Night was held in November with entertainment and exhibits. We ended
the year with Christmas Caroling.
This year saw 4-Hsign erected, on Massachusetts Avenue purchased with funds made available through Susie
Logan, the Worcester County Extension Agent. A new crafts club "The Creative Minds" with twelve members led by
Mrs. Dot Connell was organized. They have been busy making lovely gifts and household articles which they have
had on display. Mrs. Agnes Prices's Club "The Cookie Crumblers" have learned many forms of food preparation and

Activities began to gain momentum early in the year and culminated in the opening of the school on September
20, 1971. When it became obvious that the construction of the school could not keep to the original timetable, an
"Interim Plan" was developed that would make it possible to open the school within the limitations of the District's
agreement. Hence, an agreement with the City of Fitchburg permitted utilizing the Fitchburg Vocational School as
an "Annex" to the partially completed Technical School.
A cornerstone-laying ceremony on April 25, 1971 heralded the enrolling of students. Congressman Father
Drinan, State Representative Gerald Lombard, Representative George Burke, Monsignor Batista, Representatives of
Labor, our Advisory Committee, and the State Department of Education were among the dignitaries.
Of the 706 applications processed, 662 students were listed in the October Report to the State Department of
Education. This did not include 200 applicants who were placed on a waiting list. Among the tasks that were ac-
complished and necessary prior to the opening of school were staffing, writing a staff handbook, student handbook,
developing a school calendar, master schedule, personnel policy, student policy, equipping, ordering books and supplies,
developing a transportation system, and obtaining approval from the various regulatory agencies. The staff participated
in a 5-week training program, in cooperation with two other Regional Vocational Schools and co-sponsored by the
Network of Innovative Schools and the Department of Education. The workshop developed curriculum, teaching materials,
and teaching methodology.
The third Licensed Practical Nurse graduation was held on September 2, 1971 when 27 graduates received their
diplomas. This was the first class of the newly developed 12-month program as compared to the previous 15-month
program. The curriculum had been approved by the Board of Nurse Registry. The average score this year for our
students is 511 as opposed to 480 a year ago. Mrs. Patricia Traini was named the new Licensed Practical Nurse
Director. A new program, the Rehabilitation Assistant (Occupational, Physical and Speech Therapy Assistant) was
organized in cooperation with Rutland Heights Hospital under the direction of Miss Bessie Wilkin, R.N. In addition,
six Nurse Aide programs were conducted this year at Rutland Heights Hospital under the direction of Mrs. Rachel King.
As a result of the annual survey, nearly all of the LPN and Nurse Aide graduates are employed in hospitals and nursing
homes in the area. Our Advisory Committees continue to function in their appraisals of equipment, staff, and curriculum.
A Certificate of Occupancy was granted to Montachusett Regional for the first four units in September of 1971
and, for the remainder of the building, during the Christmas vacation holidays. The students from the Fitchburg
Vocational School were phased into the remainder of the building when it opened January 3, 1972.
At the regular School Committee Meeting of December 22, 1971, the Committee adopted an Operating and
Capital Budget of $2,858,338 for 1972. The District is entitled to receive 50% reimbursement of all net operating
expenses and 100% reimbursement for all student transportation under Chapter 74. In addition, the State contributes
toward the re-payment of the bond indebtedness for the District. This will continue until expiration of the indebtedness.
Also, P.L. 90-576 monies will be available for constructionand equipment, in addition to specific programs currently
in the school. Finally, member Towns are entitled to 15% reimbursement under Chapter 779 as members in a Regional
District. Such funds are on Chapter 70 entitlement and are directly sent to the Town Treasury.

NEW "WRINKLES"
Several innovations were introduced in Lunenburg Schools during 1971 in an attempt to refine, update,
and improve the instructional program. Many of these new thrusts were aimed at "what goes on in classrooms"
and reflect careful planning and evaluation by teachers, students, and administrators. We have purposely
avoided adopting the concept of "bandwagonism", i. e., "doing new things simply because other school systems
are doing them".
However, Lunenburg teachers and administrators must be exposed to new curriculum, new ideas, new
research, and must constantly seek self-improvement and renewal. It is also vitally important, if not more impor-
tant, to develop workable strategies for change in Lunenburg Schools which create an atmosphere of openness
among people and provide for careful experimentation. Conflict will never be totally reduced since teachers,
parents, and students have differing philosophies on just what education is or should be in the Lunenburg Public
Schools. Although sometimes painful, a certain degree of conflict is healthy and quite necessary if any genuine
progress is to be made. However, this conflict has to be manageable conflict or else Lunenburg schools run the
risk of moving backwards instead of forward. Helping maintain this balance, this perspective, is not only the job
of a Superintendent of Schools but is the responsibility of everyone associated with the school system.

pupils."
The times have drastically changed, today's students present different problems, and a vast knowledge ex-
plosion has affected US all; however, although the jargon is different, certain comparisons can still be made with
Miss Proctor of 1881. The results of our Parent Survey this past year prove this point--everyone respects teachers
who are committed to their profession and who sincerely like and have a true concern for their students. These
are definitely prerequisites for any good teacher. However, we also should be concerned about a teacher's skill
or "their methods of imparting instruction".
The teachers we hire and retain in Lunenburg must, therefore, possess both the teaching skills and compe-
tencies and the personal qualities that the Town Fathers were talking about when they described Miss Proctor in
1881. Fortunately, in Lunenburg, we have (and have had) many fine teachers who contribute to the outstanding
success and reputation Lunenburg Schools enjoy.
A SUMMARY OF SPACE UTILIZATION
The following is a brief report on how instructional space is being utilized in Lunenburg Schools:
ELEMENTARY SCHOOLS (K-4)
As of October 1, 1971, there were 822 students in Kindergarten through Grade 4. This compares with 833
in 1970, 849 in 1969 and a projected 790 (estimate) in 1972. In other words, enrollment in grades K-4 during the
past three years has decreased by 27 students and in 1972 is expected to decrease by another 32 students. Even
with this decrease, class size in elementary classes will average a desirable 24.5 (estimate) in 1972-73.
All 24 classrooms at the Passios School are being utilized--23 as classrooms and one as a library. All

rooms. As previously mentioned, hopefully, funds will be appropriated this year to continue the 5-year plan to
upgrade the Annex. This plan calls for an annual expenditure of $5000. - $7000. per year between 1971 - 1976.

MIDDLE SCHOOL (Grades 5-7) -

imately 28 in the Middle School next year and because of achievement grouping practices in basic skills areas,

SCHOOL COMMITTEE ACTIVITIES
The busy, sometimes hectic, pace set by the School Committee in 1970 continued and in fact, accelerated
in 1971. Considerable time was spent by school committee members in collective bargaining sessions with the
Lunenburg Teachers' Association and the AFL-CIO Cafeteria Union.
Negotiations with teachers continued right through Town Meeting last March and were not concluded
until after mediation and formal fact-finding last June. Because of a projected 30% tax increase and failure to
reach a salary settlement with teachers prior to Town Meeting, voters refused to approve additional budget monies
to cover a possible salary settlement. When a 4.8% salary increase was finally negotiated, the School Committee
found itself with over a $20,000. deficit in their salary budget account. Fortunately, the wage-freeze, the
receipt of additional federal funds, the hiring of eleven first year teachers to replace veteran teachers who left
the system and other economies enabled the School Committee to struggle through the year on the budget sum
appropriated by the Town in March.
Negotiations with the AFL-CIO over a six-month period resulted in the signing in November of a two-
year master contract with cafeteria workers. The contract provides for a salary reopener on March 31, 1972.
Cafeteria workers received a 5% salary increase for 1971 minus the period covered by the wage-freeze.
Currently, the School Committee is negotiating with the Lunenburg Teachers' Association with outside
negotiators serving as spokesman for each group. Both groups are desireous of reaching a salary settlement prior
to Town Meeting; however, collective bargaining is never a predictable business.

JEAN LARKIN and LOUIS FORGUES
After contributing to three very productive and important years in Lunenburg education, Mrs. Jean Larkin
and Mr. Louis Forgues are stepping aside.
Mrs. Larkin has given numerous hours of outstanding service to Lunenburg Schools. She initiated our
school volunteer program, worked with teacher committees in improving teacher lounge and student learning
areas, and has been active in Regional and State School Committee activities, most recently serving on a State
School Committee Task Force on Collective Bargaining. Mrs. Larkin has given of herself above and beyond the
normal duties of a school committee member and her energy and enthusiasm, concern, and deep commitment will
be truly missed.
Lou Forgues leaves the committee after having spent the last year of his term as Chairman. In his letter
announcing his decision not to seek re-election, Mr. Forgues referred to his years on the school committee as
being anything but routine. He referred to the experience as one that makes an individual "more intensely aware
of people, problems and rewards--so much so, that upon leaving, he will find himself a more humble, a more
understanding, and a more humane person. Lou Forgues seriously invested himself in the duties of his office and
carried out his responsibilities admirably.
Besides receiving, both he and Mrs. Larkin gave greatly to Lunenburg during their fenure on the School
Committee.
