10 days (2 calendar weeks) on the next succeeding June 1, and thereafter on each succeeding June 1, to 5 years of continuous
service. 2. Persons first employed during the months of August through May of any year shall on the next succeeding June 1 be
credited with one day for each full month of service completed, and thereafter on each succeeding June 1 to 5 years of continuous

Article 21. To see if the Town will vote to amend its By-laws by adding under Article V the following new Section to be
known as Section 12: Prohibiting Unnecessary, Excessive or Unusual Noises: A. It shall be unlawful for any person or persons
occupying or having charge of any building or premises or any part thereof in the Town, other than that section of any establishment
licensed under Chapter 138 of the General Laws to cause or suffer or allow any unnecessary, loud, excessive or unusual noises in
the operation of any radio, phonograph or other mechanical sound-making device or instrument, or reproducing device or instrument,
or in the playing of any band, orchestra, musician or group of musicians, or in the use of any device to amplify the aforesaid, or
the making of loud outcries, exclamations or other loud or boisterous noises or loud and boisterous singing by any person or group
of persons or in the use of any device to amplify the aforesaid noise where the noise is plainly audible at a distance of one hundred
and fifty feet from the building, structure, vehicle or premises in which or from which it is produced. The fact that the noise is
plainly audible at a distance of one hundred and fifty feet from the vehicle or premises from which it originates shall constitute
prima facie evidence of a violation of this by-law. Any person violating this by-law shall be punished by a fine of not more than
fifty dollars for each offense. B. It shall be unlawful for any person or persons being present in or about any building, dwelling,
premises, shelter, boat or conveyance or any part thereof, other than that section of any establishment licensed under Chapter 138
of the General Laws, who shall cause or suffer or countenance any loud, unnecessary, excessive or unusual noises, including any
loud, unnecessary, excessive or unusual noises in the operation of any radio, phonograph or other mechanical sound-making device,
or instrument, or reproducing device or instrument, or in the playing of any band, orchestra, musician or group of musicians, or
the making of loud outcries, exclamations or other loud or boisterous noises or loud and boisterous singing by any person or group
of persons, or in the use of any device to amplify the aforesaid noise, where the aforesaid noise is plainly audible at distance of
one hundred and fifty feet from the dwelling, building, premises, shelter, boat or conveyance in which or from which it is produced.
The fact that the noise is plainly audible at distance of one hundred and fifty feet from the premises fromwhich it originates shall
constitute prima facie evidence of a violation of this by-law. Any person shall be deemed in violation of this by-law, who shall
make, or aid, or cause, or suffer, or countenance, or assist in the making of the aforesaid and described improper noise, disturbance,
breach of the peace or a diversion tending to a breach of the peace, and the presence of any person or persons in or about the
building, dwelling, premises, shelter, boat or conveyance or any part thereof during a violation of this by-law shall constitute
prima facie evidence that they are a countenancer to such violation. Any person violating this by-law shall be punished by a fine
of not more than fifty dollars for each offense, or act anything thereon. VOTED UNANIMOUSLY TO PASS OVER ARTICLE 21.

A CAPSULE REVIEW - LUNENBURG SCHOOLS, 1973;
Under new State legislation, the Lunenburg School Committee began 1973 with the complex task
of formulating an 18-month transitional School Budget (January 1973 to June 30,1974) as all towns
and cities in the Commonwealth moved from a calendar fiscal budget basis to a July 1st to June 30th
fiscal year. This conversion presented unique problems for the school department since costs for
one and two-thirds school years (e. g. two winters) tended to inflate budget totals. Also, because
of State laws, two additional months of teacher summer salaries had to be included in the 18-month
budget figures, a factor which increased the salary budget by an additional $210,000.
Because of these costs factors, the Lunenburg School Committee adopted austerity measures and
made significant budget cuts in the athletic, library, periodical, capital outlay, staff travel and
conference accounts, Nevertheless, a budget request of $3,569,306. was presented and approved by
the voters at the March Town Meeting, As in the recent past, fixed school costs in the areas of
negotiated teacher and non-teaching salaries, transportation, fuel and utilities accounts represen-
ted 90% of the budget appropriation--factors making it very difficult for the School Committee to
make substantial budget cuts by eliminating or curtailing other school expenses.
Also in March, Roger Raboin was elected as the new Chairman of the School Committee and Howard
Brock was re-elected to the committee for a fifth consecutive term. In June, Mrs. Marjorie Still-
man resigned as a school committee member in order to apply for the position of School Lunch Super-
visor. The School Committee and Selectmen in joint session then appointed Mrs. Jean Larkin, an ex-
school committee member, to Mrs. Stillman's position on the committee. At this writing, John Davis
has decided to seek re-election to a second term on the committee, however, Mrs. Larkin and Walter
Keeler, a past chairman and committee member for the last nine years, have decided not to seek re-
election. Both Mrs. Larkin and Mr. Keeler have given innumerable hours of outstanding and dedica-
ted service to the Town of Lunenburg and typify the fine quality of individual who has consistently
served on Lunenburg School Committees over the years. Mr. Keeler, in fact, has also served as the

OCCUPATIONAL EDUCATION AND UPGRADING FACILITIES AT LUNENBURG HIGH SCHOOL:
Each year, major goals are set by the Superintendent and his management team. These goals
and objectives apply to the school system as a whole as well as to individual schools and depart-
-78-
===PAGE 81===
ments within the system. Principals and other key administrative personnel are held accountable
for specific objectives and are asked to submit written evaluations on goal progress at the end of
the school year. Many times specific goals might span one or two school years. One such goal
concerned updating High School facilities and providing for an expanded occupational education
program.
During this past year high school administrators and department chairmen were asked to under-
take a serious study of their space needs and reduce to writing their findings and recommendations.
From this data, I determined priorities and submitted a proposal to the School Committee for their
consideration. The proposal called for an expenditure of $39,513. over the next five years to
renovate classroom areas in science, industrial arts, the library, and in physical education. Also
included within the cost figures was the purchase of needed instructional equipment in these reno-
vated areas. All in all, four new classrooms would be created and equipped under this five-year
renovation plan with federal funds responsible for the creation of two of these new classrooms.
The big question asked by taxpayers is "why"? Why this capital expenditure at a time when
school budgets and property taxes are already spiraling upward at an accelerated pace? Although
it is difficult to summarize a 40-page proposal in a paragraph or two, a few of the major reasons
and determined needs for this renovation program are as follows:
(1) Because of our enrollment bulge, High School enrollment will increase by over 100 stu-
dents in the next five years. This projection is based on actual (current) enrollment
statistics. We are currently turning students away from the library and industrial
arts courses and are using hallways and storage closets in physical education classes.
This situation will definitely worsen in the future and may become critical if the
community should suddenly experience any new population growth.
(2) Because the High School is 18 years old, changes in curriculum and new State mandates
(e. g. new regulations concerning libraries, special education and physical education)
during the past several years have outstripped available facilities. For example, all
science courses now utilize lab areas, however, we have 19 class periods per week
which are now taught out of a lab setting. Because of the significant changes in
science instruction during the past two decades there is a desperate need to create
additional science stations and update our current science classrooms and equipment.
(3) The New England Association of Schools and Colleges granted Lunenburg High School a 10
year accreditation in 1969 but in its recommendations reflected serious concerns with
the lack of space and facilities in Science, Physical Education, Industrial Arts, and
the Library. If Lunenburg High is to continue to keep its accreditation status and
remain on par with the demands of public secondary education in the 1970's, 1980's and
beyond, it must act now to remediate these deficiencies in facilities.
(4) With the receipt of nearly $70,000. in federal funds for a Graphic Arts program, we
now have an opportunity to expand our Industrial Arts program and offer occupational
education courses in seven (7) major areas of industry--plastics, wood, metal, power
mechanics (small engines), technical drawing, electronics, and graphics. Coupled with
our developing Work/Study program (which now includes 40 students), business education
and home economics courses, tutoring and career development opportunities, and the
Middle School career awareness program, Lunenburg High School can really begin to call
itself a comprehensive high school meeting the needs of all its students and not just
the college-bound.
It is this last point that needs further elaboration. People become confused when they hear
that Lunenburg High School is offering occupational courses in graphic arts, metals, electronics,
plastics, etc. They feel that Monty Tech should be providing programs of this nature for our stu-
dents and fear that Lunenburg High may be duplicating programs already available at Monty Tech.
Further confusion exists when it is learned that Lunenburg is 30% below its enrollment quota at
Monty Tech. Why then, the taxpayer asks, must we offer occupational education courses at Lunenburg
High?
The answer is quite simple. Firstly, Lunenburg has a student quota of about 10% at Monty
Tech. This year, for example, our undergraduate quota as of October 1st was 94 students and we
sent (and were assessed for) 63 students. This is a problem area we are actively working on with
Monty Tech and hope to rectify in the near future. However, even if we were at full quota, we
would still have to provide for over 800 students at Lunenburg High School of which less than 50%
-79-
===PAGE 82===
go on to four or two-year colleges. For example, in the 1973 graduating class, 47% of the seniors
went to either four-year or :wo-year colleges, 6% went into nurses training or other post-secon-
dary training, and 47% went to work. This trend, which reflects a greater number of students
going to work rather than to college, is not unique to Lunenburg but reflects a general societal
pattern. What we are trying to do then by developing occupational education courses at Lunenburg
High School is to better prepare those 47% for the world of work. Presently, we feel we are doing
a credible job preparing students academically and don't deny the extreme importance of teaching
communication, computational, and other basic skills and attitudes; however, little has been done
in the past to expose students to a variety of occupations and to specific skill training on at
least a job-entry basis.
Secondly, occupational education at Lunenburg High School cannot be equated with the voca-
tional training at Monty Tech. The majority of students at Monty Tech spend one-half day for four
years in a superbly equipped shop learning their specific trade or skill. They get in-depth skill
training in a trade. Occupational education courses at Lunenburg High make no such claim. For
example, eventually, it is planned to cycle all 8th graders and some 9th graders through short
orientation courses in all areas of occupation education available at Lunenburg High. Hopefully,
this exploration will encourage many of these youngsters to transfer to Monty Tech for more exten-
sive schooling. As 10th and 11th graders, Lunenburg's non-college students will spend approxi-
mately 10 hours per week concentrating on two occupational areas and hopefully as seniors they
will have narrowed their choice down to one occupational area where they are concentrating their
study and skill development. We have a Career Education Committee which is currently developing
a curriculum for occupational education at Lunenburg High and it is realized that it will take a
few years before the High School occupational education curriculum is fully implemented. The
need, however, to begin the expansion, renovation and equipping of our Industrial Arts area is
therefore pressing. The receipt of our graphic arts grant could not have come at a more appropri-
ate time.

SUPPORTIVE TUTORIAL PROGRAMS:
A. LEARNING DISABILITIES PROGRAM
Some students are just not ready or able to learn efficiently for their age and ability level.
Many of these students have problems in seeing and interpreting schoolwork and other visual mater-
ials. Some cannot really hear and distinguish verbal instructions from other kinds of "noise".
Others have not developed their muscle skills and coordination to the point where they can write
well, manipulate school materials, or even begin to coordinate their muscles for reading.
These types of learning problems are handled by the learning disabilities program. After a stu-
dent's particular learning problem is diagnosed,a sequence of activities is developed to improve on
the needed skills. Sometimes the student and classroom teacher learn to develop alternate ways to
get around the particular problem. Many high school students work under supervision with children
having the less severe problems, much to everyone's benefit.

good academic skills. Because of this poor skill development and often inappropriate behavior they
become increasingly frustrated by school and frustrate the school in its efforts to teach them. For

REPORT OF MIDDLE SCHOOL PRINCIPAL
FLOYD F. BARNEY
ROBERT V. ANTONUCCI

BS, ME, M+15
The Turkey Hill Middle School became a reality in 1958 although we had to resort to double
sessions with the T. C. Passios Elementary School and share their facilities with them until March
of 1969.
The Middle School is comprised of grades 5, 6 and 7 and although our enrollment fluctuates
during the course of the year, the enrollment has stabilized around the 575 figure.
Since its inception, the Middle School has made some changes which we think have made school a
more enjoyable experience for the students and yet we have never lost sight of our main obligation,
"to meet the educational needs of each child to the best of our ability".
Middle School children in Lunenburg usually range in age from 10 to 12 years old. As all par-
ents and teachers know, this is a difficult age. They are no longer little children and not quite
adolescents. Because of this, we are constantly faced with the question, "how can we best serve
the educational needs of this age group?" Although the primary responsibility of a school is to
-88-
===PAGE 91===
teach the three R's, we also feel it is our duty to expose the students to as many facets of living
as possible. Hence, we have included in our curriculum many things that we hope will have a direct
influence on their social and personal lives and better equip them for adolescence and adulthood.
It is impossible in a limited report to go into great detail on every phase of our program,
but I would like to outline some of the things currently going on at the Middle School. Some of
the programs are new which we hope will be of benefit to our students.
DATA BANK AND SCIS:
Under the direction of Mr. Antonucci, the Assistant Principal, a new approach to the teaching
of Science and Social Studies was introduced this fall on the fifth and sixth grade level. Work-
shops were conducted by Mr. Antonucci and other staff members during the summer for teachers who
would be involved with the teaching of these new disciplines. Both of these subjects are now based
on an "inquiry approach" where a student is taught to investigate certain problems and make his own
conclusions. Instead of being "told" how something occurs the students learn by "doing". Elemen-
tary Science is no longer simply another reading lesson but now has a greater laboratory/activity
focus.
SCIS is an abbreviation for Science Curriculum Improvement Study which is an elementary
science program for Kindergarten through Grade 6. This program is currently being introduced in
our 5th and 6th grades and has been enthusiastically received by students and teachers.
Data Bank is a Social Studies program which again is for all elementary school grades. The
components of the kit, which are known as Data Banks, contain all the necessary material a student
needs for any given lesson. The fifth grade program is based around United States History while
Technology is the sixth grade theme.
OPEN CLASSROOM:
Under the direction of Mr. Bishop, Mrs. Crisci, and Mr. Therrien, a new approach to teaching
was piloted in grade six this year. It is called the open concept or team teaching approach. These
three teachers plus a student teacher were assigned 90 students and are responsible for all the
disciplines taught. The key to this team teaching approach is flexibility. They are not locked
into a bell schedule and can devote more time to a student, or a group of students whom they feel
need more help in any given subject.

The 1973-74 school year opened with an enrollment of 804 at the Elementary School--identical
to that of the previous year. Predictions have indicated that elementary enrollments would
decrease but this has not materialized. In looking back six years when the public kindergarten
was opened in Lunenburg, the enrollment was 132. Our present kindergarten is 129. Class sizes are
above the desired average of twenty-five per class. With the exception of a transitional class at
the second, third and fourth grade level, which average 17 students apiece, our present average
size in other classrooms is: first grade, twenty-seven; second grade, twenty-nine; third grade,
twenty-seven; and fourth grade, twenty-nine.
In spite of class sizes we have done much in the curriculum area this past year and introduced
new programs. Some of the highlights in the curriculum area follows:
MATHEMATICS:
A pilot program in mathematics is being carried on this year in two second, three third, and
three fourth grade classes. The program is called IMS (Individualized Mathematics System). This
program provides maximum creativity and flexibility for teachers and pupils, and does not penalize
pupils if they are not proficient in reading. It covers ten topics: numeration, addition, sub-
traction, multiplication, division, fractions, mixed operations, money, time and measurement.
Within each topic, there is a carefully arranged sequence of skills to be learned. The skills are
organized into nine levels of difficulty, and each skill or "behavioral objective", is incorporated
into a separate skill folder. There are 376 skills in the IMS Curriculum. The skill folders pro-
vide for various types of learning styles, from manipulation of concrete materials to increasingly
difficult perceptual and abstract styles, Written directions, especially in the lower levels, are
minimal so pupils with limited reading ability can successfully use the materials.
To date, nearly all students and teachers have expressed enthusiasm for the program. The
program will be evaluated at the close of the school year to determine if it should be expanded
for the coming year.
SOCIAL STUDIES:
A new system of Social Studies called Databank was introduced into two fourth grade classes
this year. At this particular grade level the topic for study is "Inquiring About Culture". Two
areas of concern in social studies textbooks have always been "motivation" and "reading problems".

MRS. LUCY W. CHARTRAND
