Board of Selectmen
Board of Selectmen: left to right - Rudolph Masella, Chairman Normand J. Thompson and Sherwood G. Winchester.
In the year 1973 Lunenburg faced the "transitional year" -- the year when all communities in the Common-
wealth of Massachusetts converted from the calendar year to the July 1st fiscal year. All departments entered the
year with some trepidation, as they were concerned about accurately forecasting their budget needs for an eighteen
month period rather than the twelve month period to which they were well accustomed.
Each and every one of these departments should be commended as the only problems which have been encoun-
tered in regards to staying within budget appropriations were beyond the control or foresight of anyone in local gov-
ernment. Who could have predicted the energy crisis with any degree of accuracy? Who could have predicted that
the State House would do a "flip-flop" on its approach to highway allocations? Many area towns experienced fin-
ancial problems which required special town meetings to bring about solutions. Lunenburg recently had one which
will not affect our future tax rate.
The year 1973 was also the year of the energy crisis which required US to cut back as much as possible on all of
our energy usage. All Town Departments have worked hard to assure that they did not use more than was absolutely
necessary, and they have done an admirable job. The Board of Selectmen have decided to hold the addition of any
further street lighting to an absolute minimum due to the present energy crisis.
During 1973 over $20,389.00 was provided under the Federal Emergency Employment Act of 1971 for personnel
employed by the Town. For the period October 1971 through December 31, 1973, this has amounted to over $50,431.00
that the Town of Lunenburg has received for personnel employed under this Act.

The Board of Selectmen acting in its various capacities held thirty-six regularly scheduled meetings throughout
the year and approved expenditures amounting to $6,602,333.52. As the Local Licensing Authority ten hearings were
held during the year. These hearings were held relative to applications submitted for renewal of licenses, and ap-
plications for issuance of initial licenses for the sale of various types of alcoholic beverages and also applications for
licenses to store inflammables.
The Highway Department, under the supervision of Superintendent George G. Munyon, Jr., continued to main-
tain our streets. Many new procedures have been instituted which will save money, and at the same time keep serv-
ices as they were or will improve them. Not the least of these changes is the usage of two-thirds less salt than was
previously used in our Snow Removal Program. This should have a decidedly good effect on our ecology.
The usage of our Rescue Unit continues to be of ever-increasing value to townspeople. Under the capable su-
pervision of Fire Chief, James P. Deming, there were 142 calls made during the year with 143 people being trans-
ported.
In 1973 the Town of Lunenburg as it has in previous years received the Massachusetts Selectmen's Association
Award for our 1972 Annual Town Report. This Report was compiled under the direction of Mr. Richard J. Eley with
the help of many other people all of whom are to be congratulated for their fine work.
The Board wishes to express its appreciation to the citizens of Lunenburg for their cooperation and understanding
throughout the year. We consider it a privilege and honor to serve as Selectmen in a Town such as Lunenburg.
Board of Assessors
The Board of Assessorshad a very active year in preparation for the new fiscal year cycle. All new and known
construction has been assessed so that the Town can collect all taxes that are due. There will be two tax bills in
1974--one for the six month period January 1, 1974 through June 30, 1974 and another for Fiscal Year 1975 which
is from July 1, 1974 through June 30, 1975. The latter bill will be payable in two installments.
The Assessors have again attempted to maintain the program established by revaluation in 1970. This was done
by continually reviewing properties, maintaining sales records and holding extra meetings. The Assessors have found
that the inflationary trend has most certainly affected the value of real estate. The increased cost of building supplies
has also affected this value.
New construction has increased the total valuation of Real Estate to $51,014,700 for 1973 as compared to
$49,847,000 for 1972. Personal Property valuations amounted to $2,712,180, an increase from the $2,180,490 in
1972. These increases give a total valuation for the Town of $53,726,880. In 1973 the tax rate was increased again
from $46.00 to $52.00 as a result of increased expenditures and the eighteen month budget. The Assessors hope that
the six month budget will show a reduction because of fixed expenditures voted at the 1973 Annual Town Meeting.
Mrs. Joan Souliere was hired to do the clerical work for this department to fill the vacancy created by the
resignation of Mrs. Donna LeCuyer. Board members again attended school as required by the Department of Corporations
& Taxation with the school being held this year at Fitchburg State College. The Board wishes to remind taxpayers
that certain exemptions have been enacted for the value of residential property, not exceeding specified amounts,
owned and occupied as a domicile by persons of various classes for whom the payment of taxes could constitute an
undue burden. If a taxpayer feels that they qualify for any of the following exemptions, they should contact the
Assessors' Office and request an application which lists all the requirements that must be met in order to qualify.
Section 17 - Widows, minors whose fathers are deceased, and persons over 70 - $2,000 in valuation or a tax of $175,
whichever is greater.
Section 22 - Disabled or wounded veterans, or their wives, widows or parents - $2,000 to $10,000 or a tax from
$175 to $875, whichever is greater, depending on degree of disability.
Section 37 - Blind persons - $5,000 valuation or a tax of $437.50, whichever is greater.
Section 41 - Persons aged 70 or over - $4,000 valuation or a tax of $350, whichever is greater.
Section 42 - Unmarried widows of police officers or firefighters killed in the line of duty - $8,000 valuation or a
tax of $700, whichever is greater.
Section 43 - Minor orphans of police officers or firefighters killed in the line of duty - $8,000 in valuation or a
tax of $700, whichever is greater.

Park Commission
The highlights of the 1973 Park Commission activities were the expanded Tennis Program; the use of the Town
Beach facilities; Girls' Softball; and the new refreshment stand at the Marshall Park Little League Field.
Our Tennis Program, under the leadership of Dave Johnston, realized an all time high in attendance including
a large number of adults taking tennis instructions. Our Town Beach, under Jerry Beloin's guidance, was utilized
more this year than any year since the second year of operation. The addition of the new raft proved to be very popu-
lar with the residents. The hot summer, the new raft and the gasoline shortage had much to do in the greater utili-
zation of this facility. Our life saving and swimming programs under the able direction of June Riddle were a great
success again and assures US of continued qualified applicants for lifeguards for the beach. These programs are con-
ducted in conjunction with the Lunenburg Red Cross.
The Lunenburg Girls' Softball Team, under the tutilage of Chuck Winneberg, had a very successful season.
This program was a lost cause two years ago, but thanks to Chuck's enthusiasm and organization, Lunenburg enlisted
into a girls' league in the North County this summer. The future of this program appears bright. Thanks to our Lu-
nenburg Little League Organization, we now have a refreshment stand at the Marshall Park Little League Field.
This project required many free hours and money from many Lunenburg residents. The end resultwas a structure dedi-
cated in memory of Mrs. T. K. Ware which will be utilized as an officials' stand and will help defray expenses of
Lunenburg Little League Baseball.
The Park Commission supports many other programs which include Winter Tennis, Junior Basketball, Teen-Age
(non-team playing) Basketball, Young Men's Basketball, Open Gym for all residents, Adult Physical Education,
Skating at Marshall Pond (no lights this year), Junior Hockey, AAU Track Meets, Men's Golf, Couples' Scotch
Foursome Golf League, Men's Softball, Summer Teen-Age and Junior Basketball, Street Hockey (not successful),
Babe Ruth Baseball, Pop Warner Football, Summer Morning Baseball, Arts and Crafts, and playground activities which
included trips to Benson's Animal Farm, Willard Brook State Park and many side trips in town. Other Playground
activities included intra-playground competition, a picnic at Normand Park, swimming at the Town Beach and bar-
beques. The Playground Supervisors, in conjunction with the Arts & Crafts Supervisors, conducted another very suc-
cessful Muscular Dystrophy Fair this year.
This year for the first time Marshall Park was utilized by the Lunenburg High School Girls' Field Hockey Team.
Fitchburg State Baseball Team and the Fitchburg Center for Brain Damaged Children also utilized Marshall Park.
Many thanks to the taxpayers of Lunenburg as the Park Commission now has the storage space and the tools it needs
to get the job done without depending on other departments. We feel that this has saved you many tax dollars. Our
parks are basically in good repair under the capable supervision of Bob Garneau. Wallis Park has been repaired and
sealed, the Marshall Park driveway has been oiled, and the fence at Wallis Park has been repaired as has the backstop
at the Marshall Park Baseball Field.
Our plans for 1974 are to remove the condemned Quonset Hut at Wallis Park and erect a new building in its
place, paint the fence at Wallis Park and backstop at the Marshall Park Baseball Field and install cement slabs for
the Marshall Park Baseball Field. The Park Commission also plans to change the location of the fence on Chestnut
Street at Marshall Park for more safe parking, complete the dugouts and erect a scoreboard at Marshall Park Base-
ball Field, and erect a fence down the left field line and clear out the undergrowth at Lesure Field. The future of
Lunenburg's recreation includes more softball and little league fields, more lighted tennis courts, the possible erec-
tion of a hockey rink and a recreational center. These ideas at this time are strictly ideas, as extra cost to the tax-
payers under present circumstances, we feel is too much to ask. Lunenburg will eventually need a Recreational Di-
rector who can devote his full time to the Park Commission before we can accomplish these long range goals.

It hasbeen predicted that in the next decade more than half of the growth in
U.S. population will come in the 20-34 year-old group, the age bracket in which people start earning good income,
get married, set up housekeeping, start families and spend in a big way. The needs, preferences and habits of this
group will demand priority in all economic and social decisions made in the years ahead, and their life style, including
attitudes towards child-bearing, will exert the greatest influence on our community, as it will on society as a whole.
Therefore, in a community such as ours, creation of new households is forecast to accelerate for most of the next
decade - both from newly married couples and the growing number of single persons who prefer to maintain separate
residences. Even before the energy crisis, a preference among this age group for small cars and motorcycles washaving
a marked effect on the automobile industry, and this preference is sure to influence road-building patterns and traffic-
control procedures. More adult-type recreational facilities will also need to be developed. Elected officials will
have to give increasing weight to the viewpoint of these new voters.
The second fastest growing group in the next decade will be the 35-49 year-old group. By 1984 their numbers
are expected to increase 19%, a reversal of the pattern in the last 10 years, when the population between 35 and 49
dropped. These are the people who, climbing up the income ladder, move into bigger homes, become multi-car fam-
ilies, and in general upgrade living standards. This pattern of more expensive homes may well provide some measure
of relief for the average taxpayer. For example: in our Town today a $25,000 new home will engender an annual
property tax of $1, 170. If the family building the home has two children, the net cost to the Town for their schooling
will be $1,986, and it will cost approximately $335 for all other municipal services - a total of $2,321, or $1,151
more than will be received in taxes. This means that if, at present cost levels, 100 $25,000 homes are built, each
housing two school children, it will cost the existing taxpayer an estimated $40 more on his annual tax bill. On the
other hand, a $60,000 new home with two children will generate approximately $2,808 in taxes, and will create a
surplus of $487 over the $2,321 cost figure. Therefore, 100 $60,000 homes would reduce the tax bill of the existing
taxpayer by something more than $16 per year.
Whereas it is predicted that the number in the 50-64 year-old group, that with the highest average income,
will grow slowly and then diminish in numbers, the number of Americans 65 and older is forecast to rise 17% in 10
years. These people have finished raising their children and helped finance an additional generation of children, yet
they are still paying an average of 20-25% of their retirement income in local property taxes. Perhaps withmore num-
bers and a larger voice they will be able to spearhead a drive which would give them some relief from the property
tax which in many cases is driving them out of their homes, and stir the collective conscience of the rest of US to a
new recognition of their plight. The number of people under 20 is projected to drop 3% by 1984. This will mean
temporary drop-offs in school enrollment and could mean a break for taxpayers after years of rising education costs.

It is predicted that during the next decade the American way of life will be more limited in material ways
but perhaps more beneficial in tempo of living, human relationships and basic values. A resurgence of conservative
values could have a profound effect on the manner in which our Town grows. Few are willing to forecast what pol-
itical mixture will result from the mingling of conservatism with youthful emphasis on liberation, but it is evident
that confidence in all major institutions is down sharply, and that the new generation will no longer tolerate a
"Father Knows Best" attitude on the part of public officials, but will demand, as they should, to know the whys and
wherefores of the decisions that affect their daily lives.

Planning Board
This past year a new member, James Mann, replaced William Mason who served his Town for ten years on the
Planning Board. Another member, Harold Harley, served his twenty-sixth year on the Board.
A Look at This Past Year's History
First, the March Town Meeting - Following much study and controversy, the Planning Board saw the Town vote
for a new stream setback and a new zoning map which introduced a new "watershed" zone, Residence B. The Board
also saw the Town Meeting reject the proposed increase in lot sizes and the elimination of two family housing, both
items which were designed to conserve natural resources and to stabilize on a long range basis both development and
taxes.
Subdivisions - This year the Board has been working on five subdivisions. One new subdivision, "Whalom Acres"
was approved. It is in Whalom and contains 15 lots, all of approximately 1/2 acre size. Application for one new sub-
division proposed by John Lampi, containing six lots on Reservoir Road, was dropped after submission of plans.
Another subdivision containing 198, ½ acre lots, called "Heritage Hill", is still in the process of being ap-
proved after submission in 1972. This subdivision is on West and Pleasant Streets on the old "Arnold" Farm. The

Tree Warden
Few roadside problems cause as much disappointment and frustration to mu-
nicipal and highway departments as Dutch Elm disease which has, up until now,
resisted control for more than a half a century. Carcasses of trees left in the
wake of the arboreal plague can be seen in urban and rural areas across the
nation - ugly monuments to one of nature's more irresistable forces. One of the
newest chemical deterents to Dutch Elm disease is Benlate fungicide made by the
DuPont Company. Basically, the objective is to prevent disease-causing micro-
scopic fungus from entering the sapstream of the elm. Once the fungus is estab-
lished there, tree tissues become plugged and the infected tree invariably dies.
The fungus moves from tree to tree in two ways - by elm bark beetle and
through root grafts that tie adjoining elms together. Over the years we placed
emphasis on methods of keeping the beetle population under control, and as a
result may have developed the mistaken notion it is a beetle-caused disease. An
individual beetle carries one million or more fungal spores that cause the disease.
More than a million beetles can emerge from dead elms left standing which
Richard W. Brown, Tree Warden
served as beetle brood-wood. Emerging from the wood, they begin feeding on
nearby healthy elms where those spores rub off their bodies and become established
in the new host. After feeding, the beetles seekout diseased, dead or weakened elm trees to serve as brood-wood.
A second brood of beetles emerges later in the season and the cycle is repeated. Fortunately, usually are more
resistant to infection by August. Timely removal of beetle brood-wood before the beetles emerge in the spring or
summer is vital to the success of any control program.
The second type of spread is through the root systems connecting diseased and healthy elms. The simplest and
most effective method in reducing losses is to mechanically sever the roots. Benlate (technical name Benomyl) po-
ssesses two important features. 1. It is systemic and moves internally through the tree. 2. It is active against the
DED fungus at very low concentrations without damaging the elm and with no toxicity to plant and animal life.
Infections from root grafts or late season beetle feeding do not respond to treatment preventively or thera-
peutically. Apparently the fungus in infected trees becomes too firmly established in the root system where it cannot
be reached by the chemical. We do not know whether Benlate will work against native bark beetle transmissions
because it has not been tested adequately against this possibility. New formulations of the chemical and other
methods of soil and trunk injections are in advanced stages of testing whether the success rate can be increased, and
whether the stubborn "root graft" and "carry-over" trees can be made to respond.
(Above are excerpts from article by Dr. Gayle L. Worf, Department of Plant Pathology, University of Wisconsin.)

Library Trustees
One of the most important things to happen to the library this year was a start on revitalizing itsback storage
room for public use. This year the "Friends of the Library" group raised almost $700 through bake sales, craftsales,
book sales, a very successful rummage sale on the front lawn of the library, and through membership fees. The
"Friends" have decided to spend this money to make the former storage room into a room which will be available
for groups to meet in, for craft demonstrations and art exhibits and for the Children's Story Hour. This room can be
closed off by doors from the rest of the library so groups meeting there will not disturb others wishing to read. Since
there is no medium-sized free public meeting room in Lunenburg, it is hoped by the library staff and Trustees that
such a room will be useful to the Town.
Asummer reading program for children in Grades 3 through 6 was conducted in July and August. The program
started off with a showing of the film of the children's classic "The Secret Garden". Children were asked to try to
read at least fifteen books during the six weeks of the program. Thirty children read the required number of books
and many read a great many more. In all, the children read 690 books during the six weeks period. Upon comple-
tion of the program, the library held a party for the readers and one of the Trustees, Mrs. Janet Cragin, presented
each child with a paperback book. Mrs. Emily Foster was in charge of this summer reading program.
In the second year of running an adult film program on Sunday nights, the library receiveda grant of $825 from
the Massachusetts Council of the Arts & Humanities. This money was used to pay film rental costs which are not
covered in the annual budget. Twenty-four films were shown including a six film festival of Swedish movie direc-
tor, Ingmar Bergman's work. The third year of the film program started this fall. A Film Society made up of people
interested in helping the program survive both financially and spiritually was formed in November by Lunenburg resi-
dents who like coming to the movies at the library. Membership is open to all.
The pre-school Story Hour was so pupular last year that this year Emily Foster and Gloria O'Brien divided the
Story Hour into two sessions--one at 10:00 and one at 11:00 on Friday mornings. Coffee and doughnuts are served
in the reading room to the mothers while the Story Hour is in session.
In 1973, 23,821 children's books, 16,803 adult books, 1,030 magazines and 500 records were circulated. One
hundred and twenty-six inter-library loan requests were filled and 228 films were lent. The following people gave
books to the library: Dr. Thomas Mulholland, Mrs. Donald Cabot, Mrs. Gloria Karlson, Mr. Tozer, Mrs. Ruth
Proctor and the Lunenburg League of Women Voters. The Memorial Fund gave $50.00 for children's books which
was spent on a new set of Thornton Burgess books.

Highway Department
Don't sell highways short in Energy Crisis!
Suddenly the unthinkable has happened and a
nation geared to the automobile and truck faces cur-
tailment in highway transportation. Officials and
engineers in the public highway agencies need to
keep their perspective in this time of emotional stress
over fuel shortages. The situation will tend to widen
the gap between urban and rural highway and trans-
portation planning, which will not be in the public
interest.
What ahead? For one thing you can count
on the continued wearing out of existing highways
from traffic and elements. Any slowdown in con-
struction, reconstruction and resurfacing that might
result from this crisis must be followed by a feverish
catch-up program. This was demonstrated following
World War II.
Waiting in the wings today are improved models
of highways and bridges that have taken decades to
evolve. Nothing shouldunduly delay the construc-
tion of such roads on a steady programmed basis, in
both urbanized andrural regions. It will take years
to catch up with the vast highway development job,
should it be allowed to get out of line because of
George G. Munyon, Jr. Highway Superintendent
current panic thinking.
(Excerpt from Editorial by Harold J. McKeever, Ru-

Dog Officer
Dog has been "Man's Best Friend" for centuries. When man still
lived in caves and was clad in skins of wild beasts, the dog was his
friend. These men trained their dogs to hunt, carry heavy burdens and
to guard their personal property.
At present there are over 200 different domestic breeds of dogs
while wild breeds can still be found in Australia and in some European
countries. Dogs in addition to being pets are used for hunting and work-
ing such as a Shepard, Husky, Collie and Pincher.
Many dogs are mixtures of these standard breeds and are often
referred to as "mutts". It is often the "mutt" that disrupts otherwise
quiet neighborhoods with excessiv barking day and night and most often
on the rise and fall of the moon as our records of disturbance calls indi-
cate. These dogs are usually to blame for the vicious attacks on other
animals, damage to personal property, harassment of people on horse-
back and on bicycles and, of course, to pedestrians. The number of dog
bites to persons on bicycles increased this year, probably due to the fact
that the use of bicycles has become very popular.
August N. Misner, Dog Officer
It was very encouraging to note that residents in and about the
Center of Town restrained their dogs and thus minimized the problem
of dogs harassing school children during recess and going to and from school. The number of dog packs decreased
slightly in outlying areas as owners have become more concerned and have taken necessary precautions to prevent
dogs from gathering in packs. A more concerted effort to minimize nuisance complaints will be made in the Wha-
lom Area where a very heavy dog population is found. Therefore, with patience, discipline, and training and by
keeping a close vigil over our dogs, we can all show our neighbors that we have high regard for each other's rights,
and that we have not all "gone to the dogs".
