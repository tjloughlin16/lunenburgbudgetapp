Board of Selectmen
SKERWOOD G. WINCHESTER
RUDOLPH MASELLA
ANN P. HALL
Board of Selectmen: left to right - Sherwood G. Winchester, Chairman Rudolph Masella and Ann P. Hall.
All Town Officials spent much time in 1977 at what has become the perennial task of sorting through the maze
of regulations, requirements, and restrictions formulated by higher governmental authority. The Board of Selectmen
dealt with their share of the sorting, together with the regular duties of the office, during the 38 regular meetings,
2 special meetings, and numerous other meetings with other Boards and Committees at the local and regional level,
and on site inspections.
Although electionsby their very nature bring about changes, this year's seems to have been noteworthy on two
counts: one, the retirement of Normand J. Thompson after fifteen years of service a period of great growth and
change in Town government -- during which his cumulative knowledge and experience was of great value to this Board
and to the Town; and two, the election of the first woman to the Office of Selectman.
The proposal for the construction of a sewerage system in the Whalom area found the Board spending much time
on preparation for holding of a Public Hearing and a Special Town Meeting, the details of which are covered else-
where in this report.
Acting in the capacity of the Local Licensing Authority, 17 licenses were issued for the sale of alcoholic bev-
erages; 53 one-day special licenses were issued; and 9 public hearings were held in connection with licensing mat-
ters. It was a particularly busy year for Board of Health matters. Among the more far-reaching was the change in
the Town Nursing Service. The resignation of Mrs. Janet N. Pace, combined with the preliminary draft of regula-
tions for extended nursing service coverage, prompted the Board of Health to join the Nashoba Nursing Service. Mrs.
Shirley M. Carpenter was employed by Nashoba to serve Lunenburg providing continuity to our patients despite the
change. The school nurses will be in the direct employ of the School Department for the future.
A "Final Decision ordering the Town to comply with the regulations for the operation and closure of the Lun-
enburg Municipal Refuse Disposal Area was received from the Massachusetts Department of Environmental Quality
Engineering. In orderto comply with the orderto coverall refuse daily andstill keepthe taxpayer's cost within rea-
son, the Board of Health was forced with regret to reduce the number of days of operation at the MRDA. We are
pleased to report that the DEQE is satisfiedwith the Town's efforts in this direction and have under review the Clo-
sure Plan which was submitted on November fifteenth. The Refuse Disposal Planning Committee and the Selectmen
are working closely together on the search for a new site, and their recommendations will be brought before the Town
sometime during 1978.

The preceding matters and many, many more have involved the long hours and hard work of many of our citi-
zens. The Board of Selectmen want to thank each and every one who has participated in the workings of Town gov-

As the year draws to a close, we are preparing a proclamation for the New Year's Day ceremony initiating Lun-
enburg's 250th Anniversary Observance. This gives US pause to reflect over our long and varied past: "Lunenburg -
The Heritage of Turkey Hills", a history of our Town, published in December by the Lunenburg Historical Society is
an invaluable resource for the study of the people and events which shaped the Town as we know it. The Board of
Selectmen urges everyone to join in the celebration of our Town's birthday, and hopes that all Lunenburg residents
will take advantage of this special opportunity to see one another, meet some of your neighbors you haven't met be-
fore, and together enjoy this wonderful Town we all call home.
Planning Board
The primary work of the Planning Board in 1977 consisted of: A. The preparation, presentation and adoption
at the Annual Town Meeting of the 80,000 square foot lot area in the Watershed/Residence B section of Town; B.
The processing of one new residential subdivision containing ten lots; C. The adoption of the new subdivision rules
and regulations; D. Processing of 73 new lots not requiring subdivision approval as required by Chapter 40A; and E.
Preliminary work on updating the Protective By-law of the Town of Lunenburg.
A complete recodification of the Protective By-law was necessitated to insure compliance with Chapter 808 of
the Acts of 1975 as mandated by the Commonwealth of Massachusetts. All cities and towns must find themselves in
compliance by July 1978. To this end the Lunenburg Planning Board at the time of this report is taking steps to re-
view and organize the Protective By-law of the Town of Lunenburg for presentation at the 1978 Annual Town Meeting.

Board of Assessors
The Board of Assessors wishes to report that 1977 was anotherslow year for new construction. The majority of
construction during the past year has been additions to existing housing and additional outbuildings. We would es-
timate the additional valuation coming into being during 1977 to be in the area of three quarters of a million dollars.
One large parcel of landwhich is now tax exempt is the former property and buildings owned by the Fitchburg
Railroad Company which is located on Summer Street. This property was taken by the Massachusetts Bay Transporta-
tion Authority (MBTA) on December 24, 1976. The property had been valued at $310,100.00 and was taxed for
$14,884.80. This is now exempt property and the tax money is lost to the Town.
The Board of Assessors is again this year requesting funds through an Article to be inserted in the Warrant at
the forthcoming Annual Town Meeting. We wish to change our present method of recording land transactions in the
Assessors' Office and acquiring topographical maps made of a size to be usable in the field. Our problem is that the
aerial maps now being used to show property lines are becoming worn from twenty-seven years of use. Each sale is
now drawn on to the existing photographs by hand in ink, and the lots are described by the owner's name and area of
the tract of land.
We propose to have topographical maps drawn from our photomaps, reduced to a manageable size and a new
numerical system of recording ownership established. Each tract of land in Lunenburg would have a number, a card
file would show the owner's name. In the event of a sale, a new card would be filled out and the change would be
complete. The existing photomaps would be available for useby the public and the other departments for the viewing
of property, but will not be maintained as to future changes in property lines. We do trust that the voters of the
Town will agree with our request and vote affirmatively on this Article at the Annual Town Meeting in April 1978.
We wish to report on new laws pertaining to exemptions that have been recently changed or are now in the
works at the State House in Boston. We wish to advise veterans with V.A. certified disabilities who are additionally
certified as 100% unemployable, that they may qualify for a greater exemption under SECTION 22E. The State Leg-
islature has now rewritten the State laws dealing with the several exemptions available to the taxpayerswho qualify,
raising some of the maximum amounts that a taxpayer may have in order to qualify for these exemptions.
The Board of Assessors wishes to remind taxpayers of certain exemptions that have been enacted concerning the

of taxes could constitute an undue burden. These exemptions may not exceed specific amounts. If a taxpayer feels

Park Commission
The 1977 Summer Program was a huge success under the leadership of our Director, Richard Stachowicz. He
was ably assisted by Karen Upton at the Center Playground and Tim Mulligan at the Wallis Park Playground. Both of
these playgrounds were very well attended because of the quality and enthusiasm of both instructors. We should also
not fail to mention the fine Arts and Crafts Program conducted by Cindy St. Arnaud and Joan Carey. The Town Beach
was very ably directed by Gerry Beloin. He and his staff are to be commended for the quality of their Swim Instruc-
tion and Life-Saving Programs. The Town Beach is batting 100% as far as serious accidents are concerned none!
John Therrien has done a great deal to promote tennis in our Town. In spite of court conditions, he instructed
143 people last summer. This figure can be broken down as follows: 9-13 years of age 65; High School 18; Ad-
ults - 30; Whalom (Youth and Adult) - 30. Chuck Winneberg led the girls' softball teams to a most successful season.
More girls are becoming involved in this program each year. The men's Softball League had a very good year with
five teams being represented.
We would like to commend the Personnel Board for their granting of our request for increased pay for Playground
Supervisors in order to be able to be more competitive with the area. One of our projects for this year is the setting
up of a master calendar. Each group which uses our facilities during the spring and summer will be asked to submit
their schedule to US in advance. There are so many demands placed uponour existing facilitiesthat we feel a calen-
dar is very much called for, in order that four teams will not try to practice or play agame on the same field at the
same time.
If the joint Conservation/Recreation Plan gets a priority rating from Boston, we plan to carry out a number of
programs within aperiod of fiveyears. First Year: To bringour three tennis courts up to satisfactory condi-
tion and to increase the number of courts to a minimum of five (maximum seven). The Town ownsthis land already,
and the Commission would like to build additional courts in Whalom and in the Center. Second Year: To turn the
landfill area at Hickory Hills into arecreational complex baseball field, playground equipment, etc. Third Year:
To improve our recreational offerings at Shirley Reservoir. We plan to have a skating area as well as establishing
several cross-country ski trails. Fourth Year: To take into consideration at each of our new facilities, the recrea-
tional needs of the elderly. During this fourth year we would take a look at all our facilities and programs to see if
we can provide for the needs of the elderly. Opportunities to be offered are shuffleboard, horseshoes, playing cards,
etc. Fifth Year: To erect a building for Arts and Crafts in the Whalom area. The youngsters deserve and need this
type of program. We would also like to addto some of our existing playground equipment and replace other outdated
equipment. Our concern is for Kindergarten through upper Elementary.
If you look very closely at our five year action plan, you will find that in this plan are represented the re-
creational needs of the Townspeople from one to one hundred years of age. With your support at the Annual Town
Meeting, this proposed five year plan will be able to be put into ACTION.
Our Commission would like to thank all who in any way helped to make our programs so successful. Special
thanks to Town Clerk, Mary Padula, and her very able staff who have given US their invaluable assistance whenever
we needed it. Last, but certainly far from least a very special thank you to our Supervisor, Robert Garneau and
his able assistant, Greg Bingham, who have kept our facilities in excellent condition. They certainly have a love
for their Town. Bob can be seen on a Sunday clearing the ice on Marshall Pond this is real dedication.
In closing, the Commissioners would like to appeal to the Townspeople to support US at the Annual Town Meet-
ing. We feel that we have your interests at heart why not have ours at heart.

Library Trustees
Library Trustees: Seated left to right - Barbara W. Brown, Barbara T. Murray and Beverly A. Mulholland. Standing
left to right - Librarian John E. Ramsay, John J. Boursy and Janet M. Cragin. Not shown is Robert S. Tapply.
Several major projects designed to improve library services and better utilize existing spacehave been accom-
plishedat the Ritter Memorial Library during the year 1977. The layout of the Library has been completely rearranged
to make it more convenient and functional for its patrons. The children now have a separate area on the lower level
of the building, which gives them a place for their own programs removed from the flow of adult services, and also
enables the Children's Librarian to devote all her energies to serving the younger members of the community.
The main entrance level now contains the card catalog and copier machine, along with the reference collec-
tion. The most arduous part of the moving involved the addition of new wooden shelving for the fiction collection,
now also located on the main level. This project was undertaken and completed by Librarian John Ramsay and Library
Assistant Chris Mulholland who, with funds for materials being furnished from Library Trust Funds and the Friends of
Ritter Memorial Library, and the greatly appreciated use of the woodworking facilities of Lunenburg High School,
managed to design and construct the shelves at a 95% savings to the Town.
Locating the reference and fiction collections, plus the copier machine and card catalog, on the entrance
level makes these materials accessible to all Lunenburg residents, especially to the elderly and handicapped who
before found it difficult or impossible to avail themselves of the Library's facilities. Furthermore, this relocation
assures that there will always be a staff member nearby tohelp patrons use any of these sources. This total reorgan-
ization has involved a lot of hard work on the part of the staff and temporary inconvenience to Library users, but
should enable the Ritter Memorial Library to continue to service Lunenburg effectively for many years to come.
Despite ongoing changes and occasional confusion, business has remained steady at the Library. The circulation
of adult materials kept pace with last year's figures which had shown a 16% increase over the past five years. Adult
totals for 1977 are: Books 18,838 (109 interlibrary loans), Phonograph Records 960, Magazines 396, Pamphlets 13,
and Talking Books 3. Film circulation (71) dropped off because of stricter lending policies imposed by the regional

Conservation Commission
The major portion of the Conservation Commission's time was spent implementing the Commonwealth's Wetland
Protection Act. Neither snow, nor rain, nor heat, nor gloom of night stays the Commission from making Wetlands
site inspections and determinations. To this end the Conservation Commission held 21 regular meetings, 3 special
meetings, 5 public hearings, and acted upon 13 "Requests for Determination". While this is atime-consuming part of
the Commission's job, the members feel that it is better handled at a local level. The interests of the citizens of our
Town are better protected by local people than by some slow-moving bureaucratic office of the State Government.
One of the highlights of the year was providing abuilding on the "Laurelbank Farm Conservation Area" for the
Lunenburg High School "Project Green Thumb" and cooperating with the School Department in making available con-
servation land for our young people who participate in this valuable experience.
The Commonwealth has recently notified the Lunenburg Conservation Commission that a check in the amount of
$12,500 from the "Self-Help Program" will soon be sent to the Town of Lunenburg to reimburse the Town 50% of the
purchase price of the "Laurelbank Farm Conservation Area". Because of Federal and State requirements for a Con-
servation/Recreation Plan, the Conservation Commission and Park Commission have worked together this year to im-
plement a Conservation/Recreation Plan for the Town of Lunenburg. This Plan will make Lunenburg eligible for fu-
ture "Self-Help" funds. However, because of new regulations about population density and income levels, the Com-
mission expects that such funds will be severely limited for suburban and rural communities, such as Lunenburg.
The Massachusetts Legislature has traditionally acknowledged the importance of wetland protection, and this
year recognized the need to conserve our valuable resource of arable land by passing the Farmland Protection Act.
The Conservation Commission feels our local farm people should take advantage of this Act to insurethat our valuable
farmland will be here for future agricultural use.
Lunenburg has long recognized the need to acquire land for conservation to protect our heritage. Your Com-
mission believes it is good business for the Town to purchase more land for conservation when sensible opportunities
arise, and urges Townspeople to support all Town Meeting Articles for land acquisitions.

activities. One such activity ishiking which included visits to such places as the Cowdrey Nature Center, Leominster
State Park, Willard Brook, Pearl Hill, the Wapack Trail and Purgatory Chasms. The members planto make their own
backpacks for future hikes. Sliding, skating and a sleigh ride, followed by hot refreshments, are some of the cold
weather activities. In preparation for the strong spring winds, the group is designing and decorating their own kites
and will fly them at Mount Wachusett. The culminating activity of the year will be a weekend camping trip. Formal
meetings wereheld once month led by Dot Connell and Joyce Downey and assisted by Susan Downey. The members

Horses and instructed all attending on halter competition and English and Western Equitation.
Our guest speaker in March was Kim Craffey who discussed the Equestrian Program at Worcester State College

at this show.

ings at the Town Hall, we have conducted field tripsto suspected problem areas, heldmeetings with Whalom residents,
andmet with other Town departments for their input on problems and possible solutions, all with a view to getting close
enough to the problems to analyze their magnitude and to begin to see what might logically be done about them.
At the invitation of this Committee, many residents of the Whalom/Bakerville area attended a hearing and
completed a questionnaire. As a result of information gathered, we have determined that there are several types of
problems which require further study, many of which are related to inadequate surface water drainage. Several times
during the year, many residents are bothered by standing water and backed up septic tanks resulting from land satura-
tion. Some of these surface-water problems might possibly be solved by improved maintenance and repair of existing
storm water drains, by minor extensions of these drains to serve low-lying areas not presently connected to the drain-
age systems, by improvement of street grading to direct street water into existing catch basins, and by regrading of
private property by owners, in order to direct rain water run-off into the existing drainage systems.
Many residents have indicated that although their original septic systems, installed before modern health
department standards were in effect, were inadequate and caused problems, installation and proper maintenance of
modern septic systems have provided effective solutions. It appears that many of the still-unsolved problems relating
directly to sewage disposal could also be solved individually, either by installation of new systems or by more effec-
tive and extensive maintenance programs.
Recently, the Environmental Protection Agency has been officially encouraging the use of noncentralized,
low cost treatment technologies in rural and suburban areas, and it is this Committee's feeling that such problems in
the Whalom area as cannot be solved privately might yield to this type of solution. An example would be the instal-
lation of large engineered septic systems on existing vacant land, to serve clusters of homes whose lots cannot accom-
modate adequate private systems. This Committee is recommending that its life be extended for an additional year to
complete investigation of this and other possibilities, and to determine the availability of federal and state monies,
preferably grants, to finance such small-scale solutions.

Highway Department
Nothing but "bridle paths" served our early set-
tlers and horseback riding was the only way, other than
foot, to pass from place to place. Trails blazed through
the wilderness were the only routes to follow, with
streams to be forded, as there were nobridges for years.
Once housesbegan to appear in Turkey Hills, the
Meeting House erected, and frequent Town Meetings
called, there was a need for better roadways wide en-
ough for carts and wagons which often bogged hubdeep
in mud. Peoplewanted tobe able to travel about to vi-
sit friends in other new towns in the area.
An entry in the records of the Townof Lun-
enburg dated February 13, 1729" describes the original
layout of Northfield Road and Lancaster Avenue. At a
town meeting in 1732 conducted by Sam Page, Modera-
tor, itwas "voted and grantedye sum of thirty-twoshil-
lings to pay Colonel Josiah Willard for eight days serv-
ice done for said town in laying out of highwayes for
time past".
One of the earliest projects, possibly 1724, was
George G. Munyon, Highway Superintendent
cutting of trees and clearing of the way from Lancaster
to the center of town, a portion of which is now Lan-
caster Avenue. Next the course over the hills west to South Fitchburg was cleared. This route started at Main Street
between Town Hall and the Daniel Putnam store (Honey Farms) and continued through "Salt Peter Hollow" passing
Stillman Stone's on Massachusetts Avenue, thence to J. W. Mossman's on what is now Beal Street and by Levi White's
on the present White Street. It was said to have no bridges, nor was it graded, but timber and brush were cut away.
In 1736 Benjamin Bellows and others asked the General Court for 450 acres of land to build the present North-
field Road. It proceeded from Flat Hill passing what is now Massachusetts Avenue and the present Northfield Road,
past Pearl Hill school house, thence to Ashburnham and was to continue to Ticonderoga. The cannon from Fort Ti-
conderoga was brought down over this road by oxen. When they came to a farm that had a yoke of oxen, they put
them on to take the place of those that had made the weary trek.
In the beginning the Selectmen were overseers of highway projects in conjunction with a highway surveyor or
commissioner appointed by them. A copy of a portion of the 1853 town report shows that the practice of tax abate-
ment for work done continued, in addition to paying for extra labor and other work on roads and bridges. (Excerpt
from "The Heritage of Turkey Hills".)

Police Department
The highlight of 1977 was the completion of the new police facility which was occupied in September. We
are now on ground level and the dispatchers can serve everyone who comes in the door, for either police or fire
business, as well as relaying inquiries for other Town departments on the local government frequency. There is a
waiting area for people desiring to see an officer, or for those seeking permits or reports. The Police Department
secretary has more room to do her work away from the general line of traffic. There is a private office for the Chief
of Police; duty officers have a place to write reports adjacent to the records; and there is garage space for one
cruiser.
On the lower level, officers have space for their lockers and there is an evidence room which canbe locked
up, and also a storage room. The two Sergeants have ample room for their desks, andthere is privacy for interroga-
tions and interviews in either the Sergeant's Room or the combination Patrolmen's and Conference Room.
A separate police facility with all the advantages mentioned above has been sorely needed for a long time.
Thanks to the vote of the Townspeople, the perserverance of the Building Committee, the extra effort extended by
the architect and contractors, and the generosity of local businessmen, it has come to be. An Open House held on
November 27, 1977 was well attended by the public.
In 1977, 5,750 calls for service were received by the Police Department. These included 117 breaks into
private homes and businesses, 196 larcenies of personal property, and 41 stolen vehicles. The total amount of all
property stolen in Lunenburg amounted to $198,771.59 and the recovery of stolen property equalled $103,250.00
or a 48% recovery record.
Investigated were 316 accidents, 33 hit and run (both property damage and personal injury), 163 acts of
vandalism and 34 assaults. Also, there were 710 house checks made, 214 summons served, 166 gun permits issued,
84 people taken into protective custody, and 475 alarms (false or otherwise) responded to. As a result of stopping
1,633 motor vehicles, 521 citations were issued.
Some other activities requiring police investigation or assistance were drug complaints, child abuse and ne-
glect, sudden deaths and suicides, violation of Town By-laws and a variety of other complaints handled as part of
routine police duties.
At the end of May, Patrolman August N. Misner was forced to retire because of health reasons. Gus's ded-
ication to his profession, and his relationship with the young people of our community is missed. Patrolman Stanley
W. Barney was named as his replacement.
On June 8th, the Police Mutual Aid Pact between the communities of Fitchburg, Leominster and Lunenburg
was signed. Essentially, this Pact now gives police mutual powers when in neighboring towns, upon request.
To more effectively meet the demands for police service, additional manpower is being recommended to the
Board of Selectmen. To improve operation of the Department, adoption of written rules and regulations for police
officers is also recommended. To take advantage of the Mutual Aid Pact, the installation of a base station for
police only is suggested. A frequency for this has been issued by the Federal Communications Commission, and
all mobile and portable radios are equipped to use this frequency.

Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District
At the close of oursixth year of operation, our school received an additional five years accreditation from the
New England Association of Schools and Colleges, making a total of ten years accreditation through 1985. "The
Commission action indicates a high degree of satisfactionwith progressrealized since the school's evaluation in 1975.
With eighty-seven percent (87%) of the recommendations now identified as either Completed or In Progress and an-
other three percent (3%) Planned for the Future, clearly the staff and administration at Montachusetthave responded
appropriately to visiting committee suggestions. The Commission reviewedwith interest many significant changes
that have occurred within the past two years to include the conversion of some facilities to accommodate specific
learning activities and relocation of other subject areas to better implement the school's philosophy."
For the first time, the Regional Vocational Technical Schoolswere notified of their entitlement to Title I Pro-
grams. The School Committee authorized the Administration to develop a proposal for use of such funds for educat-
ional Remedial Programs in the Title I Area in Reading and Math within the Academic Curriculum.
Our facility continued to have a high enrollment in all areas with 1,049 high school day students; 108 Allied
Health post-graduate students and 17 Early Child toddlers. The Adult Education Evening Program accommodated
1, 246 adults; the Summer Program enrolled 245 adults and the Afternoon Program from 3PM to 5PM had 138 students.
The Program Advisory Committees have continued to be exceptionally active and their work and interest is
sincerely appreciated as it has resulted in the continued success of the school.
Montachusett Regional was one of fourteen secondary schools which participated in a Pilot Program with the
Digital Corporation and the State Department of Education with a summer teacher training program and equipment
being received from Digital. A cooperative Data Processing Program with Mount Wachusett Community College in-
cluded collaborative programs for the Fall Term in Data Processing, Computer Operation and Programming. During
the Spring Term, a cooperative program was conducted in Graphics and Design.
Student activities continued at an active pace and with many participants from all courses. The Drama Club
presented "Fiddler on the Roof" which was one of the most successful events of the year. Social Studies students
traveled to the United Nations in New York City; the annual Winter Carnival was again success; and the
Commercial Art Department painted an 8' X 17' mural at Montrath of Groton, ahospital for severely handicapped
children. Carpentry students constructed 14 tent platforms for the Nashua Valley Council of the Boy Scouts of
America. Students from Carpentry, Electrical and Air Conditioning shops participated in the House Building Pro-
gram. These are just a few of the many constructive enterprises performed by our students.
George L. Ross, Superintendent Director, was severely and critically injured by a hit-and-run driverin
Ellenville, New York in July. Mr. Ross was hospitalized in Albany, New York for more than a month and has been
recuperating at his home since that time. He is expected to return to work in March of 1978. Thomas F. Markham,
Jr., Coordinator of Area/Continuing Occupational Educational Programs, was votedby the School District Committee
on August 3, 1977 to be Acting Superintendent - Director.
At the annual reorganization, Mr. Robert G. Raboin, of Fitchburg, was elected Chairman of the School Com-
mittee and Mrs. Janice U. Corser, of Royalston, was unanimously elected Vice-Chairman.
A number of commendations were awarded to the School, particularly our effort in the United Fund with SUC-
cessful participation by Staff and Administration; our participation in various civic activities; the Blood Bank which
is held in our nursing facility; visits to nursing homes and hospitals; and participation in community sponsored fairs.
With our additional five-year accreditation, the efforts of our Program Advisory Committee, our General Ad-
visory Committee, the School Committee, Administration, Staff and students, we at "Monty Tech" have again com-
pleted a year of proud achievements and accomplishments in all educational areas.

Treasurer
Financial crises engulfing some of the nation's largest cities have stimulated greater concern about the credit-
worthiness of municipal debt, which for decades had been viewed as "risk free". A recognized need for closer atten-
tion to the handling of government funds in order to moderate these adverse trends has resulted in radical changes in
the emphasis placed on municipal finance and cash management, a rapidly changing area in which the City or Town
Treasurer plays a key role.
Effective cash management is generally recognized as essential to good fiscal management. This is particu-
larly true as mounting costs have placed ever-increasing pressures on local revenues, and we must find ways to meet
increased costs while placing a minimum additional burden on the taxpayer. Many of the policies and procedures that
are only now being introduced to many local governments for use in investing so-called "idle cash" (money that is
available in the treasury but is not needed for current day-to-day financial transactions) have been employed in our
Town for many years, and in Fiscal 1976 and Fiscal 1977, for instance, resulted in total interest earnings in excess of
$125,000. Even so, the Treasurer/Collector's office is in the process of reviewing and improving collection, deposit
and disbursement procedures to assure even greater availability of funds to meet cash needs and for investment pur-
poses. The development of procedures which bring revenue dollars into the treasury as quickly as possible and keep
those dollars aslong as possible can net significant increases in investment dollars earned. The following graph dem-
onstrates the degree to which an expanding cash management program in our Town has and will offset Town Office
expenditures, which have been increased appreciably in connection with the conversion to a fiscal year, with its
double tax billing procedures, and which have been further inflated to carry out a carefully supervised investment
program requiring considerable time and thoughtful administration. As can be seen, even with additional personnel
and higher wage levels requested for Fiscal 1979, the projected net cost of running the Town Office in Fiscal 1979
is $15,518 less than the all-time high for Calendar 1973, and only $3,906 more than the net cost in 1970.

It has been written (tongue-in-cheek no doubt) that current investment programs would undoubtedly be more
effective and efficient if a nineteenth-century practice were still in vogue: treasurers were allowed to keep as per-
sonal income any interest that might be earned on invested public dollars. The public as a whole did not realize the
benefits of such efficiency (that is, vested interest as an incentive), and the practice fell into disrepute with grow-
ing awareness that the interest earnedon invested public funds is public money justas much as the principal invested.
Still, one of the biggest problems with cash management in cities and towns is that the communities do not have the
capabilities to implement such program, which requires capable personnel, compensated accordingly (the vested
interest principle strikes again), and the result of such short-sightedness is the substitution of a "seat of the pants"
approach to investments, In Lunenburg, we have been fortunate in having policy-makers (Finance Committee, Per-
sonnel Committee, etc.) who recognize that it does cost money to make money, and who have been willing to auth-
orize staffing the Town Office with personnel capable of doing the job right. Some states produce an annual compar-
ative evaluation of cash investments and tax collections on a population basis. Were such an evaluation to be made
in Massachusetts, Lunenburg would rank exceedingly high.
A combined Treasurer's, Collector's andAccounting office facilitates Lunenburg's cash management program.
At the present time, taxes and Treasurer's receipts are being deposited into a regular savings bank on a daily basis,
-36-
===PAGE 39===
and are converted into higher-yield securities as quickly as possible. Separate "zero balance" checking accounts are
maintained for payrolls and other payments, with transfers being made into these accounts as checks are disbursed.
Very often, if securities are not neededon the day they mature, repurchase agreements are executed for short periods
of time, thus permitting higher yields than would be effected if the monies were placed in regular savings for the same
period.
The cover page of the Town Office Report (page 35) contains a sample cash flow chart (month of February,
1978), which has been included as an example of the type of information-gathering which is being utilized in order
to make accurate investment decisions. Estimates of specific dates of receipts and disbursements to determine cash
availability and develop an investment strategy are also referred to as cash budgets. A cash budget answers the ques-
tions: "how much" cash is available, "when" and "for how long?" Included on the final page of the Town Office
Report (page 83) is the Town Treasurer's somewhat ambitious plan for further improvements in Lunenburg's cash man-
agement program and the financial management of our Town.
