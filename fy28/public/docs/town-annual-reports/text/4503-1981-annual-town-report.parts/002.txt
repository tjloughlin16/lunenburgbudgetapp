ELEMENTARY SCHOOL COPING WITH PROPOSITION 2½:
Although Proposition 2½ has had a devastating effect on numbers of staff at the elementary
level, Lunenburg educators are trying desperately to maintain the quality educational program for
which this town is famous.
Class size has reached a disproportionate teacher/pupil ratio. It is not uncommon for class-
room teachers to be dealing with an additional seven or eight students more per class than they
have been accustomed to in previous years.
Classroom methodology and materials have had to be altered to adequately provide for attention
to student needs. Maintaining a high quality elementary school on a "shoestring" with a "skeleton
crew" is no easy task. However, the community should be made aware that a very dedicated, very
professional group of educators are attempting to do just that by giving 150% of themselves. Need-
less to say, we have an exceptional yet exhausted staff.
The school committee and superintendent have been very responsive to emergency needs of the
students by providing additional part-time assistance as required by either state mandate or over-
crowded conditions.
Curriculum assessment has taken place and a curriculum development plan is being formulated.
There will be heavy teacher-input in the development of the curriculum, Curriculum development
will not be an easy task given our fiscal constraints. However, the enthusiasm generated by the
staff and the willingness to become involved is universal.

ENERGY CONSERVATION:
For several years the Lunenburg schools have been concentrating on conserving energy. During
the summer of 1981 a number of improvements were made to the T. C. Passios Elementary building to
help cut utility and heating costs.
Ceilings were lowered under the skylights in all classrooms by the installation of insulated well
ceiling panels. This allowed for the removal of a length of heating pipe in each room, as as
the retention of room heat at the lower level.
The incandescent lights in the row under the skylights in each room were replaced with energy
efficient fluorescent lights. In addition, all the incandescent lights in fifteen of the twenty-
four classrooms were replaced at this time. The remainder will be replaced as funds permit.
The lobby of the Passios School was enclosed by the addition of a set of interior doors time at the the
corridor end. It was felt that this will save much of the heat that was being lost each
outer doors were opened. It is the goal of the School Department to make this building as energy-
efficient as possible.

FLOYD F. BARNEY
September 1, 1947 June 30, 1981
(from Fitchburg Sentinel
Monday, May 11, 1981 issue)
After thirty-four years in the
Lunenburg School System, Floyd F.
Barney is retiring this June. In
1947, when then Superintendent of
Schools Charles Robinson asked recent
college graduate Floyd Barney if he
would help out and fill an empty teach-
ing position, the latter hesitated. He
was home visiting his parents and about
to accept a job in New Hampshire and
didn't think he wanted to teach. He
replied, "I'll fill in for awhile, but
I don't want to stay." Well, he did
stay and found he liked teaching and he
liked kids.
Barney grew up in Lunenburg. After high school, he went to Springfield College. World War
II interrupted his college career. While in the Army, stationed in Texas, he met his wife-to-be,
Gladys. They married and after he was discharged, he returned to college and was active in
sports, especially basketball. In 1947, he was named by Boston sportswriters as the "Best Play-
maker" in New England. That year he graduated and began teaching in Lunenburg.
Barney partially credits his career to being in the "right place at the right time." In 1958
he coached the boys' basketball team to the Wachusett League championship and in 1961, did the
same for the girls' basketball team. After teaching for many years, he became the assistant prin-
cipal and then the principal of the newly built Turkey Hill Middle School (1967).
He remembers the first day at the new school. "We moved into the Middle School in February
1968. It was a very orderly move. Everyone had saved shopping bags for weeks before and on that
day-it was a Monday-the kids carried their books in the bags to the new building. Then we had
a snowstorm you wouldn't believe. We sent the kids home by noon and stayed-closed all week." The
following Monday, school reopened. "It was a little hectic and about a half hour later (the snow)
came down in buckets." This time school was able to resume the next day.
He hasn't planned exactly what he'll be doing once he is retired. He enjoys gardening and
tends a big vegetable and flower garden. He expects that will keep him busy until October or
November. He used to train bird dogs and hunt, but hasn't done that for awhile because there
"just isn't time." The part of the hunt he liked was the dogs. Barney spoke with enthusiasm.
"I had more fun watching a dog in action. There is nothing more beautiful." His daughter, Beth,
needlepointed a picture of an English Pointer which now hangs on his office wall. This interest
started as a boy when he used to watch a professional dog trainer who lived in town, work with
his dogs.
Paperwork is one aspect of the job that he will not miss. Over the years as principal he
has seen the paperwork increase to great proportions. At times he has felt "locked in" and not
able to spend as much time dealing with people as he would like. "I will miss the people. I
have a great staff of teachers, secretaries, custodians, and cafeteria workers. I've enjoyed the
kids. The vast majority of kids are good kids. Ninety percent are hardworking."
Barney considers teaching one of the most demanding jobs a person can have. "If you build a
boat and it has a burr in it you can throw it away. A child with a burr in it-you must smooth
066." He thinks teaching now is more difficult than it used to be. Years ago, a child who was
not motivated "dropped out and could get a job." Now teachers have to cope with them and try to
motivate them. "When the bell rings you get rid of 25 kids, but 25 more come in. They are all
different with different personalities. 1 have great respect for teachers.'

RICHARD J. CAREY
September 1, 1946 June 30, 1981
(from Fitchburg Sentinel,
Wednesday, April 1, 1981)
Thirty-five years ago a young man from Natick
applied for a teaching position in a town he'd
never heard of--Lunenburg. Richard J. Carey got
the job. He began as a teacher and the varsity
baseball and basketball coach, later moved on to
the assistant principalship and nineteen years ago
was appointed high school principal.
Carey comes from a family of teachers with
two brothers also in education. Once his eldest
brother was his English teacher. When report cards
were handed out, the other students gathered to
see what mark he received. "A 'B'," they said,
"You're lucky your brother is the teacher." "I
went home and told my brother never to give me a
'B' again. Don't flunk me, but give me a 'C', I
said."
He is sure his own children suffered some
from having gone through Lunenburg schools with
their father as principal. It was a rule that they
were not to talk to me about school, except academic areas, not about other students. I'm sure
they had problems that I didn't hear about.
Before coming to town, Carey served in World War II, flying heavy bomber missions over
Germany. "I was a member of a nine-man crew and we became like a family. We still keep in touch."
They celebrated their 35th Reunion at the Pocono Mountains last summer. It was such a success
that they plan to get together every two or three years instead of the five to ten they had been
seeing each other.
An active man, Carey frequently rises to point out something that illustrates what he is
saving: an aerial photograph from about 1948 which hangs on his wall showing what is now the
Primary School and the Historical Society Building, then the entire Lunenburg School System--the
way it was when he arrived. The plaque that tells when the present high school was built, and
the shelves below the window where the high school yearbooks are kept. He uses them to look up
students whose name he may have forgotten, but on seeing their pictures he remembers them.
"I like the kids. I take a half-dozen walks around the halls daily to see what is going on.
I also do cafeteria duty every day. I don't have to, but it's a good way to have conversations
and keep in touch with the kids. In that low-key atmosphere, it is easier to seek out someone
with a problem and talk to them than to call them into the o office where a student gets on their
guard. I don't think the kids are afraid of me. Not that I can't give a class the devil when it
is needed. But I also make sure they know why a penalty is invoked."
He met his wife, Priscilla, in his first year of teaching in Lunenburg where she was also a
teacher. They married and raised five children. "I never moved on to another school because my
family was settled and 1 liked living here."
He speaks with obvious pride about his family. "Priscilla is a first grade teacher in Leo-
minster. She took ten years off to care for our family." The children are all college graduates.
"Now that they are all out of college, 1 can retire. Mary Pat, his oldest is a professional
dancer in New York City. Richard, Jr., works in Lowell as a computer programmer. Rosamond ("I
am the only one who calls her that-to everyone else she is Posey") works with Mary Padula at the
Town Hall. Christopher lives in California and works on construction. The youngest, Priscilla,
is a chemical engineer.
"I don't know how it will be when 1 retire (this June). I'll sleep an extra half hour in
the morning, he laughs. "No, retirement is not mandatory, but 1 want to retire while I can still
enjoy myself. 1 think I have gotten along well with the kids and the personnel. I will miss
being with the people every day and all the comings and goings. I'm going to miss the action."

RICHARD J. ALLAIN
September 1957 June 1981
A 1954 graduate of Fitchburg State College receiving his bachelor of science degree, Dick
entered the armed services. Upon his discharge in 1956, he was hired as a teacher of grade six
in the Lunenburg Elementary School. He received his master of education degree from the same
college on August 11, 1961. With the opening of school in September 1967, Dick was transferred
to Lunenburg High School to teach science, including electronics, to replace a teacher who had
resigned. Over his years of tenure he had taken courses at Fitchburg State College, Framingham
State College, Lowell Technical Institute, and attended various in-service courses accumulating
forty-five credits beyond his master's degree. Due to Proposition 2½, Dick chose to take advan-
tage of an "early retirement" and after twenty-five years of outstanding service to the Lunenburg
Public Schools, he retired effective June 30, 1981.

TOWN CLERK - The Town Clerk's position carries with it
the responsibility of maintaining a list of public ways
in the Town. Since 1728, when Lunenburg was incorpor-
ated, the town has voted to accept, alter or discontinue
the various roads in Town. Many of the original accept-
ances contained wording such as "thence across on the
dam till it comes two rod south of a large pine stump
on the south side of the pond " (March 31, 1798) It
has been a problem determining where such roads are 10-
cated today. Through the years, cities and towns have
more or less bluffed their way through this dilemma.
In recent years, the courts have begun to make commun-
ities more accountable in this regard and cities and
towns are having to produce proof of "facts" based on
assumptions. The Town Clerk has been fortunate in being
able to engage Robert Ebersole, a third year law student
willing to work for minimum wages, who is also a member
of the Planning Board and an active and interested mem-
ber of the town's Historical Society, to carry out the
necessary research. With this information, the town
will now be able to back up the decision of whether a
MARY L. PADULA
road is public or not. This determination is also vital
for the administration of Lunenburg's affairs on a day
to day basis. Many departments in town need to know if a way is public. The Highway Department
needs to know because the Massachusetts General Laws require that public ways "shall be kept in re-
pair at the expense of the town. " The Selectmen need to know for they regulate the use of streets.
The police need to know for prosecuting traffic or open bottle bylaw violations. The Planning Board
needs to know for sub-division purposes. The Building Inspector needs to know for zoning conside-
rations. Town Counsel needs to know so that the town can avoid huge damage awards for personal
or property damages. Additionally, this research will result in a valuable historical reference
source. Many layouts of roads not previously in the town's possession have been located. These
should prevent those situations which in the past have required new engineering layouts for highway,
sewer or water line installations. This alone should save the town thousands of dollars over the
years.
Another Town Clerk's record which has particular significance in guiding Lunenburg's future is
the record of births. The 1982 Annual Town Meeting will be asked to create a Regional School Dist-
rict Study Committee. One of the assumptions prompting this study relates to school enrollment
projections which are in part based on birth statistics. It is important that both past and present
local birth trends be accurately considered in any such projections, as well as up to the minute
local, state and national demographic trends. The following graph demonstrates that erratic birth
trends during the last decade could produce equally erratic projections.

ABOUT THE COVER PAGE - For years municipal funds have gathered dust (but not interest) in checking
accounts at local banks. The State required Cash Management Achievement Chart on the preceding page
demonstrates that this is no longer the case. Cities and Towns throughout the Commonwealth are now
typically over 80% invested. There are bankers and others who will argue that this shift in municipal
investment policy is damaging local economies by redirecting several billions of dollars that could
otherwise be used for real and personal property mortgages. Although this arguement is politically
powerful and indeed has merit, the trend toward tighter investment control of municipal monies is
not expected to reverse as long as cities and towns like Lunenburg, (where the approximately $200,000
in annual investment earnings offset $2.35 on the Fiscal Year 1982 tax rate) can continue to generate
hundreds of thousands of additional municipal dollars.
In spite of the constraints of Proposition 2½, Lunenburg is extremely healthy. We ended Fiscal
Year '81 with an unrestricted fund balance of $520,155 and no uncollected Real Estate taxes. Our
Fiscal Year '82 tax bills were mailed on time and Real Estate taxes were received on or before the
usual November 1 deadline. We have not had to borrow in anticipation of revenue since 1973. We were
able to weather the first year of Proposition 2½ without any appreciable reduction in services and,
even with a substantial reduction in our teaching staff, our present pupil/teacher-supervisor ratio
