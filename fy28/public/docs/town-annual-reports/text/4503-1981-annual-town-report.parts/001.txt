Board of Selectmen
Board of Selectmen: Seated left to right - Ann P. Hall, Chairman August N. Misner and James T. Van
Buren. Standing left to right - Executive Secretary Richard J. Eley and Town Counsel Timothy S. Hillman.
At the conclusion of 1981 and with it, six months of budgetary limitations due to Proposition 2½, it has again
been demonstrated that we are extremely fortunate in Lunenburg to have responsible and dedicated Department Heads
who have a close working relationship with the personnel in their respective departments, and who together have
carried out their duties in a very commendable manner. This harmony and "can do" attitude is not always found in
communities our size, and was quite evident this year. It must also be pointedout that through the collective efforts
of the Selectmen, Assessors, Treasurer, Tax Collector, and other elected officials, and the dedication of personnel
on various Boards and Committees, especially the Finance Committee, the mandate of the voters was carriedout with
a minimum of disruption and/or curtailment of services to our residents.
In May, the Board amended the Traffic Rules and Orders of the Town to enforce "No Parking Zones" during
certain times on Lakefront Road in order to effect better control and minimize the problems in that area, Also, the
system of having rubbish containers on the Lakefront was continued, and although this program was revised with re-
spect to only certain months of the year due to budgetary limitations, it has proven to be a program well received.
In August, the Study Commission reported to this Board that Lunenburg should continue to receive services from
the Nashoba Associated Boardsof Health based upon the fact that it would not be possible to acquire all the services
now received at a more reasonable price. The Study Commission also reported that communications and misunder-
standings were major problems with regard to Nashoba. The Selectmen and Nashoba, cognizant of this fact, have
undertaken steps to eliminate misunderstandings and to improve communications. A detailed report of Nashoba act-
ivities during 1981 may be found in the Board of Health report.
In November, after several years of study, research, evaluation, and many hours of meetings, etc., the new
Sanitary Landfill located off Youngs Road was officially opened and operational, and should meet the needs of the
Town for several years to come.

Board of Health
The following report was received by this Board from J. David Naparstek, Director of Public Health, of the
Nashoba Associated Boards of Health which consists of sixteen member towns.
Boards of Health are among the oldest town boards in Massachusetts. Your Board of Health is responsible for
the preservation and promotion of the health of the Town's residents. Certain police powershave been delegated to
them by the Commonwealth, such as the enforcement of the Sanitary and Environmental Codes and the control of
communicable diseases. Other responsibilities relate to promoting health in the community in such areas as clinics,
dental health and health education.
The Nashoba Associated Boards of Health helps your Board of Health carry out both the mandated and the re-
commended public health activities. Your Board in association with other member Boards, decides what services
Nashoba will offer; Nashoba's professional staff provides these services. The following services were provided by
the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health in 1981:

Well Child Conferences - These clinics are intended to promote optimum health among pre-school children
and to prevent the spread of communicable disease through immunizations. Unfortunately, low utilization in a num-
ber of towns and budget cutbacks necessitated the elimination of these clinics on July 1, 1981.

In order to provide protection to the public, establish a uniform practice in the design and layout of driveways,

Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors - left to right - Secretary Jacqueline A. Keeler,
William F. Reed, Bernard J. Paton and Chairman Kathryn M. Herrick.
The year 1981 was a very busy year for the Assessors, with the "Sudbury Decision", Classification and Prop-
osition 2, it became more important than ever to bring the properties in Town to 100% Full and Fair Market Value.
The decision of how best to accomplish this was put to the voters in a special warrant just prior to the Annual Town
Meeting in April 1981. As may have been expected, the voters said NO to a Revaluation. However, given the al-
ternative of having the State hire a firm and charge the cost back to the Town via the next year's Cherry Sheet re-
turns, an Article to hire an outside firm was passed.
- 10 -
===PAGE 13===
The Assessors drew up specifications based on State guidelines and solicited bids. OF the threefirms who sub-
mitted bids, the firm of Robert J. Finnegan, though not the lowest bidder, was the unanimous choice of the Board.
The Board felt that theirpublic information policy and their maintenance program would best serve the Town's needs.
A contract was signed on June 15, 1981. Since that date, the firm has hired and trained a crew of Data Collectors
and the Revaluation is in full swing. Once the information is gathered, it will be converted to machine readable
data and field reviewed. When this is completed, taxpayers will receive notification of the new value placed on
their property, at which time they may set up informal hearings to question their assessments if they so desire.
Since all assessments are currently a fraction of full value, all will rise when the Revaluation is completed.
It should be noted, however, that the tax rate will decrease in the same proportion as the increase in valuation. The
largest increase is expected to be in vacant land. Historically, land values in Lunenburg have been low. Because
property is to be assessed at "Full and Fair Market Value", the main criterion for determining this value is the sales
history over the last three years. However, there is a glimmer of hope for those taxpayers with parcels of land of
five acres ormore. A new section hasbeen added to the General Laws Chapter 61B Recreational Land which per-
mits lower assessments for parcels of at least five acres of vacant unimproved land. A more detailed and complete
list of exemptions is given at the end of this report.
As a result of implementation of Proposition 2½ and the necessary budget cuts, the tax rate was reduced from
$54.00 for Fiscal Year 1981 to $44.00 per thousand valuation for Fiscal Year 1982. What constraints will be re-
quired this year will depend in part upon the results of the Revaluation, i.e. how much additional valuation results
from the process. We expect preliminary figures in time for the Annual Town Meeting in April 1982.

Library Trustees
Library Trustees - Seated left to right - June Dettenrieder and Barbara T. Murray. Standing
left to right - Sheila A. Jalutkewicz, Edward A. O'Brien, Director Elizabeth H. Burnap,
and C. David Hobson. Not present when photo wastaken was Chairman Patrick J. Slattery.
Libraries in Massachusett are finding diverse ways to with budget cuts since the passage of Proposition 2½.
Although no public library charges for the most basic services--issuing cards, checking books out and in, and reference
work many have increased fines and now charge substantial amounts to reserve books and replace cards. Some
charge for use of meetings rooms that had been free. Contrary to all past library practices, some must now devise
ways to reduce the demand for services. While Ritter Memorial Library has not yet resorted to many new fees, the
fine charged for overdue books has been raised from 2¢ to 5¢ per day, resulting in a substantial increase in the amount
turned over to the Town. A positive side effect has been aslight drop in the number of late returns. It is hoped that
it will not become necessary to charge other new fees because in each such instance, the library becomes less "pub-
lic", less "free" and less available to at least some Townspeople.
The most noticeable change brought about by budget restrictions at Ritter Memorial Library has been there-
duction in hours open. Since July 1, 1981, the library has been open 48 hours per week, 7 hours less per week than
before. The hours cut from the schedule were the least busy hours of the week, and, being late in the day, allowed
for some reduction in the amount of electricity used, as well as a small saving of heat. This relatively minor change
in hours open does not seem to have seriously inconvenienced patrons, although Townspeople in Lunenburg have been
accustomed to longer library hours than are residents in most towns of the same size. Seven community groups, in
addition to the Friends of the Library and the Trustees, have used the library meeting room during the year, but shorter
evening hours have prevented some groups from meeting there this fall.
An unusual number of changes in library personnel occurred during the year. Diane Polk, who had become
Director in March 1980, left in May 1981 to become Consultant for Children's Services in the Central Massachusetts
Regional Library System. Elizabeth Burnap, Assistant Director since December 1974, was appointed Director. Since
it was necessary to cut staff hours in compliance with budget restrictions, the Assistant Director's position was reduced
to half-time, and has been filled since July 1981 by Elizabeth Vezina, who has a Master's Degree in Library Science
from Simmons. Richard Harper, Custodian for many years, retired at the end of June, and two of three Pages left
during the year, resulting in three more positions filled by new employees. Library service continued without inter-
ruption despite all these changes due to the dependable support of the three remaining staff members: Emily Foster,
Children's Librarian; Nancy Bigelow, Library Technician; and Kelly Horgan, Page.

Because the Assistant Director's position had, been reduced to half-time and the other new personnel were at
starting rate rather than the higher rates earned by the former employees, it was not necessary to cut hours of the re-
maining staff and a substantial amount of money already voted into the budget for personnel could be allocated for
other purposes. The total for books and other library materials was restored to slightly below the FY81 level. This
is still a serious cut, considering the rate of inflation, but it has not reduced book purchasing power to an impossible
level. Other budget categories--postage, telephone, electricity, heat, and equipment are also at levels comparable
to FY81, and, with care, should be sufficient for the rest of this fiscal year, barring an exceptionally cold winter.
Also, $600 less than last year is available for building repairs and maintenance; and this is a real cause for concern
because for many years the library has been restricted to doing only the most urgent repairs and minimal maintenance.
It will be remarkable if this category does not have to be overspent in FY82.
Children's Services, under the direction of Emily Foster, experienced another year of continuous activity and

2, 210 books. Special events in conjunction with this program were: a magic show, a treasure hunt, T-shirt designing,
a chalk-drawing contest, a wild animal talk, dancing on the green, a puppet show and movies. An additional reading
program was started in the fall. Three pre-school story hours with capacity enrollments helped three to five year olds
learn to enjoy the library, as did a new program, "Movies for the Very Young", using regional films. Other children's
activities included craft workshops and after school and vacation movies. The Primary School classes visited regu-
larly throughout the school year.
The Friends of the Library have maintained their vital supportive role again this year, making possible many
things that could not have been done without their volunteer time and financial backing. Funds raised with their
annual yard sale and "Book and Bake" sale, two roller skating parties, the boutique table, and sale of their cookbook,
"Favorites of the Friends", have been used to purchase all craft and food supplies for the children's programs and the
books awarded at the end of the reading program. The Friends provided the means to pay all the performers for
children's programs and sponsored the annual Children's Art Festival. A very substantial recent gift from the Friends
is a new paperback rack, allowing a much more attractive display of adult paperback books, Friday evening "Inter-
national Nights" arranged by the Friends this year included talks with slides on England, Ireland, Labrador, the
Pacific Northwest, and a Japanese tea ceremony. The morning "Interest Series" presented programs on medicinal
herbs, spring decorating, art prints, egg decorating and makeup techniques. Again this year, the Friends purchased
a pass for Children's Museum in Boston which admits a family free and can be reserved through the library. The fol-
lowing gifts have enriched the library's collection and are much appreciated: two books on Cape Cod given by the
Women's Club; "Shallow Waters", given by the Robert Moisan family in memory of Vern Ketonen; "The Story of
Writing", given by John Boursy, and a year's subscription to "New England Farm Journal", given by Robert Stockwell.
Many generous donations of used books were made, some of which were added to the collection, and the rest given
to the Friends for their sales.
In spite of shorter hours and reduction in staff, the library has handled a larger circulation of materials than
ever before. A total of 52, 193 items were borrowed as follows: 25, 595 from the Adult collection, 24, 619 from the
Children's collection, and 1, 979 from the Young Adult section. This represents a 6% increase in materials circulated.
The challenge underlying all library service during 1981 has been to cope with change, and some of this change has
been of a nature not experienced before by the present Library Trustees and staff. An increase in book circulation
has always been one way for a library to judge that "we must be doing something right" -- tangible evidence that
the desired books are being bought, that Townspeople are satisfied with programs and services, and that growth is
taking place. But increased circulation and growing number of people in the building to study or participate in
programs must now be handled in fewer hours by a smaller staff working with a reduced budget, compelling all con-
cerned to realize that growth in services may no longer be arealistic goal. Some of the less public aspects of library
operation are already being neglected and could eventually accumulate until all service is adversely affected.
While larger libraries and regional systems are now looking to automation for the answer to the problem of
shrinking budgets, this is not feasible at the present time for a town the size of Lunenburg. It is encouraging that
Ritter Memorial Library has not yet been restricted financially to the point that services, other than the hours already
cut, must be deliberately curtailed. Moreover, Townspeople can be confident that all funds allocated for the library
are carefully managed to provide maximum return. The record of years past indicate that Lunenburg values its library
to the extent that financial support will continue at a functional level.

Highway Department
If a motorist should skid on wet pavement or a
patch of ice, have a blowout, or just take his eyes
off the road for a second, he could be among the 300
to 750 people killed each year in accidents involving
rural mailboxes. Accident records from one state
indicate that in a single year it had 3,326 accidents
involving rural ilboxes, resulting in 15 fatalities,
725 personal injuries, and 2,585 property damage
accidents. Those who are not killed are often blinded
and disfigured for life because the boxes and their
supports penetrate the windshield and hit the victim
in the face.
Ibox owners are limitedonly by their imag-
ination. Steel tractor wheels, old-time stoves, water
pumps, plow shares, milk cans filled with concrete
and massive I-beams are but a few of the devices
used to support mailboxes. Although such supports
may be artistic to some, most are serious roadside
hazards to motorists.
Design specifications for rural mailboxes are
promulgated by the U.S. Postal Service, but spec-
ifications for mailbox supports are not. In fact, with
the exception of one state, there appears to be no
national, state or local standards for the size, shape
or number of rural mailbox supports.
Mailboxes are hazardous primarily because of
their mounting height. Most boxes are mounted ap-
proximately 42 inches above ground to facilitate the
George G. Munyon, Jr., Highway Superintendent
placement of mail in the box by the carrier. This
height places the boxes in direct with the wind-
shield of many vehicles. Mounting of several boxes on a wood board, a common rural occurrence, compounds the
problem since the board can spear through the windshield.
Ideally, a single support post should have sufficient tensile resistance and friction in the ground to hold the box
until it is propelled forward at the speed of the vehicle, then pulled from the ground and ridden over or thrown off.
One state has tentatively chosen the 2 lb./ft. steel U-post as the most cost effective of the single supports it tested
and intends to implement its use. Standard 2 by 2 by 4 inch mailbox mounting bracket angles, one on each side,
are recommended for attaching boxes to both the single and multiple standards. The box is attached to the angle
with 3/16 inch stove bolts. The angles are attached to the standards with a 3/8 inch bolt, lock washer and nut.

Historical Commission
The Historical Commission is currently taking an inventory of the Town. This inventory is the first step and the
essential step in preservation. It provides a record of the areas, buildings, monuments, sites, burial grounds and
structures which are of historical, architectural, or archeological significance to the community.
The basis of all local preservation planning is this inventory. This would provide the local Planning Board,
traffic committee, Conservation Commission and other municipal agencies with information about historic properties
which should be considered in community planning. The inventory provides the information with which the Mass-
achusetts Historical Commission can help to defend historic properties from adverse effect caused by publicly funded
and licensed projects such as, highway construction, urban renewal and subsidized housing.
This inventory will provide the basis by which the local Historical Commission, or other local group conducting
an inventory, can determine the priorities for suggesting properties to the Massachusetts Historical Commission for
consideration. The inventory also becomes part of the State Historic Preservation Plan and is considered in all State
planning.
Lunenburg's Historical Commission welcomes and encourages local citizens to participate in this endeavor.
All those wishing to volunteer their services may contact a member of this Commission. Our meetings are posted at
the Town Hall and are open to the public. The Historical Commission comprises the following membership: Co-
Chairmen, Gwendolyn K. Holmes and Martha J. Moore, Robert J. Ebersole, Paul B. Porter, Raymond J. Powell, Jr.
and Glenn S. Stillman.

Personnel Committee
From the Personnel Committee's point of view, the past year might better be described as "reflective" than
"active". Probably due to the constraints of Proposition 2½, there have been virtually no proposals for departmental
expansion or revision brought before the Committee. New hires have been limited to replacements in staff depleted
by terminations. Further, the Committee has undertaken some major revisions in the Salary Administration Plan over
the past several years. This year offered an opportunity to watch how well they work. By and large, the results
have been satisfying, thanks in large measure to the department heads responsible for administering the provisions
of the "Plan". It is not, after all, the rules which govern, rather the rules serve as guidelines for government, and
work no better than the ability of administrators to use them.
The Committeeha taken advantage of this "lull" to do some careful review of what is going on in the business
world, as it relates to employer/employee relations. Sooner or later, many of the changes will affect our munici-
pality, and we will need policies and/or bylaw provisions tailored to dealing with them. Municipalities are not
always subject to every change that takes place in the management/labor scene in industry, but it is necessary to be
aware of such changes and to constantly reassess the Town's position in relation to them. The point of all of this is
to minimize our vulnerability to problems which in the past our bylaw has treated sketchily, or not at all. Thus,
the Committee has put considerable time into examination of such matters as adequacy of pre-employment informa-
tion gathering, termination procedures and the like. This study is ongoing, and will eventually result in some changes,
either in the bylaw itself or in the practical procedures recommended to department heads administering the "Plan".
The Salary Schedule proposed at this year's Town Meeting will containonly one new category - that of "High-
way Supervisor/Landfill Operations Manager". This does not create any new position or additional salary, but merely
brings the stipend being paid the Highway Supervisor for Landfil management under the Salary Schedule, as prescribed
by the "Plan"; something which was not possible until the operation was underway and the responsibilities involved
could be properly assessed. Aside from this, no revisions are proposed except for what the Committee feels is a
moderate cost-of-living increase for all non-union Town employees.

Conservation Commission
The Conservation Commission met twenty-two times in 1981 and held three public hearings. The decrease in
new construction was directly responsible for the low number of hearings under the Wetlands Protection Act, and
was much appreciated by the members of the Commission. This gave the Commission moretime to spend on planning
and the management of the large acreage of conservation land under its jurisdiction.
A large house and barn on the Laurelbank Farm Conservation Area on Pleasant Street was razed during the past
year. These buildings had fallen prey to vandals and had become an ugly eyesore in an area of developing rustic
beauty. The area was graded and seeded with bird-enticing grains and grasses. In a very few years, shrubs and trees
will obliterate any signs of former human habitation. All Townspeople are invited and encouraged to hike these
conservation areas. It is practically impossible to maintain propersigns because of constant vandalism on these pub-
lic properties. However, residents may call any of the Conservation Commission members for the location and uses
of these areas. Hiking, snowshoeing and cross country skiing are encouraged. Automobiles, trucks and motorcycles
are not.
The Conservation Commission lost two of its most valued members during the past year when Russell Harvey and
David Richards both movedout of Lunenburg. Robert Leupold, a registered forester, and Glenn Stillman, a graduate
horticulturist, were named to fill these vacancies on the Commission. Their knowledge in their respective fields can
only be a plus for the Conservation Commission and the Town of Lunenburg.
As we peer into the last two decades of the twentieth century, the Conservation Commission asks its fellow
Townspeople for help and encouragement in its quest for the protection of Lunenburg's water supplies. It would be
a terrible shame to leave our children a parched Lunenburg.

Inspector of Wires
The year 1981 was the initial year of the office of Inspector of Wires. As of August 1, 1981, all wiring done
in the Town must be performed by a licensed electrician and accompanied by the proper permit and inspections.
Generally, all wiring must be inspected in its "rough" stage and then its "finish" stage. All wiring must conform to
the Massachusetts Electrical Code (1981 edition). Before the Inspector of Wires cansign the certificate of occupancy
for new residences, these inspections must be made and full compliance to the Code must be exhibited.
Inspection of electric work is not limited to new residences, however. All wiring must be shown to meet ap-
plicable portions of the Code through inspections by this office. Examples of wiring which require the Inspector's
attention include (but are not limited to): homes, commercial/industrial properties, additions, garages, bathrooms,
electric service changes, and most important swimming pools. The safety and welfare of the citizens make wiring
inspections imperative.
Since August of 1981, a total of 22 permits for electrical wiring have been issued, and this office performed
31 inspections. Wiring permits may be obtained at the Town Clerk's Office.

Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District
Nineteen hundred eighty-one was the first full year under Chapter 580 of the Acts of 1980, commonly referred
to as Proposition 2½. Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School endured all the pangs and problems exper-
ienced by the cities, towns, and other school systems throughout the Commonwealth. The budget process which OC-
curred early in the year was a difficult one for the school. Programs of proven value were discontinued. Reductions
in staff meant the loss of members who had been with the school since its earliest days. Emotions ran high. Among
the programs lost were all of those in the post secondary department: practical nurse, medical laboratory technician,
medical assistant, dental assistant. The two-year dental laboratory technician program is being phased out. At the
high school level, the child development and commercial art programs were marked for elimination and are now being
phased out.
In tune with the times, Monty Tech showed its concerns about energy conservation and costs, and in January
installed an Energy Management System which operates under computer control, and from the first day of operation,
there has been a show of demonstrable savings to the District.
In June, the largest Senior Class in the history of the school graduated - two hundred twenty-three students
and thirty-three post secondary students. Six medical laboratory assistants and twenty-seven practical nurses grad-
vated in August, the last post secondary graduation ceremony. During the year, plans for expansion which had started
in 1980 were carried on with varying degrees of success. By the end of the year, two towns, Orange and Petersham,
had voted to join the District; Westminster was undecided, and Committee activity on the expansion plans continued
into the new year.
The Massachusetts Association of Vocational Administrators held its annual workshop in June under the aegis
of the Department of Occupational Education and Fitchburg State College. As usual, it was well attended and
highly successful. Early in the year, the District Committee had voted to purchase a computer as opposed to the
previous practice of renting. The machine arrived in November with additional items of equipment arriving in Dec-
ember. All parts have been humming busily since installation. A new elevator for the andicapped was installed in
December.
The School Committee appointed a new Superintendent-Director in July, and Mrs. Charlotte A. Scott assumed
the position in September. At the end of December, James B. D'Donnell of Fitchburg, Holly A. Booth and Henry P.
Ares of Gardner, left the District Committee. The new Committee members (elected in November) are Charles A.
Dunphy, Sr. of Gardner and Helga D. Epstein of Fitchburg.
Among the many donations made to the school during the year was a 1981 damaged GMC one-half ton pick-up
truck from Chapdelaine Truck Center, Inc. of Lunenburg. The truck was marked specifically for the vocational train-
ing of students in the automotive department. The donations were not all one-way. Students in several vocational
shops combined efforts under the supervision of their instructors and repaired a fire truck for the City of Fitchburg.
An oak witness stand, a replica of the original stand from the old Fitchburg Court House, was made by the students
in the Cabinetmaking Shop, and has been delivered into the main court room of the new quarters. Other students
made a deck and access ramp for a former Monty Tech student who had been permanently injured in an accident.
There were many other services performed for the cities and towns of the District, most of which represented valuable
savings to the taxpayers.
Each year, students and faculty join at Christmas time to contribute to a local charity. In 1981, Royalston
was selected as the recipient town, and the Fireman's Relief Fund and the Policeman's Relief Fund were designated
as the charities to receive the funds, The total amount raised in 1981 was $363.02.

Sewerage & Drainage Investigative Committee
In May of 1981, the Town accepted a Federal Grant of $52,600 and a State Grant of $10,500 to conduct a
Facilities Planning Study. The engineering firmof Dufresne-Henry, Inc. began its work for the Sewerage and Drain-
age Investigative Committee in June and is continuing its evaluation of cost effective alternatives to an extensive
sewer system as of the date of this report.
Our engineer has identified areas in the Town where sewage disposal has been a problem through interviews
of homeowners, review of health department records, visual observations and a review of soil surveys. The findings
of this part of the Study were presented at a public meeting on November 5, 1981. Recommendations of the engi-
neer and public comments were used to formulate areas of the Town where more detailed planning should concentrate.
The next phase of the Facilities Planning Study will be presented at a public meeting on February 23, 1982.
The Sewerage and Drainage Investigative Committee will display several preliminary designs for each area of the
Town where alternatives to conventional individual subsurface sewage disposal may be considered. Public comment
will again be solicited.
Monthly Committee meetings are posted and held at the Town Hall. In addition, fact sheets describing the
progress of the Study are available at the Ritter Memorial Library. We urge you to acquaint yourself with this im-
portant Study and attend our meetings.
The Sewerage and Drainage Investigative Committee is made up of the following membership: Chairman
Bernard M. Freeman, C. David Hobson, Mary L. Padula, Chester J. Pliska and James T. Van Buren.

IN MEMORY OF -
MAURICE P. BILLINGS
1902 - 1981
On July 4, 1981, Maurice P. Billings, former
Lunenburg Superintendent of Schools, died suddenly
while visiting his son in Richland, Washington.
He also leaves a daughter and several grand-
children. He was married to the late Anna (Ahlin)
Billings.
Mr. Billings was born in Southeast Harbor,
Mt. Desert Island, Maine, on November 2, 1902, the
son of Alton and Bessie (Dow) Billings.
He was a graduate of the University of Maine,
class of 1929 with a bachelor of arts degree and
earned his master of education degree at Fitch-
burg State College in 1948.
He taught in Westminster from 1926 to 1943
and in the Hingham school system from 1943 to
1949 when he was named Superintendent of the
Ashby-Townsend-Lunenburg school district (Union
#27). He served in that position until 1954 when
he became Superintendent of the Lunenburg school
system (the year Lunenburg withdrew from the
three-town union).
He was a resident of Townsend and served as town selectman from 1963 to 1969, He was a mem-
ber of the Golden Age Club, Charles W. Moore Lodge of Masons, Fitchburg, the Townsend Historical
Society and the Amanda Dwight Entertainment Committee.
His successor, Ralph W. Brown, had this to say about Mr. Billings in the 1962 Town Report:
Under the leadership of Mr. Billings, Lunenburg had indeed developed its own school system.
Beautiful and functional physical plants had been constructed; a strong teaching corps, imbued
with enthusiasm for learning; capable and efficient clerical, custodial and lunch personnel had
been recruited so that a spirited type of educational program was already established. For these
reasons, one does not replace a man such as Mr. Billings, he can only hope to take his place.
His personal warmth of character, his keen sense of humor, combined with his dedication and
seriousness of purpose, are qualities I have been privileged to know. Consequently, we all shared
a feeling of loss to Lunenburg as Mr. Billings began his well-earned retirement on August 16,
1962.
On the occasion of his retirement, the parents, citizens, teachers and students of the Town
of Lunenburg presented the following CITATION to MAURICE P. BILLINGS:
In grateful recognition of your tireless and unselfish devotion to your responsibilities,
as the Superintendent of our schools
for the countless hours of faithful service to
the citizens and especially the youth of the Town of Lunenburg. Your decisions rested on
the criterion "best for the child". We express our deep appreciation for your conserva-
tive caution and "Yankee Tradesmanship"
ability in negotiating for large and small
purchases of pencils or pins, chalk or chowder, books or bricks, and maps or mats. You
have always displayed the attributes of a wise counselor, a true friend, a gentleman, and
one with a deep devotion to democratic practice and principle.

Annual Report of Superintendent of Schools
It is a privilege to submit my Fourth
Annual Report as Superintendent of Schools in
Lunenburg. This report covers the year ending
December 31, 1981.
As you read the reports of the other
administrators you will receive a more
detailed accounting of the events that have
taken place in the schools. The purpose of
this report is to summarize the total aspects
of the Lunenburg Public Schools.
THE STUDENTS & STAFF:
I have always been most impressed by the
quality demonstrated by the staff and students
in Lunenburg. The results of their efforts
continues to produce a well-educated student
and a committed staff member. Our teachers,
custodians, secretaries, cafeteria personnel,
and administrators provide each student with
the supportive help that is necessary tomain-
tain a quality school system. The dedication
of all of the individuals is reflected in the
results of the students' work, whether that
be in the area of academic accompl ishments,
plays, musical productions, athletics or
WILLIAM C. ALLARD, Ed. D.
daily attendance to their work. I must indi-
Superintendent of Schools
cate in this report what takes place in Lun-
enburg is in many instances far and beyond
what one would find in other school systems.
We are fortunate that we possess such talented students and staff to make our everyday efforts
worthwhile. I compliment and thank the parents of this community for providing such a supportive
home environment that is SO necessary for a quality educational system.
THE PAST YEAR:
This past year produced for us one of the most difficult years that public education has
ever faced in Massachusetts. As a result of the passage of Proposition 2½, we were forced to
reduce overall total expenditures for our schools from $4,138,975. to $3,506,166. or a total
reduction of 15.3% over the previous year. In reducing our budget by $632,809. one must remember
that we had to buy less in goods and commodities at an inflation price of 12%. In order to do
this, we were required to reduce our staff by some thirty professional people and in addition,
reduce our supportive staff (secretaries, custodial, cafeteria) by thirty people.
To accomplish such a reduction in finances, we were required to do the following:
1. Eliminate entirely some positions li. e., Assistant Superintendent of
Schools, Director of Reading E Language Arts, full-time Director of
Special Education, etc.)
2. Increase class sizes.
3. Eliminate custodial and secretarial positions.
4. Decrease athletic programs.

===PAGE 46===
5. Reduce cafeteria staff.
6. Eliminate teaching aides.
7. Eliminate all department heads and supervisors.
8. Eliminate all advisors to student body.
9. Eliminate late bussing.
10. Eliminate middle school after-school program.
11. Eliminate all subsidies to programs such as driver education,
cafeterias, and Title 1 teachers.
12. Eliminate all capital outlay.
It does not take one long in reading the preceding list to realize that in one year we were
forced to make many adjustments that affected our school system drastically. Always a problem
in planning to budget six to eight months before one is using it is the areas that start to show
up that one did not predicate. We presently are experiencing such difficulties and are respond-
ing to these as quickly and as thoroughly as possible.
In presenting the budget that I have outlined to you, I must indicate the very difficult
year that it was for your school committee. They, probably better than anyone else, had to make
the final decisions about expenses and the dismissal of a number of excellent staff members.
All of you should know that the school committee had to expend a great deal of time beyond what
is normally required to run a school system. Greater indepth study of the budget, hearings that
were required for dismissals and overall highly charged emotions in very difficult tasks were
required by the members of the school committee. Unlike other municipal bodies, the school
committee cannot raise its fees to offset a reduction in its finances. I would urge the citizens
in Lunenburg to understand and appreciate the efforts expended by its school committee in this
very difficult year.
THREE NEW PRINCIPALS:
As if the decisions necessary to carry out the implementation of Proposition 2½ on a school
budget were not enough, the school committee and myself embarked on the process to hire three
new principals. Mr. Carey and Mr. Barney had made their decision to retire and Mr. Ralston
