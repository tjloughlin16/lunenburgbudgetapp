Board of Selectmen
Growth and change
Growth and change have been the two most prevalent items mentioned in Lunenburg Town Reports for about the
last 15 years. For Lunenburg, 1967 held as much, if not more, of both of these as any of the preceeding years. The
routine day to day business of the office of Selectmen was pretty much the same as in previous years. There were the
usual number of licenses, permits, zoning applications, dog problems, etc., as well as the usual appointments to Town
Committees. However, it was in the not so routine areas that we had our greatest confrontation with growth and change.
The Townspeople in special Town meetings voted to contruct a new school, establish a Housing Authority to
build homes for the aged, and rezone an area in the hopes of attracting an industry to our Town. All of these are
products of the growth of Lunenburg. The change which will have the greatest ramifications on the effectiveness of
future Boards of Selectmen was the result of the resignation of our fine clerk of many years, Mrs. Mary L. Padula,
because of the pressure of her many duties. The office of Selectmen's Clerk which Mary had filled on a part-time basis
was expanded to a full-time position. Mr. Richard J. Eley has beensecured to fill this position and to set upa separate
office for most all of the Selectmen's functions.
The future promises to hold even greater changes in store. The State is scheduled to take over our entire Welfare
Program because of 1967 legislation. The Town must face up to the solution of its ambulance problem and must continue
to seek a solution to its sewerage problems.
During 1967 the Highway Department continued with its program of upgrading drainage systems throughout the
Town as well as maintaining all Town roads. The reconstruction of Leominster Road was started and will continue until
completed. The new street sweeper has been of much aid to this department and even was a hit in the Old Home Days"
parade. Lunenburg's Town Report was once more awarded a First Prize by the Massachusetts Selectmen's Association.
Special appreciation is extended to Town Clerk, Mrs. Padula, and her staff for the fine job they have done through the
years on these reports. For the first time in thirty years Lunenburg held an 'Old Home Days" celebration. Although
strictly speaking, this is not a Town function, it helps to create a great deal of civic interest which in turn benefits
the Town. The Board of Selectmen wholeheartedly endorses any program which achieves greater civic interest.
Board of Selectmen: Bernard J. Paton, Chairman Normand J. Thompson, Carl W. Proctor.

Board of Assessors
Equalization Tax Program Goal
EQUALIZATION of the tax burden is the goal of any tax program. Applying this to the property tax in Lunenburg
means that all classes of property in town should carry their fair and equal share of taxes.
It is the considered opinion of the members of the Board of Assessors that a revaluation program conducted by a
firm of experts is a giant step toward achieving equalization. Therefore, the Board of Assessors is continuing its efforts
to win voter approval of such a program by submitting an article at the March 1968 Town Meeting.
Lunenburg's first and only revaluation program was instituted in 1952-1953. At the time that the results of this
program were incorporated in the property tax program (in 1954) it won the hearty approval of the majority of the
property owners because a much better degree of equalization was achieved. Since 1954, the total valuation of all
property in town has jumped from $7,000,000 to $12,300,000 and the 4441 parcels of property listed in 1967 is
almost double that number listed in 1954. Such increases, coupled with the economic changes that have occurred in
the past 14 years, point to the need for another revaluation program in order to once more bring about a desired degree
of equalization.
Another matter that presently faces the Board of Assessors is that of assessing all property at full-fairmarket value
in accordance with a ruling of the Supreme Court of Massachusetts. The Board believes that this method of assessing
can best be achieved through a revaluation program. Any other method of achieving full-fair assessments would be,
in the minds of the assessors, unsatisfactory.
The many aspects and methods involved in any local tax program create the impression that it is difficult to
comprehend. However, from past experiences in discussing tax problems with individuals, a better understanding has
been achieved. The Board welcomes and urges taxpayers to discuss problems involved in the local tax program.
In 1967 the members of the Worcester County Assessors Association elected a local member, Arthur J. Brockelman,
to serve as president of the Association.
Board of Assessors: Arthur J. Brockelman, Chairman Chester J. Pliska, Clerk-Kathleen M. Deming, Leroy Allen.

A Guiding Hand
The beginning of each new year generally signals the start of the long drive
toward the annual Town Meeting in March. These matters coupled with the attention
which the routine administration of Town business commands, is usually sufficient to
keep the pressure on right down to the final adjournment of Town Meeting. Something
new was added in 1967, however, the issue of a new school was finally settled after
having been considered for morethan two years. The hotly contested issues of -what?
-where? and -how much? were finally resolved at a special Town Meeting in January,
1967. Considerable painstaking work was necessary to make it possible for voter con-
sideration to be given to all propositions within the framework of the law. Beyond that,
the vote passed necessitated extensive land takings. Thanks to the cooperation of the
land owners the Town was fortunate to reach satisfactory settlements without going to
court, and without occasioning any delay in the start of construction.

A Need for Realism
In many a quiet little Municipality a metamorphosis occurred in the twenty years
following World War II, with quaint little towns changed to sprawling, crowded commu-
nities, the amenities for which people originally sought the peacefulness of these
suburbs were suddenly lost as the towns increased in population.
Although during this period, the influx in our Town didnot bringabout such a vast
degree of change, a population of 2657 in 1945 and 7320 in 1965 represented close to
a 300% increase and made ours one of the most rapidly growing towns in the Common-
wealth and the fastest growing Town in Worcester County.
Many communities have handled extraordinary growth well, retaining their basic
character while accommodating great increases in population and Lunenburg, byand
large, has been such a community.
Effective tools used in Lunenburg's preservation have been the early inception of
Mary L. Padula, Town Clerk,
an active, well informed Planning Board, Sub-division Control laws, Zoning By-laws,an
Tax Collector, Treasurer.
energetic Conservation Commission, strict enforcement of sanitary rules and regulations, and the early acquisition of
up-to-date assessing methods and equipment including maps, reflecting individual property lines, and photostatic cop-
ies of deeds.
If Lunenburg is to remain on the list of well preserved, progressive communities we must continue to take realistic
steps to meet the challenge of change. One such step has.been the inception, in 1965, of a Development and I ndustrial
Commission ,for acquisition of the right type of light industry, which is geared to the needs and values of our Community,
is required if we are to broaden our tax base to sufficiently accommodate the number and quality of Municipal services
which our Community deserves and demands.
There is no golden road to efficient, economical and completely satisfactory municipal government, and finance
is forever at the heart of municipal administration as the many activities involved can only be carried out by raising
and spending large sums of money. People everywhere complain of heavy taxes, but it is likewise true that people
everywhere are demanding more and better municipal services. Holdinga budget in line becomes indeed a challenging
task. Perfection is always a desirable goal, but all too often the citizens and officials alike strive for Utopia with
little regard to where the money is coming from. Now, more than ever before, there isa need for realism and common-
sense planning in local Government. A well run government, like an individual, must gear its expenditures to its
ability to pay.
More and more local communities are clamoring for "Federal Funds" to raise themout of their budgeting doldrums.
Where, but from you andme, do these Federal funds come from and what of the Federal Government's own fiscal failings ?
Assurances that continued Federal deficits and relatively unchecked inflation will not work to the detriment of our
Country do little to reassure. A household berun on a deficit so long, ascana business or a Municipality.
Why, then, is the concept of a Federal balance of income and expenditure out of stepwith the times? Britain may have
had to publicly announce that it was devaluating its currency but our country has done no less, although the govern-
ment has been careful to call it by other names.
Municipal officials particularly should be painfully aware of the reduced buying power of the dollar. Of equal
concern are the socialistic overtones of an increasing number of our local and Federal programs. In school we were
taught that we were in competition with socialistic and communistic economic systems. It hasbeen written that "Sharing
the Wealth" cannot work without strong government control (look about you at the Federal Government's creeping in-
trusion in local and personal affairs) otherwise you have the indigent supported by the industrious, and in time even the
personal pride of honest industry will fade as the industriousrealize they are being exploited. We must have more money
for our cities and towns no matter their size but we must also have more municipal executives speaking as concerned
citizens rather than politicians for it becomesmore and more obvious that "the economic carpet upon which our Nation
has been comfortably trodding is being laid over quicksand."

Dog Officer
Love Me, Love my Dog
Nineteen hundred sixty seven proved to be an exceptionally busy year for the Dog Constable, as forty-six
dog bites, twice the 1966 number, were reported. There is no doubt that many more bites occurred which were not
reported, because of family ties or friendships. This situation is of grave concern to the Dog Officer and Health officials
as every time a dog bite occurs, regardless of ownership, it is important that the dog be restrained and closely watched
to assure that he is not rabid. Although rabies occurs very seldom, for the first time in over twenty years, two cases were
reported in Massachusetts. As rabies is almost always fatal, we once more urge that EVERY DOG BITE BE REPORTED!
Seventy-six stray dogs were picked up in 1967 as compared to forty-six the previous year. Of this number
twenty-two were unclaimed and after fifteen days were picked up by the Harvard Medical School to be used for research
purposes. Residents should assure the safety of their dogs by keeping them properly collared and licensed. More and
more complaints are being received regarding dogs running in packs. This can be a very dangerous situation.
New equipment costing $1300 is being requested at the 1968 Annual Meeting. The sum of $440 is for the purchase
of a tranquilizing gun and related equipment, which can also be used by other Town Departments. Similar equipment
is already being used in many of the surrounding communit and by various State Agencies and is reported to be effective
in the humane handling of animals. Also requested is $860 for a shortwave radio to be placed in the Dog Officer's car.
There were over 2200 telephone calls regarding dogs in 1967, many of which were first received on the police and
fire phones and several of which were of an emergency nature. A car radio would put the Dog Officer in immediate
communication with the police and fire dispatcher anytime that he is on the road.
Personnel Board
The Pressures of Inflation
The pressure of inflation coupled with a national rise in wages hashad its effect on local salaries. The Lunenburg
Personnel Board of necessity must be aware of these continuing forces upon the Town's salary structure. Local wages,
both industrial and municipal, have exhibited the results of these pressures. For example, we have witnessed great
changes in nursing salaries throughout the area in a period of but one year. The recognition of these pressures, together
with a consciousness of Town costs and subsequent effect on our taxes, influences decisions of the Board.
The various Town departments are
doing a commendable job. We are for-
tunate in having dedicated employees.
The restructuring of the wage scale is not
made on the theory that the "squeeky
wheel gets the grease" but rather on sound
arguments advocating raises on the theory
that the present schedule does not truly
reflect a fair wage.
It is the losophy of the Personnel
Board to continually seek a fair wage for
every employee of the Town of Lunenburg.
This is a continuing process of evaluation.

Fire Department
Hazards and Hardships
In calling for the recognition of a need for a limited number of full-time firefighters
to be employed on a day-time basis, Fire Chief James P. Deming described the scarcity
of qualified firemen during the day-time hours a matter of great concern and the most
pressing problem confronting his department. Chief Deming also praised the quality of
service rendered by the volunteer fire force and cited their wil lingness and dedication.
As the public continues to demand a higher level of fire protection, more and more
training and experience is required of our firefighters. A firefighter must understand the
chemistry of fire, the theory of ventilation, and the principle and use of breathing
apparatus. He must have a thorough knowledge of rescue work, first aid, ladder work
and hose layouts and must know how to operate portable pumps and generators. He must
know how to use and understand the function of a multiplicity of firefighting equipment,
not least of which are the 91 different items which may be found on a single fire truck.
Needless to say, a firefighter must participate in countless hours of drill work
in order to attain the required degree of proficiency and it should be pointed out that no
compensation is received for the time involved. On December 10, 1967 the hazards
Fire Chief, James P. Deming
and hardships which can be encountered by a firefighter were brought sharply into
focus with the loss of James V. Belliveau, a Fitchburg firefighter who was trapped in a burning Iding. Smoke inha-
lation can also cause varying degrees of disability and danger from falling debris and explosive materials is ever present
in a burning building. Statistics show that the most serious fires usually occur during bitter cold weather. The bitter
cold and extreme heat are two of a firefighter's worst enemies. That a fire is imore than likely to interrupt his sleep,
his family and social life or even his employment has failed to dampen the spirits of our volunteers as they remain
ready and willing to serve.
In view of the above, and the fact that the payroll for all the call men is only a little more than the salary of
one full-time firefighter, the Fire Chief is strongly recommending that the pay rate of our call firemen be increased
and a provision for such increase has been included in his 1968 budget. Also requested are appropriations for the
purchase of hose, the updating of radio equipment, and new roofing for the Fire Station. The Chief also recommends
that land adjacent to the Fire Station be purchased.
During 1967 the Fire Department answered 185 calls, a decrease of 71 from 1966. Although above normal precip-
itation during the last half of the year accounted for a substantial cut in the number of brush and grass fires, a slight
increase was noted in accident, service, cooking fires and mutual aid calls. A breakdown of calls follows: service
calls-56; brush or grass-40; accidents-16; car or truck-16; building-11; Town Dump-11; resuscitator calls-9; mutual
aid-7; cooking-5; needless-4; false-3; electrical-2; chimney-2; clothes dryer-1; drowning-1 and rescue-1.

Knowledge versus Unreasonable Fear
There are many things which we would rather not
think about. At any time a nuclear bomb might fall in
our immediate vicinity. It is past the thinking stage
and we must plan.
We can no longer ignore the threat of nuclear
attack. Certainly the issue is distasteful but with a
small effort preparations can be made for fallout survival.
Practical facts about radiation and fallout are easily
accessible but civil defense warnings are to no avail as
public apathy mounts dangerously. It is time that know-
ledge replaced unreasonable fear. Pressure must be
applied in a pressing matter and efforts to educate must
be increased.
Not many realize that simple precautions could
save lives. Cellars should be stocked with water.and
non-perishable food, first aid supplies and a transistor
radio. To store a shovel, an axe, a kerosene lamp and
other camping gear, is common sense.

In view of the above survey and other findings the Committee concluded that a solution to our sewerage problems

regional concept, which is under consideration by the Planning Board and Selectmen, could afford a higher degree of
Federal and State funding than is available on an individual basis. In any event a public sewer for the Town could not
be installed within the next year or two as there is a considerable amount of groundwork to be performed before the
actual engineering is started. It is, therefore, recommended that the study committee be continued and an article
calling for its continuation has been included in the 1968 Annual Meeting Warrant.

Council for the Aging
Interest Expressed
There are today in excess of 620,000 persons in Massachusetts 65 years of age or over and this number is increasing
yearly. It has been established that approximately 12% of Lunenburg's residents are 65 years of age or over. Reverend
Leon R. Oliver and Reverend John M. O'Brien serve as Co-Chairmen of Lunenburg's Council for the Aging, which was
organized in the summer of 1966 to carry out programs designed to meet the problems of the aging.
Two surveys have been taken by the local Council. The first showed recreational, social and housing needs.
The second, a special survey on housing needs, showed 60 persons interested in low-rent apartments in Lunenburg.
The Council then investigated the means of establishing a local housing authority and on November 20, 1967 at a
Special Town Meeting called for that purpose, the Townspeople voted to organize and establish the Lunenburg Housing
Authority to provide housing for elderly persons of low income, the members of which are to be initially appointed by
the Board of Selectmen and then, with the exception of one member who is appointed by the Governor, elected by the
Town Meeting.
To be eligible for this housing a citizen must be 65 years of age with an income of less than $2,500; a couple's
income cannot exceed $3,000. Dwellings are planned for single or double occupancy and dwellings for two persons
may be occupied by a married couple, two men or two women or a brother and sister and in some cases by an elderly
tenant and a non-elderly person provided that person is necessary for the physical well being of the elderly person and
is of low income.
At the present time programs for the elderly are held in the Legion House on Wednesday afternoons from 2:00 to
4:00 P.M. and elderly citizens are invited to take part. More and more owners of various types of recreational facil-
ities and transportation companies are offering special group rates to Senior Citizens. It is hoped that the eventual attend-
ance at the Wednesday programs will warrant the planning of special excursions for Lunenburg's Senior Citizens at
special group rates.
