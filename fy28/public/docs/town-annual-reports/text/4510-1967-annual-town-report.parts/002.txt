Community Action Committee
Off to d Head Start
Although the economic well being and prosperity of the United States has surpassed any achieved in world history,
poverty continues to be the lot of a substantial number of people. The United States can achieve its full economic and
social potential as a nation only if every individual has the opportunity to contribute to the full extent of his or her
capabilities, by opening to everyone the opportunity for education and training, the opportunity to work and live in
decency and dignity. With this in mind, the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 was established. The office of the
Economic Opportunity Act directly administers such programs as, Community Action, Job Corps and Volunteers in
Service to America (VISTA). Some related programs are: Neighborhood Youth Corpsand Small Business Administration.
The Lunenburg Community Action Committee was appointed. on November 7, 1966 by the Selectmen and given
the task to study and develop programs under the Economic Opportunity Act. One such program is "Operation Headstart"
for pre-school children. The purpose of "Operation Headstart" is to improve the health and physical ability of
children, to develop their self-confidence and ability to relate to others, to increase their verbal and conceptual skills,
to involve parents in activities with their children. The first Operation Headstart" program was held during the summer
of 1966 with 26 children taking part in it at a total cost of about $8,000. The cost to our Town was approximately
$800 which was the value of providing space in our schools. A similar program for 30 children was conducted in the
summer of 1967. Application to the Economic Opportunity Act for allocation of funds for the 1968 summer program
has been submitted.
During 1967, four youths of our community worked in the Neighborhood Youth Corps.
The Fitchburg United Fund, of which Lunenburg is a member, has allocated funds for the Legal Aid program and
during 1967 there were ten cases in which aid was given to local residents. Presently under study is the possible
affiliation with the Fitchburg Community Action Committee, which is a member of the Montachusett Opportunity Council,
as changes in requirements for representation under the Economic Opportunity Act may make this desirable.
No other programs are planned at present as your committee feels that further study is necessary in this area.
The Home Department of the Extension Service met regularly during 1967. Emphasis was placed on adjusting patterns and crea-
ting original styles. Meets were generally held on the first Thursday of each month. Included in the services rendered by the Home de-
partment was a donation to All Saints Chapel and the donation of pattern books to the Library and the Junior-Senior High School.
Every organization has its ups and downs and "4-H" is exception. We were fortunate in having Margery Stillman accept the
Town Chairmanship. Marg's capable, enthusiastic leadership was what wasneeded to revitalize the local 4-H organization To see
these youngsters start at nine and grow in knowledge, abilities and interests to capable teenagers is extremely satisfying. A 4-H'r
who has participated in the various 4-H programs is more than likely to grow into responsible person and become an asset, not a pro-
blem, to the community. The many unselfish ladies who have given of their time, reaprewards when they see smiling 4-H'rs wearing
the dresses they have made or leading a heifer from the show ring as he carries away a shiny trophy.
A 4-H'r participates in many activities, not just the local club meetings. In September,4-H displays were placed in each school!
and a letter regarding the 4-H program was home with the school children. As a result over sixty children and parents attended the
first rally which consisted of singing, games and refreshments and a talk on the 4-H programs. In November, Awards Night was held,
with a pot-luck supper, a film was shown and awards were presented by Robert Smiley, our 4-H agent in Worcester County.
Our older 4-H'rs are part of the State and National programs. This year Gereth Stillman was a delegate in entomology to the
National 4-H Congress in Chicago and he also participated in the County Exchange trip to Michigan. Gereth was also chosen a member
of the State 4-H Dairy Judging Team, following in the footsteps of his brother and sister, Gregg and Suzanne. Stephen Craig received
a trophy in the Egg Laying Contest in the Poultry project. Ten members of the Wachusett Dairy Club participated in the Spring Dairy
Judging Tour. In August Gereth and Shaun Stillman showed their heifers in the State Dairy Show in Springfield. Lunenburg made a
good showing at the 4-H held at the 4-H Camp in Spencer, at which time Shaun, Gereth and Glenn Stillman won rosettes for out-
standing achievement and Shaun was presented a trophy for Intermediate Showman and Glenn a trophy for outstanding Junior Holstein
Showman. In cooperation with the local Grange Fair the 4-H committee presented Karen Bourque and Kathy Miller trophies for out-
standing dairy and sewing exhibits.

submitted to the School Building Assistance Bureau early in March and, after minor changes were made, the Building

Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical High School
The purpose of this report is to provide a summarized record of the major activities and developments of this past
year. Our School Committee worked diligently and faithfully to insure that the youth and adults of the Montachusett
School District will have a facility that will provide the highest quality Vocational and Technical education. Con-
siderable progress has been made toward the consummation of plansand hopes for the new Vocational-Technical School.
The structure will be designed for 1,000 students with core facilities that will make expansion possible to eventually
house 1,500.
On January 4, a Superintendent Rating Board was organized, with Mr. Richard Dunn, Chairman, Mr. R owland
E. Bonner, and Dr. Clarence P. Quimby, members. Subsequently, 26 applications were received and were qualified
by the Bureau of Vocational Education in Boston. After interviews, oral and written examinations, the Committee
elected Mr. George L. Ross, Superintendent-Director, effective July 1, 1967. The formation of the Montachusett
Regional-Vocational Technical School District was validated by an act of Legislation and signed by Governor Volpe
on June 12, 1967.
The "Business and Industrial Advisory Committee" was organized on February 1st, and held its first meeting
on February 8th. Its concept and function is to promote school, employer and union relationships to provide recommend-
ations for course offerings and adult training and re-training programs, and to evaluate the need for initiating pro-
gram changes. This committee has aided in developing survey as well as being instrumental in initiating an industrial
public relations program. As an additional aid for the design of shops, laboratories, and facilities, 15 craft committees
were organized. Their membership consists of a cross representation of specific skill areas. These include employers
of large and small companies, unions, engineers, and technicians. The craft committeesare of invaluable aid in recom-
mending equipment, course content, and in keepingpa with technological advances. Their long range function will
be to maintain school-craft relations and to provide clinics and demonstrations supplemental to the classroom.
The School Committee has adopted a part of its philosophy "a program of instruction that will be updated and
flexible, provide for career education and be readily available to people of all ages and needs". It plans to operate 12
months a year, 6 days a week and evenings, and expects to include occupational courses aimed at the general course
student, as well as those with technical level potential. It will have adult training and retraining programs. Thus it
will provide for upgrading skills for those already in industry, aswell as benefiting the under-employed socio-economi-
cally disadvantaged. Based on this philosophy, "Montachusett Regional" and the State Board of Education's Vocational
Bureau conducted series of surveys and studies to determine the curriculum. Twenty-eight courses were thus determined
as the most essential and promising for career orientation in this area. The program will consist of Grades 9 through 12
and will include post graduate courses. The courses offered beyond the 12th grade will include beginning skills as
well as advanced courses in depth. The same facilities that are designed for the day students will be used for 2000 or
more adults and out of school youths in the late afternoon, evenings, Saturdays and during the summer.
At the request of the town of Westminster, early in the year, an amendment to the Agreement was drafted per-
mitting Westminster's withdrawal from the District. The amendment was submitted to, and approved by, the towns and
cities.
At the first meeting in January, and again in April, at the annual reorganization meeting, Mr. Richard Dunn,
Chairman, and Mr. Francis A. Carbone, Vice-Chairman, were elected unanimously. Mr. Morrill G. Sprague was re-
appointed as Treasurer, and Mr. Victor Warner was reappointed as Secretary. Mr. Bradstreet had resigned as Chair
man to accept the position of Director of Guidance and Curriculum Development, effective January 1, 1967. Mr.
Herbert N. Hoffman was appointed to take Mr. Bradstreet's place as Lunenburg's representative on the Regional School
Committee.
On April 5th, the School Committee voted to obtain approval for incurring debt up to $150,000 for the purpose
of acquiring a site; preparing architectural and engineering plans; and for the construction of a Vocational-Technical
School. This action was approved by the member communities and the Massachusetts Emergency Finance Board.

The District entered into a formal contract with the firm of Korslund, LeNormand & Quann, Inc., as the Archi-
tects. The District then voted to incur a debt of $65,000 for acquiring a site, and for preparing engineering and

by eminent domain the site known as the Roper Estate and a parcel of land owned by Weyerhaueser Company on Route

School Committee
Superintendent's Annual Report
Over the period of the past five years, the annual report of the Superintendent of Schools has
been submitted as an all inclusive report incorporating the activities of all of the schools. In an
effort to vary its style somewhat this year, separate reports have been written by the Principals con-
cerning their respective schools. My portion of the narrative report will, therefore, be somewhat
more brief and general in nature in an effort to avoid duplication.
1967 started off educationally on a positive note, for after much soul-searching and struggling
within ourselves as a Community, the citizens at a Special Town Meeting in January, overwhelmingly
voted to build a much needed new school. This action represented a significant forward step and was
found to have a profound influence upon the future of education in Lunenburg, and many of our efforts
were devoted to fulfillment of this project during the year. Since the approval of this new school
signalled the eventual end of improvising and squeezing and afforded the opportunity for long delayed
program improvements, every effort was made to expedite its progress. Firm bids for school construc-
tion were received in May and groundbreaking took place in early June.
These were encouraging steps, yet much work remained to be done, for plans had to be devel-
oped to determine how best to implement this school as well as to crystallize the changes which
would result in the present schools as a result of this new facility. Perhaps, we should first answer
a few questions about just what a Middle School is.
- 93 -
===PAGE 96===
What is A Middle School?
Actually, there are very few prototypes of Middle Schools in our country to emulate, although
interest in them is growing at a rapid pace. A half dozen or more are currently in the planning stage
in Massachusetts with four or five in actual recent operation. Generally a Middle School is one
which would house students between the ages of ten (10) and fourteen (14). Such an age span provides
considerable latitude in the combinations of grade levels which may be grouped in such a school.
Possibly, the most popular grouping consists of Grades 6, 7 and 8, yet one of the unique
qualities of this school is its flexibility since it contains both self-contained classrooms and special-
ized areas which can meet the needs of pupils at the Elementary level as well as at the Junior High
level. In Lunenburg, because of the combined dictates of our school enrollment and the existing
schools, our Middle School will house initially four (4) sections of Grade 4 and Grades 5, 6 and 7.
With a facility having such specialized areas as Art, Music, Homemaking, Industrial Arts,
Science Labs, a Language Laboratory, Specialized Reading Lab and a sizeable library, it provides a
stimulating challenge to look at our educational program in the intermediate grades to see how we can
possibly innovate and improve our educational efforts and thereby capitalize on the learning opportun-
ities made possible.
The challenge seemed exciting ! The School Committee felt that such educational possibilities
in this school required that a Principal be selected as early as possible so that planning could proceed
forthwith. As a result, Mr. Floyd Barney was appointed to the Principalship with duties which began
in January of 1968.
At this point in time, decisions have been made that would lead to at least a movement away
from the traditional self-contained one teacher concept toward limited departmentalization, and ability
grouping by subject area rather than by a single group in all disciplines. French and Spanish will be
offered to all students at Grade 7 leading toward the concept that a second language is almost neces-
sary in today's world and an early start helps in acquiring mastery. Limited Industrial Arts, Home-
making, greater emphasis on Science also will be brought in at this earlier age. Continued refinement
of plans for the effective implementation of this school will proceed with Superintendent, Principal,
and the staff during the winter and spring months of 1968 so that the school can open with a meaning-
ful impact.
How the Opening of the Middle School Affects the
Rest of the School System
The Architect, Contractor and Building Committee have given reasonable assurance that the
Middle School will be ready for occupancy with the opening of school in September. Accordingly, the
School Committee has planned its programs in September 1968 with this thought in mind.
This housing relief provided by the new school, brings into focus many positive benefits for
the rest of the school system as well as in the Middle School.
To begin with, it means that there will no longer need to be rented classroom space, the
Elementary Cafeteria can be reopened and improvised spaces will no longer be necessary. These are
the obvious benefits, but there are also more important gains which are not so openly evident. What
are these? We must recognize that since we have not had sufficient educational space over the past
three years that we have been able to accommodate our students only by increasing class sizes and by
renting additional spaces. Such restrictions can result in oversized classes which inhibit meaningful
education and results in stifling limitations in progressive curriculum expansion as our enrollment in-
creases and as needs of the times dictate.
As a result, the School Committee has planned that:
(1) Our pupil-teacher ratio at all grade levels should be reviewed with the thought of
working toward goals recommended by the State Department of Education for quality
learning opportunities.
- 94 -

School Committee - Teacher Relations
Early in 1967, the School Committee signed its first contract with the Lunenburg Teachers
Association, which is affiliated with both the Massachusetts Teachers' Association and the National
Education Association. This action, resulted from the passage in late 1965 of Chapter 763 of the
General Laws which established the right of public employees of all types to organize and through
majority vote of the membership to select the organization they desired to represent them in Collective
Bargaining involving matters relating to Wages, Hours and Working Conditions. The teachers chose
to act in accordance with the provisions of this law, and the Lunenburg Teachers' Association was
lawfully elected as the representative bargaining group for all teaching personnel. During the fall of
1966 and in early 1967 much work and many meetings preceded the adoption of the above mentioned
contract which enumerated specific grievance procedures and provided a yearly opportunity to review
matters relating to salaries.
At a time when much dissention has been prominent between School Committees and organized
teacher groups throughout the country, our teachers and School Committee have continued to work
straightforwardly, effectively and harmoniously. Approximately two and one-half months of talking,
studying and negotiations during the year preceded final mutual agreement on matters relating to
salaries which become effective in September, 1968. The groundwork has been laid for more careful
and detailed refinement of items covered in the Contract. The outcome of this future matter of con -
sideration can lead to the strengthening of a good school system if the future relationships continued
to be maturely handled by both parties. This new dimension in School Committee-Teacher relations
has involved many hours of time in meeting as well as considerable study, research and additional
pressures on the part of School Committee members. One of my very real concerns is that the addition
of these responsibilities and increased demands will make it more and more difficult for qualified
School Committee members to find the time to give of themselves to the extent required. To date,
Lunenburg has in my mind been uniquely blessed with quality school committee members who have
been outstanding in their unselfish devotion to the assignment.

National Seminar on Educational Innovations in Hawaii
In June 1967, Lunenburg was selected as one of about twenty school systems in the State to
participate in a conference on educational innovations held in Hawaii for a period of eight days during
the month of July. This conference was jointly sponsored by the Kettering Foundation and the United
States Office of Education and brought together many of the most outstanding scholars from all over
our country, Canada and England. Lunenburg was selected as a participant because of the somewhat
unique pre-school program it had initiated as a result of seeking and receiving a federal grant under
Title III of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. This program was started in October of 1966
and carried on through June of 1967. It was granted funds for a second year of operation in the summer
of 1967 and is currently in operation. The objective back of this program is to screen all pre-school
children by means of interview, testing, observation and any other means in an effort to discover any
pupils who might potentially have considerable difficulty for one or several reasons when they enter
school for the first time. Finding these an attempt is then made to find ways of helping them over-
come these difficulties before they become too strongly entrenched. We started out with approximately
160 pre-schoolers and ended up with about 30 that we spent some months with during the intervention
process. Our results to date have not been exceptional, yet they have been sufficiently positive that
we hope to have a significant contribution to make toward educational innovation. We are continuous-
ly receiving inquiries as well as visits from many areas of the State and Country seeking information
on this Project.
It was my privilege to represent Lunenburg at this important conference where all types of
innovative ideas in education were exposed. It was an experience which made me keenly aware of
the need to constantly improve in education if we are to provide the types of opportunities so necessary
in today's world.
The Cost of Education
Like practically all phases of life, education continues to become more costly. This is
largely due to the fact that the demands are greater both in numbers of students and the sophistication
of learning. Salaries which long were low have moved rapidly forward in recent years and the demands
for better educated teaching staff have increased immeasurably. Along with these facts, ever increas-
ing inflation has taken its toll. We are told that what cost ten dollars ($10.) only a year ago would
now cost eleven dollars and twenty-seven cents ($11.27). To improve then continues to take in-
creasingly greater effort.
Lunenburg, through its School Committee, has strived generally to be at least at the median or
above in its support of its educational efforts. With the standardization of school budgeting through-
out the country within the past two years, it is now possible to make some valid comparisons which
follow:
Since the area of Instruction is our main product, I would like to direct attention to this
specific portion of our effort. School Management, a professional education magazine, reports in
January, 1968 that the National Median reflected in expenditures for Instruction in 1967 amounted to
78% of the total budget. Lunenburg's effort in this area amounted to 74.8%, slightly below the median.
This figure reflects expenditures for all instructional salaries, Principals, Teachers, Clerical and
Coaching personnel. Analysis of this shows that we are not slightly below the median because of
inadequate competitive salary schedules, but rather more because of not having been able to provide
more in numbers of personnel because of limited space facilities. This is one of the areas where im-
provement will be made with the opening of the new school as has already beenreflected in this report.
In the important area of Textbooks, the National Median was $5.10 for each student while
Lunenburg was somewhat above this figure at $5.61 per student. In the areas of Administration,
Operation and Maintenance of Plant, we were generally at or somewhat below the median.

Conclusion
It takes the cooperative efforts of many people to make a school system truly meaningful and
effective. Basic, of course, is the parent and taxpayer who wants, expects and is willing to support
quality education. An enlightened, dedicated School Committee endowed with perseverance and big-
ness of mind is essential to establish realistic goals and to insist upon their implementation. Basic
also, of course, is the quality, interested teacher who does the teaching job on the firing-line each
day directed by enlightened leadership of Principals and supervisory personnel. Efficient clerical
personnel expedite smooth operation while custodians provide an inviting environment in which to
work.
The cooperation of all of these people is exemplary in Lunenburg and their combined efforts
have contributed toward making the Lunenburg Public Schools a symbol of pride and achievement.

Lunenburg Jr.-Sr. High School
Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School opened this year with an enrollment increase of fifty-four
students above the previous year. Because of the removal from the building of two sixth grade class-
es the demands on our instructional facilities became no greater than they were in 1966-67, at which
time the school operated above capacity.
With the anticipated opening of the new Middle School next September we expect that the secon-
dary school program (8-12) will become more effectively organized and implemented, with an occupancy
of eight hundred students. This reduced number of students will allow more flexible scheduling with
maximum use of special areas because of the absence of restrictions caused by above normal enrollment
for the facility.
Among the many items purchased this year for instructional improvement through State and Federal
Education Funds was a Steno-trainer for use in the business education department. This effective
piece of equipment enables a class of students studying shorthand to progress at individual and va-
ried speeds of dictation. Funds were also obtained for equipment and supplies for the science and
guidance departments and for the purchase of books for the school library. These available funds
importantly supplemented the Lunenburg School Department budget appropriation. Plans have been sub-
mitted for obtaining materials for several other academic departments for next year.

Lunenburg Elementary Schools
There is widespread concern among Americans today about education. The rapid changes in modern
life impose new conditions with which all citizens, young and old, must learn how to deal. Know-
ledge is multiplying far beyond the limits of general comprehension. The introduction of new tech-
niques is making even recently acquired skills obsolete. Inherited modes of thought and conduct
have been displaced or are under challenge. Although greater demands have been made on the institu-
tions of higher learning in the fields of Science and Technology, gradual changes have to be made at
the elementary level to keep pace.
Here, in Lunenburg, the administration, teachers and supervisors have endeavored to continue to
examine and update the curriculum and introduce new techniques and services, which we hope will cul-
minate in a better understanding each child will have in the world in which he finds himself. We
have been somewhat handicapped in this respect by the overcrowded conditions at the elementary level.
It was necessary this year to find two more spaces to house first grade children as the high
school needed more classrooms which were occupied by our sixth grades. We were most fortunate in
being able to obtain two more classroom spaces in the United Parish Building, making four first
grade classes in this location. Although this greatly relieved the situation, one more space could
have been utilized for the first grade to bring the class sizes down to the number recommended by
the State Department of Education and our own local school committee. We have continued for the se-
cond year to house two sixth grades in the Elementary Cafeteria and one sixth grade at the Junior-
Senior High School.
In keeping pace with change, we have, during the past school year, implemented two new programs
and greatly improved upon a third which have been direly needed for some time. Although they have
been carried on in sub-standard quarters, we feel that they have been more than successful and a
great step forward in our educational system. These are in the area of Guidance, Speech and Hearing
Therapy, and improved Library services.

has been developing for half a century but

ENGLISH CURRICULUM STUDY
During the summer of 1967 the English Curriculum Study Committee formulated a language program
for Grades One through Twelve which had as its basis the work being done presently throughout the
grades. The current texts were used as guide lines. The Committee put the curriculum into written
form so that the Junior High teachers, for instance, would be cognizant of the work in the sixth
grade; so the ninth grade teachers would be cognizant of the work in the Junior High; and, converse-
1y, so that the fifth grade teachers would be aware of the requirements for the sixth grade.
Some of the committee's major concerns were: continuity of work through the grades, uniformity
of material covered within grade levels, and an awareness of the relationship of each grade's pro-
gram to the total English program. The committee considered literature on the elementary level and
recommended a greater emphasis in Grades Four through Six. Also, it was necessary to attempt to as
sure sufficient practice in written composition in every, grade.
