This book is respectfully of dedicated to the Memory
Carl W. Proctor
Born in Lunenburg and a Selectman since 1967, Carl retired in 1964, after forty-one years service,
from the New England Telephone & Telegraph Company in Boston where he was Plant Extension
Manager. He actively operated a dairy farm in this community. Although retired, Carl devoted
his time and talents not only to matters of municipal government of which he had a most progressive
attitude and knowledge, but to the many civic and professional organizations of which he was a
member. He was a gentleman in the finest sense of the word and, therefore, not only possessed
integrity among other virtues, but used it as his personal guideline in his many activities.

Board of Selectmen
Many Areas of "Responsibility"
THEMPSON
F. CLIFTON MILLER
RUDOLPH MASELLA
Board of Selectmen: left to right - Rudolph Masella, Chairman Normand J. Thompson and F. Clifton Miller.
Lunenburg, Worcester County, Massachusetts, population approximately 7500, 29 square mile area, 82.79
miles of road, a typical middle-class New England suburban community. The average visitor, reporter or resident
would think this about our Town. Lunenburg Selectmen have different thoughts of our Town. They are more apt to
think of thirty-five departments, appointed offices and committees which are their direct responsibility.
They think in terms of Monday night regular meetings lasting on an average of four to four and one-halfhours,
approximately seventy-five to eighty meetings of all types per year--budgets, dog complaints, building and Board
of Health permits, licenses (all kinds), street light requests, taxes, drugs, expansion of all departments, new industry,
snow removal, new highway construction, drainage problems, housebreaks, new townbuilding needs, bombing threats,
town sewerage, sanitary landfill and many more items too numerous to mention--any and all of which might come up
in forty to fifty items on the agenda during their Monday night meetings.
They see the Town as big business and little business, borrowing $900,000.00 for various purposes during the
year, approving payment of $3,908,299.12 in fifty-seven warrants, everything from a new highway garage to
paper clips.
In 1970, Lunenburg continued to grow one hundred and six zoning permits were issued, new roads were
accepted by the Town and more are in the offing. The P. J. Keating Company was granted permission to proceed
with a $3,000,000 expansion program. The five year street lighting programstarted in 1965 was completed, and for
the time being no extensive revisions to this program appear necessary. Renovations to the Town Hall (final phases)
were begun and hopefully the program will be completed in the near future.

The Town was presented a problem of major proportion when the State Department of Public Health informed US
that because of the national pollution problem, Lunenburg was to cease operation of its present town dump by July 1,
1971. The Selectmen have given many hours of deliberation to this problem and are at present in the process of dis-
cussing the solution of the solid waste disposal problem (perhaps on a regional basis) with several neighboring towns.
In July, the Board and the entire Town was saddened by the sudden death of our brother member, Carl W.
Proctor. Carl, although not a young man, brought to the office a progressive and renovation-oriented approach
coupled with years of executive experience--a rare combination. Carl was a decidedly valuable asset to the Town of
Lunenburg. His death created the need for the first special election in Lunenburg for many years, and the election
of F. Clifton Miller as the new Selectman. This death served to point out the wisdom and advantage of having
full-time secretary to the Board. Richard J. Eley, our secretary, was invaluable during the time the Board operated
shorthandedly. The Town is indeed fortunate to have Cf man of his calibre and ability.
We wish to express our appreciation to our fellow townspeople for their cooperation and understanding of the
problems of the Selectmen in attempting to fill a complex position on a part-time basis.
Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors: John F. Nicoll, Clerk Kathleen M. Deming, Chairman Leroy Allen and Chester J. Pliska.
In 1970 Lunenburg went to full and fair valuation. This was accomplished by revaluation of the properties by
the firm of Whipple-Magane-Darcy, Inc. These results were later reviewed by the Assessors, a land committee's
report was received and these recommendations were taken into consideration before determination of final valuations.
Less than 10% of the taxpayers filed for an abatement. In the opinion of the present Board of Assessors, the present
values are much more equitable and proof of this has been shown in recent sales.
The Tax Commissioner assigned a valuation for Lunenburg of fifty-three and one-half million dollars. Through
several appeals made by the Assessors it was reduced to fifty and one-half million for the years 1971 and 1972.
It was with deep regret that last year's Chairman, Arthur J. Brockelman, Sr., and a member of the Board of
Assessors for the last nine years, decided that he would not seek office again. His long and devoted service and his
interest in the community should not be overlooked. We would like to take this opportunity to say to Arthur-
"Well Done".

Town Clerk, Tax Collector, Treasurer
A Measurement of Growth
One way of measuring the growth of our Town in the last two decades is by
comparing the activity, functions and complexity of duties of the Town Clerk's
Tax Collector's and Treasurer's Offices during 1970 with corresponding items for
1950. Although the Federal Census figures for 1970 have not been verified, it is
agreed that our 1950 population of 3906 has just about doubled.
Town Government like everything else has become more and more complex
as the General Court over the years has added constantly to the duties and com-
plexities, problems, authority and responsibilities of the various Town Offices and
Officers. This is particularly true in the case of the Town Clerk, Tax Collector and
Treasurer whose duties stem from, are interlocked with and come under the juris-
diction of many segments of State and County Government including the Secretary
of State, Commissioners of Corporations and Taxation, Director of Accounts, At-
Mary L. Padula, Town Clerk,
torney General and the County Commissioners.
Tax Collector, Treasurer
As a result, on almost adaily basis, Town Clerks are now required to make decisions, interpretations and judge-
ments of a quasi-legal nature. As an example, the laws relating to elections and Town Meetings are most complex.
In the case of Town Meetings, failure to comply with literally hundreds of legal requirements can often invalidate
actions taken by the citizens in Town Meetings or cause challenges which result in court actions. Similarly, failure
to observe any one of the hundreds of relating to the registration of voters, the conduct of elections or the cer-
tification of election results, can erupt in legal problems. The fact that 190 Senate Bills and 43 House Bills, all
pertaining to elections, are presently pending before our State Legislators demonstrates the degree of change to which
these functions are subjected. Other areas which frequently involve the Town Clerk in on-the-spot judgements relate
to the administration of the Commercial Code, adoption of By-laws, Zoning and Subdivision Control and Vital Records
pertaining to births, marriages and deaths. The Town Clerk relates to nearly every Town Official in some way, as
well as to all Boards and Committees. As Town Government has become more and more complex the number of spe-
ial committees with which this office works and interrelates has also considerably increased.
The collection of taxes is strictly governed by State Laws and these laws, too, have greatly increased in number
and are subject to constant revision. Although the poll tax has fallen by the wayside, our citizens hardly need to
be reminded of the drastic increases which have occurred in other tax areas in the last twenty years. For whereas the
total motor vehicle excise collected in 1950 amounted to $23,166, the corresponding figure in 1970 was $229, 553
and whereas total taxes collected in 1950 amounted to $260,323, the total taxes collected in 1970 amounted to
$1,828,796. (A prudent Tax Collector would stop right here and explain that the Collector has absolutely nothing
to do with determining the amount of taxes to be paid. The gross amount to be raised by taxation is determined by
the voters at Town Meeting and State law governs what receipts and reimbursements can be used as offsets. It then
becomes the responsibility of the Board of Assessors to establish a net amount to be raised and, based on the values
which they assign to each parcel of property, to set the tax rate and determine how much each taxpayer's share will
represent.)
Although Bond Issues in connection with School Construction, establishing eligibility for State and Federal
Grants, more refined and detai budgeting procedures, the adoption of a Salary Administration Plan, and a multiplicity
of accounting functions related to the receiving and disbursing of funds have added to the scope and complexity of
the Town Treasurer's Office, accounting procedures prescribed under the State System of Accounts have changed very
little. As amatter of fact, it might almost be said that the State Accounting System, to which we must strictly adhere,
is archaic and should most certainly be updated to encompass more modern accounting methods and machinery. The
State Auditing Department has estimated that it takes new accounting officers at least five years to become thoroughly
proficient in this unique system. The amount of monies received and precisely accounted for in 1950 totalled only from
$448, 908 as compared to a corresponding figure for 1970 of $4,936,297 and total disbursements increased
$384,967 to $4,808,299. The scope and volume of work currently performed in the Treasurer's Office give further
testimony to the continuing growth of our Town.

Library Trustees
Resources for all Ages
Library Trustees: left to right-Janet J. Cragin, Jeanne L. Crocker, George R. Wallace 3rd,
Beatrice M. Fay, Carey D. Rhoten and Robert S. Tapply.
Your Town Library continues to perform its unique function as the one, unbiased, non-partisan bureau of in-
formation for all the people. Trustees and staff endeavor to maintain conditions so that townspeople of all ages will
feel welcome and "at home" in their library, will find the resources they need and enjoy a respect for one another
that will be of mutual benefit to everyone.
Circulation figures are as follows for the year 1970: adult books 16, 189, children's books 25,388, magazines
and pamphlets 1,698, for a total of 43,275. An increase in magazine circulation was offset by declines inboth adult
and children sections. Adult levels of circulation have remained relatively constant over the past several years,
but the circulation of children's books has declined as the school library programs are expanded. There isgreater
use of the library as a reference and study center in the evenings, so although circulation figures are down by 1,706
for the year, attendance is up.
Regional book truck deposits at this library numbered 582. One hundred forty-six regional films were shown
in Lunenburg and 117 interlibrary loan requests were completed for individual library patrons. The library purchased
500 adult books and 384 children's books. The McNaughton Rental Collection was increased to 500 volumes which
means an automatic exchange of 50 books per month. Discarded or "weeded" were 157 adult books and 184 children's
books. According to approved policy these are for sale at 25c a copy.

Town Water Study Committee
At the Annual Town Meeting held in March of 1969, it was voted to appoint a Committee of seven registered
voters to investigate the feasibility or desirability of the Town taking over the operations of the Lunenburg Water
District as a Town department. Laurence Ebersole, Wilbur Barney, William Clapp, R. William Brown, Jr., Ray
MacCracken and Orrin Foss were chosen by the Selectmen and Moderator, and Joseph Kibling was chosen by the
Board of Water Commissioners to serve on this Committee. At the 1970 Annual Town Meeting it was voted to continue
the study. Laurence Ebersole and R. William Brown, Jr. resigned from the Committee and Ernest Dow and George
Larkin were appointed by the Selectmen and Moderator to take their place.
During the course of its study this Committee met at intervals with the Board of Water Commissioners, the Board
of Selectmen, the Industrial & Development Commission, the Planning Board, the Fire Chief and the Conservation
Commission. It was also represented at a series of meetings regarding the extension of water mains on Route 13 in
connection with the planned expansion of industrial development on the so-called Niemi property.
The legal aspects of placing the operation of the Water Department under the control of the Town were dis-
cussed at length with the Town Counsel, Robert J. Larkin, and the financial aspects with representatives of the State
Street Bank of Boston. An in-depth study of the functioning of the existing Water District was conducted. An analysis
and evaluation of growth potential and future expansion of the water lines was made with respect to the existing set-
up as compared to the considered conversion. Immediate needs and long-range planning were taken into consideration.
After careful consideration of all the information at hand and of the opinions of the members of the various
departments and boards with which it met, this Committee concluded as follows:
1. That the Lunenburg Water District, as it exists, is an extremely efficient, well run organization.
2. That the existing set-up has, and will, continue to permit desired expansion programs.
3. That, therefore, it would not be desirable, practical or economically sound for the Town to take over this
department at the present time.
4. That the Conservation Commission should acquire and set aside on a continuingbasis parcels of land which
are contained in the 4000 acres which constitute the Town's watershed.
5. That the best interests of the Town will be served by having a new Committee appointed in two years to
reassess these findings.
In conclusion, this Committee wishes to thank the various Boards and Committees which parti cipated in this
study.

This was accomplished when a public inspection was held at the new housingsite at 131 White Street on De-

elderly tenants.
When inflationary costs were at an all time high for building, there was no turning back, for this project had
gone through all preliminary phases and had been authorized. However, several economies were made tobring the
costs down, namely-eliminating brick siding and simplifying the design. White stucco was chosen for the six apartment

peted floors and all-electric service.

for them at entrances. Special precautions were taken to comply with public health standards for sewage disposal,
and in fact the site was chosen because of the excellent percolation found in the soil. Of the ten available sites,
the Central sites failed to rate as acceptable.

Conservation Commission & Town Forest Committee
Environmental Awareness
In 1681 William Penn decreed that for every four cultivated acres in Philadelphia one acre must be left in
woodland. What good sense Mr. Penn had, and how prophetic are his recommendations for today! Experts not only
agree and recommend that every fifth acre should be in woodland, but also tell US that for every managed acre of
woodland a ton of wood plus a ton of oxygen is produced every year. Mr. Penn probably did not realize the full
implication of his sage advice, but he did know that there had to be a proper balance left for a healthy, as well as
esthetic, community.
The year 1970 has been heralded as the year of Environmental Awareness because of the widespread concern
regarding pollution, over-population, poisons, herbicides, trash, noise, etc. Finally after some seventy years in
the blind faith and false security that technology can solve all problems, Man has come to realize that he is, indeed,
a very real part of the eco system (just one small link in the cycle of nature) and that he cannot ignore this natural
process.
Lunenburg's Town Forest Committee and Conservation Commission has over the past ten years directed their
efforts to the planned acquisition of marshland and marginal land for the preservation and enjoyment of the people
of Lunenburg. Much credit for this programand its implementation must be given to the townspeople themselves who
are aware of the value of green areas, wildlife, and woodlands and have supported the program.
During 1970 forty-eight additional acres of land were acquired. The Chase Wildlife Area, forty acres of
swampy and steep land on Chase Road, and eightacres of swamp and woodland abutting the Cowdrey Nature Center,
were purchased.
In addition to land acquisition and the supervision of town-owned conservation lands, the Commission hasrep-
resented Lunenburg at three state-wide conservation meetings, one state-wide soil conservation meeting, six Nashua
River meetings, a Montachusett Regional Planning meeting, twelve regular local meetings as well as taking part in
Lunenburg High School's excellent Earth Day in April. The Commission continues to be impressed and gratified with
the active interest and support of both students and teachers in the quest of a stable and better environment.
The following is quoted from a letter dated December 30, 1970, from Mr. Arthur W. Brownell, Commissioner,
Department of Natural Resources, which was sent to Mrs. Nancy Sholl, Chairman of the Conservation Commission.
"On behalf of the Board of Natural Resources and this Depart-
ment, I am happy to forward this check for $1,050.00 to the Town of
Lunenburg as the Commonwealth's share in your recent openspace land
acquisition.
You are to be commended for your continuing effort which has
made your Commission a leader in open space planning & acquisition.
We hope that this Department may continue to assist you in your pro-
jected acquisition program."

Fire Department
"Dry Hydrant"
During 1970 the Fire Department answered 209 calls as follows: Rescue Calls 55;
Brush Fires 42; Service Calls 36; Building Fires 19; Car Fires 15; Accidents 13; Town
Dump 12; Mutual Aid 8; Bomb Scare 3; Asphalt Plant 1; Dog Rescues 2; Chimney
Fire 1; Railroad Car Fire 1; False Alarm 1; Accidental Alarm 1. Of these calls,
92 were alarms. Our Rescue Unit answered 55 requests and transported 51 patients.
Requests for 1970 numbered one less than 1969. The Rescue Unit is operated pri-
marily between 10:00 P.M. to 7:00 A.M. with the remaining hours being covered
by a private service. The Rescue Unit Squad strength is the same as in 1969 -seven
men on each of two squads alternating on calls. All squad members hold an American
Red Cross Advanced First Aid Certificate and attend an advanced refresher course
annually. The Rescue Unit is certified by the Massachusetts Department of Public
Health by anannual inspection. Three squad members attended a four day course in
Boston on the emergency care and transportation of the sick and injured held by the
American Academy of Orthopedic Surgeons.
The Fire Department held its bi-weekly meetings, drills and educational pro-
grams with attendance running at an all time high. The Department was well repre-
Fire

men attending. The Town's first dry hydrant put into service in 1970 was a big success. It makes available 700

as there are twelve to fifteen locations in Town where they would be advantageous.
Our equipment is in excellent condition and no replacement is planned for a few years. In order for our De-
partment to keep pace with the growth of the Town, additional space is needed. Our floor space is now filled to

also need space for storage and drying of hose, wash bay and room for the replacement of equipment.

Police Department
Citizen Cooperation - "A Police Aid"
Bombing threats, an incident of shooting at a police
officer, increases in breaks into private homes and
businesses, and the necessity of more specialized police
work, added to the multitude of complaints, ranging
from social problems to criminal acts, that confronted
the Police Department in 1970. Manned by the regular
force of Chief, Sergeant and three Patrolmen, an addi-
tional seasonal man was used in the summer to provide
coverage on Whalom Lakefront and during the critical
night-time hours.
The criteria for the Police Department should be
based on need as well as costs. The need is for ade-
quate protection for the Town of Lunenburg and for men
and equipment to efficiently and effectively meet the
demands placed upon our Department in these times.
Thought should be given to providing added manpower
to conduct special investigations, to serve as safety
officers and to perform duties that cannot be handled
realistically by cruiser officers on regular assignments.
All full-time members of the Department and some
12 reserve officers attended a series of classes on
fingerprinting with Lt. Richard Darcy of the Fitchburg
Police Department as instructor, Special training courses
Chief of Police, Frederick B. Hobbs
on narcotics were attended by three regular officers
with others slated to participate as the oursesbecome
available.
The traffic rules and regulations of the Town were amended and upgraded with previous regulations having been
in effect since 1955. Parking tickets became a first for our Town and they have helped control parking in the Center
and at Whalom Park. To further relieve the congestion and to provide more parking space in the Center, removal of
a small portion of the church lawn and of the Center Common is recommended. The new traffic rules made possible
the replacement and erection of new traffic signs with many yet to be installed. Other traffic control measures
recommended are the construction of a traffic island at the intersection of Townsend Harbor and Mulpus Roads and
the reconstruction of an island on Page Street and Lancaster Avenue.
Replacement of all incandescent lighting with modern vapor lights was completed in 1970, a project that was
spaced over a five year period. In the future, additional lighting will be based on need as determined by the Board
of Selectmen and Police Department.
The number of motor vehicle accidents decreased this past year due in part to the effective use of radar and to
a stricter enforcement of motor vehicle laws. Radar has also been used to control speeding on various secondary
streets thus reducing the number of complaints from residents relative to speeders. The Town will receive reimburse-
ment of $9,350 by taking advantage of state and federal grants for the purchase of school traffic lights and for radar
and radio equipment.

It is widely believed that any widening or straightening of roads, or opening up
of junctions is a road improvement. This is not necessarily true. The basic reasons for
improving roads are simply--to improve traffic flow and to reduce accidents. The aim
must always be to achieve both of these objectives, and the value of an improvement
must be measured accordingly.
The public is invariably misguided in the matter of road hazards, and to yield to
such pressures merely results in a non-improvement. Non-improvements can be seen
everywhere. An example is the realignment of the roadway without improving the sight
distances to match. Traffic speeds will increase, and so will accidents! An investigation
by the Road Research Laboratory on 16 sites where roads were resurfaced and better riding
quality was produced without improvements in other features showed an increase of 30%
in accidents. Resurfacing should, therefore, be accompanied by other measures, such as
improving skid resistance, better direction signing and the adoption of a higher standard
Richard W. Brown
of street lighting. Above all, in the selection of our roadworks, we should ensure that
Highway Superintendent
due priority be given to those which will prevent the most accidents.
The first law of Road Safety is that: The accident record at a site varies inversely with its apparent degree of
danger. There is nothing new in this. Shakespeare expressed the same idea in Hamlet 370 years ago- "Best safety
lies in fear". If all road hazards looked dangerous there would be fewer accidents. This explains the rather dis-
turbing conclusion in a report by the Road Research Laboratory on the investigation of 139 rural three-way junctions
that "the better the vision distance, the higher the accident rate". At junctions where vision distance is ample the
degree of danger is not apparent and caution is abandoned. Careful selection of our roadwork is the cheapest safety
measure of all, since it costs almost nothing.

with definite regulations to prevent further air contamination, especially by the promiscuous burning which produces
air contaminants. Action has been initiated to do away with the infamous and unsanitary "town dump".

to be the modern answer to effective solid waste disposal.

towns ona voluntary basis. These clinicshave been conducted first to protect dogs against the disease and, secondly,
to have the dogs act as a protective barrier for humans.

dog six months of age or older shall cause such a dog to be vaccinated against rabies by a licensed veterinarian using
a vaccine approved by the Department of Public Health". In November of the same year, the Public Health Council
adopted a set of Rules and Regulations to accompany the above law. Essentially, the regulations require that a dog
be immunized with a licensed vaccine and a suitable tag given to the owner to affix on the dog's collar. As a result
of the above law and regulations the number of dogs immunized in the 16 Nashoba towns increased from 1,713 in 1969
to 2,617 in 1970. The number of dogs immunized in Lunenburg during 1970 was 271.
