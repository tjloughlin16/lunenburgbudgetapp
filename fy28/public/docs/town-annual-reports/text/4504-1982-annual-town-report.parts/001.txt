Board of Selectmen: Jamès T. Van Buren, Chairman Ann P. Hall and August N. Misner.
Increased costs, limited finances, legal and regulatory complexity -- these are the foes the Board of Select-
men wrestle on Monday nights. The tangled skein of the law and regulations complicate the duties of all our Town
officials, but most of all the burden falls on the Chief Executive Office. Add to this the double-edged sword of
liability and a litigious society and seemingly routine actions become subject to extensive discussion and legal
review.
The consequences are longer hours for the Selectmen, greater demands on the staff for research and paper
work, and an extraordinary demand on Town Counsel. In 1982 the Board met for 155 hours in regularly sched-
uled and special meetings. Town Counsel was present for 119 hours at 34 of those meetings. Additionally, at least
an equal number of hours is spent in preparation for meetings, not to mention another 47 outside meetings attend-
ed by Selectmen and weekly warrants to be signed on non-meeting days. The Board feels it is necessary to call
attention to these facts, since they provide the background for some proposals for management changes and
future study which are being prepared for your consideration at the Annual Town Meeting.
Management efforts of past Boards of Selectmen wisely began the process of reaching out beyond the com-
munity in order to provide the best services at the lowest possible cost to the taxpayers. Formal agreements such
as Mutual Aid for Police and Fire, Membership in the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health, and the Bi-Town
Building Inspector Board continue to make available increased services which the Town could not afford on its
own.
As a member of the Massachusetts Municipal Association (MMA), Lunenburg benefits from special services
such as VACS, a computerized parking ticket processing service for the collection of parking fines which now
come directly to the Town. Effective January 1, 1983, Lunenburg became a member of the MMA organized Mass-
achusetts Interlocal Insurance Association, which pools several municipalities for Workers Compensation In-
surance at considerable savings to the taxpayers.
4
===PAGE 7===
MMA represents the interests of local government on Beacon Hill through the initiation of legislation re-
quested by the membership, lobbying activity on proposals advanced by others, and as an intervener with state
regulatory agencies on proposals which would adversely impact municipalities. Examples of their successes
are the transfer of power for the control of County budgets from the Legislature to County Advisory Boards which
are made up of representatives from each city and town in the County; effective lobbying for increased local aid
and the implementation of Proposition 2½; and modification of proposed ambulance regulations which would
have imposed unnecessary costs to municipalities.
Less tangible, but equally important, are the many benefits derived from Town officials' participation in
state and regional organizations. Your Selectmen, Town Counsel, Police Chief, Fire Chief, Highway Superinten-
dent, Treasurer, and Finance Committee are only some of those actively involved in such organizations. These
people become a voice for Lunenburg and an influence beyond our Town lines; and in learning from the experi-
ence of others and by gathering information and ideas, they are better able to serve you at the local level.
Lunenburg seems to have undergone something like the changing of the guard in 1982. The retirement of
Police Chief Frederick B. Hobbs after 35 years with the Department, 13 years as Chief, was to be only the first.
In December, Fire Chief James P. Deming after 39 years, 19 as Chief, also retired. Their many years of service
speak well for the stability of their Departments and for them. We wish them well in their well earned years of
retirement.

As a result of action taken at the 1982 Annual Town Meeting, the Planning Board has been intensely involved
in the updating of the Master Plan. After an appropriation was voted by the Town, the Board appointed Charles
Arning as Chairman of the Master Plan Sub-committee, and then solicited proposals from over twenty well quali-
fied consulting firms, from which six were selected for interviews. The public interviews were conducted at the
Town Hall. Anderson-Nichols was selected chiefly due to the extensive master planning experience of Burk
Ketchem, the person in charge of the project. The firm has completed an inventory of the Town, held several
meetings with the Board, and individually with department heads and private citizens. Public workshops have
been held to solicit input so that the plan will be the Town's plan and not the Planning Board's.
The Planning Board will not present any major changes in Lunenburg's operation until the 1984 Annual Town
Meeting to allow for further input on the proposed Master Plan update. This updating is important because of the
recommendations that result and the process itself. The process causes more communication than normal be-
tween town citizens, boards and employees. It further allows citizens to express their desire of where Lunenburg
should be in the future. The cooperation of all involved in the process is greatly appreciated by this Board.
At the 1982 Annual Town Meeting the Board presented, and the Meeting voted, a comprehensive Flood Plain
mapping and zoning provision. The Federal Government mandated that we adopt a regulation to deal with building
within flood plains. The 100-year flood plain areas are subject to the requirement of special permits for any building,
filling or excavating. This will allow the Planning Board to assure the protection of public health, safety, general
welfare and property and life, as well as preserve the natural flood control and storage characteristics, ground
water table and water recharge areas.
The driveway regulations in the Zoning By-law were revised to close a loophole that existed in the section
adopted in 1981. Driveways are now required to be located through the frontage and access strip of the lot on
which the building is located. This will help prevent those trying to circumvent the Subdivision Control Law. It was
voted to pass over the tractor-trailer regulation article due to the inclusion of vehicles not intended to be covered,
as well as to provide time for clarifying the language of the regulation. This problem should be addressed after the
Master Plan is adopted.

STATE MANDATED REVALUATION AND HOW LUNENBURG COMPLIED WITH THE LAW.
In 1978 the State Supreme Court ruled that all cities and towns must value all taxable property at 100% full
and fair of market value, (the Sudbury Decision) and that this mandate must be completed no later than Fiscal
Tax year 1983.
The State Department of Local Assessment came to Lunenburg in 1981 and checked our real estate sales
for the last three years and found that our property was valued between 40% to 50% of full market value. They
also noted that even though Lunenburg had not had a revaluation for twelve years, our property values were well
maintained.
9
===PAGE 12===
We were then notified by the State Department of Corporation & Taxation that Lunenburg would have to
have a complete revaluation of all Personal, Commercial and Residential property and that this revaluation, when
completed, would have to be certified by the State. All values must be between 90 and 110% in order to receive
certification, and Lunenburg would not be able to send out their Fiscal 1983 tax bills until this certification was
approved.
The Board of Assessors met with Jane Malme, Commissioner, to discuss ways that Lunenburg could comply
with the 100% full value law and after much deliberation the Board of Assessors voted to put two articles in the
1981 Annual Town Meeting Warrant for the purpose of a complete revaluation. The first article would have the
Assessors value the Town with professional assistance for Commercial and Personal property and the second
article called for a complete revaluation by a professional firm. The voters approved the second article.
The Town received three bids by Revaluation companies and your Assessors selected the firm of Finnegan
Associates. The work was started in October 1981 and after eighteen months and several meetings with the
Bureau of Local Assessment, complying with their many demands, the revaluation was completed. The State
Department of Corporation and Taxation certified that the new values were at 106% of full market value on De-
cember 15, 1982.
On December 20, 1982 the Town Clerk's office mailed out the new tax bills. Last year's tax rate was $44.00
per thousand and this year's tax rate is $19.40 per thousand.
Your Assessors would like to thank the Townspeople for their cooperation shown throughout the revaluation.

Park Commission
The year 1982 was a year of change for the Park Commission. Three new Commissioners were elected,
Martha M. Johnson, Robert A. Membrino and Joseph A. Giovino, Chairman. We wish to thank our predecessors
Richard A. Kinney, Richard J. Bishop and Armand E. St. Arnaud for their guidance and express gratitude on behalf
of the Town for their excellent performance over the years.
Proposition 2½ was the dominant force in our budgeting and the services provided to the Town. Salaries
and fixed costs increased, which necessitated cutbacks in services provided to the residents. This situation is
continuing into 1983 and to compensate, user fees and/or a reduction in services must be implemented. Most of
our facilities are in need of repair. The budgetary process has and is neglecting the capital assets of our com-
munity, which, in the not too distant future, will mean the loss of these to the residents. In an attempt to preserve
the facilities and eliminate hazards several articles will be placed on the Town Warrant.
A review of the programs offered: the Town Beach, under the auspices of Jerry Beloin, saw a decrease in
usage due to the mild summer and the increase in fees implemented several years ago. However, it still remains
as one of the most widely used facilities by Town residents. The Summer Recreational Program, headed by Dick
Stachowicz was again a model for the area. Fitchburg again won the championship of the eight-team High School
Basketball League. The Men's Softball playoff championship was won by the Lakeview Club. All Star Sporting
Goods won the divisional championship. Six teams participated in the League. The Arts & Crafts Program, Wallis
Playground, Center Playground and the Junior Basketball Program provided the Town youth with a needed recre-
ational program during the summer months. Day trips and picnics to Benson's Animal Farm, Canobie Lake Park,
Willard Brook and Salisbury Beach were well attended. Our thanks to the Southside Activities Club for donating
the use of their facilities for the children this past summer. The Junior Track Meet at Marshall Park highlighted
the summer program. This winter the Biddy League Basketball, Teen League, Men's Program and open gym
sponsored by the Park Department enjoyed its best year with attendance and usage substantially higher. A Girls'
Basketball team was entered into the Leominster League for the first time. It was coached by Sandy Flanagan.
Ron Lamothe coached the seventh and eighth grade team consisting of players from the Biddy League Basketball
playing other schools and towns. It was highly successful and beneficial for the players.
Our Park Superintendent, Greg Bingham, and his assistant, Randy Boutwell, did yeoman work in keeping our
parks in excellent condition. Preparation and coordination of the facilities for high school baseball, track, girls'
and men's softball, Little League and soccer were of the highest standards. Many nights there were over eight
games held simultaneously from Farm League Ball to Men's Softball. Our park facilities continue to provide for
picnic and recreational outings for many local groups. We wish to thank the Little League for the construction of
a refreshment stand at the Brian J. McNally Park (formerly Lesure Field). The Park Commissioners implemented
a name change to recognize Brian's courage and spirit in order to provide a model for the youth of our community
to identify with. A special thanks to all those who have participated in our programs and made them a success.

Housing Authority
The Lunenburg Housing Authority is in its thirteenth year in the operation of the facilities at 131 White Street
known as the Pearl Brook Housing for the Elderly. Presently, we have forty-three apartments occupied by single
persons and five occupied by couples.
The tenants have organized the Pearl Brook Tenants' Association which meets on a regular basis and ar-
ranges interesting projects for the benefit and enjoyment of its members. Mr. Eugene P. DesLauriers holds the
office of President. He is the principal liaison between the tenants and the Authority members.
The tenants are provided with weekly transportation. The shut-ins are provided with "Meals-on-Wheels' and
the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health provide the Well-Oldster Clinics monthly for the tenants. Home Health
Care provides household duties and shopping.
The requirements for occupancy are that the applicants be at least 65 years of age and in need of good
housing. Rents are variable in that no more than 25% of Income can be charged for rent which includes heat,
water, and electricity. The rising cost of energy makes it very difficult for the Authority to keep within their budget.
We invite anyone who is interested in our facilities to inquire at the Housing Authority Office where Mrs.
Eileen Alleva, Executive Secretary, will be available to answer questions. You may call 345-7655 or 342-5308.

Library Trustees
Library Trustees - Seated left to right - Barbara T. Murray, Kathleen M. Pliska, June Dettenrieder and
Director Elizabeth H. Burnap. Standing left to right - Edward A. O'Brien and Chairman Patrick J. Slattery.
Not present when photo was taken was Sheila A. Jalutkewicz.
How do the trustees and staff of a library know when their department has had a successful year? This is a
question that has the local library people wondering. Ordinarily, a library is said to be succeeding when it attracts
increasing numbers of people, offers more services, and circulates more materials than in the previous year.
Measured by these standards, Ritter Memorial Library has been progressing in the right direction for several
years.
Circulation of all materials has increased 29.5% in the past three years. In the first five months of FY83,
circulation has increased another 5% over the same time period last year. Due to demand, the pre-school story
hours have been increased from one to two every week, and enrollment in the children's summer reading program
in 1982 was the largest ever. It could be said that the library is experiencing the direct opposite of declining en-
rollment.
15
===PAGE 18===
In the same period of time, the cost of all library services has gone from $7.61 per capita to $8.64 per capita,
a rise of only 13.5% without even mentioning the effect of inflation on purchasing power. When the revenue from
library fines and state aid (granted because the library meets specific standards for personnel, hours open, and
amount expended on books) is deducted from the budget, the cost per capita in FY82 was only $7.70, much less
than the average price of one book.
Keeping expenses down, as required by Proposition 2½, was-accomplished by reducing staff, cutting open
hours, buying fewer books, economizing on supplies, and keeping the building very cool in winter. More success
the library has succeeded in living with 2½! But the dual successes of staying within the budget and increasing
services cannot add up to a successful operation indefinitely. A 29.5% increase in circulation means a 29.5%
increase in the amount of work to be done checking materials out and back in, and an increase in wear and tear
on all library materials and property.
One reason that the library has been able to cope as well as it has is the continuing outstanding support by
the Friends of the Library. Their fund-raising projects included a yard sale, a book sale, two roller skating parties,
a boutique table, and rental books. They sponsored the annual Children's Art Festival and "Third Thursday", a
monthly series for adults featuring International Nights, movies, and speakers. With the School Department, they
co-sponsored a visit from children's author, Lois Lowry, who spoke informally at the Middle School and then met
with interested youngsters at Ritter Memorial Library. The Friends gave volunteer assistance at various library
functions and provided refreshments and materials for numerous programs. They paid for all performers for the
summer reading program events, as well as awards at the end of the program. The appearance of the upper level
of the building has been greatly improved by the Friends' gift of end panels for the book shelves. Passes to the
Fine Arts, DeCordova, and Children's museums, and the New England Aquarium have been purchased by the
Friends and can be borrowed from the library for 24 hours by any adult patron.
Children's services, under. the direction of Emily Foster, continued to be a major part of the library operation.
In addition to the four pre-school story hours, there are story hours for Bon Jour Nursery School and for each
Primary School class. Following the great success of "Lois Lowry Day", plans are underway for a similar event
next spring with another author and the help of an award of $200 already received from the Massachusetts Arts
Lottery. The very popular summer reading program, with the theme "Treasures for the Taking", attracted 145
children who read 3,910 books. Summer activities included: a clown show, movies, puppet show, a chalk-drawing
contest, a magician, and a treasure hunt. Throughout the year, craft workshops and movies for various age groups
rounded out a complete schedule of children's services.
The Trustees sponsored a logo-design contest with the winning entry being submitted by Betsy Rivers. The
new logo will appear on bookmarks, stationery, and other items in the library, and is featured on the new sign
being placed at the corner of Massachusetts Avenue and Leominster Road. Painting of the sign has been donated
by Ken-Bon Sign Service, and the planting around it was made possible by a substantial gift from the Lunenburg
Memorial Fund. Other gifts received this year included: a subscription to "Fine Woodworking" from the Lunenburg
Center for the Arts and Crafts in memory of Vincent Loiselle, donations of money from the Pearl Brook Association
and the Montachusett Area Newcomer's Club, and new and used books from persons too numerous to list.
The burgeoning circulation figures include: 24,293 items borrowed from the adult collection; 27,157 from
the children's collection; and 1,789 from the Young Adult section, for a total circulation of 53,239. In addition to
books, materials borrowed included magazines, records, 16mm films and projector, pamphlets, museum passes,
and children's filmstrips and puzzles. Many other materials and services were used in the library --- newspapers,
telephone directories, college catalogs, high school yearbooks, maps, and of particular importance, the photo-
copier.
Before the end of FY81, when circulation had increased almost 8,000 items in a single year, the need for
more staff hours was obvious, but with the passage of Proposition 2½, it has been necessary to manage with
even less help. Townspeople must realize that this situation is reaching a critical point. If the library that they have
supported for so many years is to continue to provide the services that the Town expects, the means must be
found to put more staff hours in the budget. The only alternative seems to be deliberately to discourage use of the
library, and the staff and Trustees really can't picture themselves planning to do that!

Time Magazine recently emphasized that public works budget-cutting began not with President Reagan but
nearly two decades ago. In 1965, 3.6% of the United States' gross national product was being invested in public
works facilities. This percentage has been steadily declining since that date and now stands at 1.7%. This con-
stitutes a reduction of 52.8%. Is it any wonder, then, that our roads, bridges, sewerage systems, water facilities
and public buildings are wearing out?
The Council of State Planning Agencies warns that: "The deteriorated conditions of basic facilities that
underpin the economy will prove a critical bottleneck to national economic renewal during this decade unless
we can find ways to finance public works."
(Excerpt from Editorial, American Public Works Association.)

In spite of Proposition 2½, the Town has experienced little in the way of service cuts due to the loyalty of our
employees, even though their numbers have been thinned by budget related layoffs.
After conducting many meetings and obtaining as much input as possible from various boards, committees
and the Town employees, the Personnel Committee has decided on the Salary and Wage Classification Schedule
as presented in the Finance Committee Report and proposed for adoption at the 1983 Annual Town Meeting. The
Personnel Committee is acutely aware of the severe financial problems faced by the Town at this time, and realizes
that the proposed Schedule represents a slightly greater increase than strictest attention to budgetary constraints
would dictate; but also feels that it represents the minimum increase which would be fair and consistent with the
absolute needs of the Town employees.

During 1982 the Inspector of Plumbing and Gas Fittings made a total of 158 inspections including 38 for
new homes, 30 for remodeling, 6 for solar heat installations, 4 for commercial buildings, 54 gas permits for
various gas appliances, 3 for commercial buildings, 15 bottled gas installations and 8 informational calls.
Last year plumbing permits for new construction were still slow due to high interest rates and inflation, but
remodeling and replacement continued at a steady pace. The demand for gas fired hot water tanks is still notice-
able with electric rates and oil prices still out of proportion. The homeowner is being forced to convert from the
use of tankless type domestic hot water to a more economical storage tank type.
Solar and energy saving hot water tank installations are also on the rise. Any device or appliance for heating
and storing water must comply with Chapter 142 of the General Laws.
Natural gas as an energy fuel is still in demand in the area. A moratorium was placed on all industrial and
residential heating equipment. Gas for gas replacements and the piping of domestic hot water tanks is still per-
mitted, but only after authorization and log numbers issued to the gas fitters. Due to the energy emergency brought
about by the severe cold last winter, Boston Gas Company and Fitchburg Gas and Electric Light Company, our
natural gas distributors, ran into supply problems and were forced to temporarily suspend the installation of new
gas and conversion heating systems. Liquefied petroleum gas installations were also on the increase. It was used
as a supplement in some buildings when natural gas was not available.

Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District
Nineteen hundred eighty-two was a busy year for Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School, some
of the busy-ness new, some carried over from previous years.
Graduation in June saw two hundred twenty-five graduates receive diplomas while black skies and cold
breezes prevailed, but the heavy rain held off until completion of the outdoor ceremony. Six students from the
post graduate Dental Laboratory Technician program graduated in a separate ceremony which marked the closing
of the last post graduate program at Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School.
One thousand eighty-five students were present on opening day of the new school year in August, 1982. The
freshman class of three hundred twenty-seven students was the second largest in the history of the school.
Due to the funding received from approved grant proposals, Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical
School is proud to be able to offer - once again - programs for the aspiring Dental Assistant and Medical Assistant.
These programs were originally offered at the post graduate level. The new programs are for high school students,
grades 9 through 12.
The school was the fortunate recipient of gifts and the loan of two new cars for the Driver Education course,
a Mercury Marquis from Mathieu Ford of Winchendon, and a Mercury Lynx from Roberts Ford of Athol. Other gifts
included: a greenhouse, 60' X 24', from Thomas Sulin of Fitchburg, half of which has been erected, and will be
used as a laboratory for the Building and Grounds program; computer equipment from Digital Equipment Corp.;
a Diesel Engine from David Bingham of New Hampshire; a Kalamazoo Band Saw from E. T. Duval & Sons, Inc.,
Leominster; and other gifts too numerous to mention but just as greatly appreciated.
The annual Fitchburg Conference (formerly called the MAVA Conference) was held in June through the joint
efforts of the Massachusetts Department of Occupational Education and Fitchburg State College Department of
Continuing Education. Attendance was high and the programs offered were of the usual outstanding quality.
Negotiations with the teachers and custodians which consumed a great deal of effort and time for over a
year are now concluded.
The long-continued plans for expansion continued through the year and, to date, Orange, Petersham and
Westminster have voted to join the District. All eight towns and the City of Gardner have voted to accept the three
towns into the District leaving only Fitchburg uncommitted officially.
Once again, as for seven years, the students and staff joined efforts at Christmas time to raise money for a
local charity. The largest gift to date was raised in 1982, $728, and it was given to the Lunenburg Community
Council.
The gratitude of students, staff, and the District Committee is extended to all of the cities and towns of the
Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District for their continued support of the standards and
goals of the school, support which has made possible the delivery of high quality vocational education to those
hundreds of young men and women of the District who have selected this educational route into their futures.

Regional School District Study Committee
Article 23 of the 1982 Annual Town Meeting established the creation of a five member Regional School
District Study Committee whose function is to study the advisability of forming a Regional School District with
the abutting Town of Shirley and/or other towns in the area. Such study is to include all direct and indirect aspects
of quality of education, including financial and social.
Since the formal organization of the Committee by the Board of Selectmen on June 20, 1982, we have been
meeting weekly, with members being generally responsible for input on each of the specific areas of study in
progress. As the question of regionalization has bearing on many diverse facets of town planning, school man-
agement and enrollment projection, money management and legislation, the Committee has sought to obtain a
body of information from groups and individuals representing a broad spectrum of expertise. Accordingly, we
have met in Committee with members of the State Department of Education, Lunenburg's Superintendent of
Schools, the Principal of each School, the Lunenburg Planning Board, and the Shirley Regionalization Committee.
Members of the Committee have individually undertaken assignments, compiling information and reporting
back to the Committee. Among these assignments was a meeting with an official in Charlton, Massachusetts con-
cerning the Charlton-Dudley Regional School; a conference with the Superintendent of a small independent
school system (Harvard, Massachusetts); a study of the local real estate market; and discussions with the Fitch-
burg and Leominster Chambers of Commerce.
In addition, information was obtained from the Montachusett Regional Planning Commission, Montachusett
Regional Transit System, Center for Economic Development at the University of Massachusetts, and the Town
Clerk and Planning Board of Shirley. The Committee also requested that the Board of Selectmen reactivate the
School Housing Study Committee so additional information and figures will supplement our findings. At the time
of submission of this report, meetings are planned with the Lunenburg Board of Selectmen and Finance Committee,
and Shirley's Regional Study Committee. Visits are being set up with Nashoba Regional School, Groton-Dunstable
Regional School and the Tontasqua Regional High School. A fiscal study is in progress based on information from
many directions. The Town Treasurer is also being consulted.
We have been very interested in the study being done by Anderson-Nichols Consultants contracted by the
Planning Board. Our members have attended workshops and talked directly with Mr. Burk Ketchem. We will
continue attending subsequent workshops and review conclusions. We feel that their study and recommendations
will have a profound effect on our study.
This Committee is seeking a comprehensive view of regionalization. Furthermore, we are looking into alter-
nate ways to maintain an independent system in view of a declining school enrollment. We are not satisfied just
to accept figures and statistics, but are seeking a broad picture of the needs of the Lunenburg Schools, through
our independent research.

School Housing Study Committee
This Committee was asked by the Board of Selectmen to work with the Regional School District Study Com-

Enrollment has dropped from a 1974-1975 average of 2,164 to a 1981 - 1982 average (7 years later) of 1,722,
down 20%. This follows the birthrate trend adjusted for years from birth to grade in school. However, the school
population is consistently 30% to 35% above the number that births in Lunenburg would produce, caused by
population growth.
Data from the School Principals' records point to only a minor variation in the number coming into the schools
during each year compared to the number leaving. The number leaving Grades 9-12 is consistently higher than

possibly 20% in fifteen years, but with ten years at least before the effect of Route I-190 might be felt. That is
borne out by the time delay felt from construction of Route I-495 by such towns as Harvard and Littleton, who are
now feeling these effects about a decade after highway completion.

1975 usage for at least fifteen years.

ADMINISTRATIVE RE-ORGANIZATION:
With the loss of funds and the need to improve a K-12 curriculum revision in our school
system, the administration proposed and the School Committee accepted a complete revision of our
administration. The first point to be made about the re-organization is that all staff at each of
the three school levels is now under the direct supervision of the three principals. (See CHART #1)
Each principal is now charged with the responsibility of the evaluation, assignment and utili-
zation of all staff in each of the buildings. In addition, the student services such as special
education, guidance, counseling, discipline and athletics are the direct responsibility of the
principal to provide to each student within their buildings. Finally, all curriculum decisions are
to be the joint decision of the staff, principal and superintendent of schools.
At the Lunenburg High School the administration reorganization took into account the loss of
the department heads and greater control by the principal. (See CHART #2)
The attempt here was to introduce a new concept for administrative training. Rather than
life-time jobs as assistant principals, we are trying to train individuals to be administrators,
thus we limit administrative coordinator positions to three (3) years. In addition, these

for each subject area as we have had in the past, we have consolidated these positions into
Instructional Program Coordinators heading up several departments. These individuals also have a
