BOARD OF SELECTMEN
The Board of Selectmen continued discussions with the City of Fitchburg on the purchase of sewer and
water services and are happy to report that this matter was recently finalized with the signing of muncipal
agreements.
Still a matter of great conern to the Board is the disposal of solid waste. The existing landfill has been
kept open for as long as allowed by the State. However, closure will now take place in April of 1994.
A major restructuring of the Town's health insurance was accomplished this past year and resulted in a
reduction of costs to the Town.
Town Hall repairs continued throughout the year. The upstairs portion is now being utilized by various
departments. Many compliments have been received on the changes that have been made. The Board
will continue to seek funds to continue this project.
Jane Mahony, Executive Secretary to the Board of Selectmen, has resigned and moved to Ohio. The
Board will miss her input, dedication and sense of humor. Laura Dunbar is doing an admirable job in
keeping the office running smoothly.
Once again, the Board appreciates the opportunity to serve you and wishes to thank all those who have
helped them throughout the year.
AMERICANS WITH DISABILITIES ACT COMMISSION
The 1993 Annual Town meeting established the Lunenburg Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)
Commission, replacing the interim Advisory Committee on Disability. Members appointed by the Board of
Selectmen are Selectman Michael J.Sauvageau, Building Inspector Roger Williams, School Buildings and
Grounds Supervisor Wade Collier, Council on Aging Chairman Jean E. Hobbs, and Member at Large John
Romano, who serves as Chairman.
Using the $3,000 allocated for handicapped accessible modifications to the Town's public buildings,
necessary changes were made to entrances at the Police/Fire Station and Ritter Memorial Library, and
appropriate signs posted at these locations. A railing on the walkway at the Eagle House Senior Center is
slated for completion. The ramp at the Town Hall was funded by the Town Hall Renovations account, and
a new driveway for handicapped parking with appropriate signs was installed at the Eagle House Senior
Center through a grant to the Council on Aging.
Changes to the Town's Personnel Policy to preclude discrimination were recommended to and adopted by
the Board of Selectmen. The Commission also prepared a mailing for all private businesses in the
community, advising them of the implications of ADA and the availability of some tax credits for any
modifications to be in compliance. The ADA Commission will concentrate next on modifications necessary
to improve accessibility to school buildings, and ultimately recommend funding for these projects.
BOARD OF ASSESSORS
The year 1993 was a time for a big change toward a faster and more streamlined Assessors' office with
the additions of a new computer system. This computer, with its larger, faster operating capacity, assures
that the day to day routine of running the Assessors' office and the on-going property valuation process
moves much smoother. With an anticipated updated addition to our present hardware systems, the
processing of motor vehicle excise tax should also become more fluid.
This has also been a year of transition for the Board with the retirement of John Male. The remaining
Board members would like to thank John for his contributions of time and expertise made over many years
of service to both the Board of Assessors and to the community. The Board welcomes in his place Paul
M. Dickey. A resident of Lunenburg for several years, Mr. Dickey comes to the Board possessing a solid

BUILDING OFFICIAL
The Building Department is realizing a rapid increase in activity from the recent slack period of
construction that this area has experienced. The tremendous amount of refinancing has caused this office
to be very busy with brokers, banks and survey teams which are all a part of this picture. As most
residents know, several large scale projects are in motion at this time. However, because of their
enormity, this Department has been involved with plan reviews, pre-construction conferences and zoning

4146 for appointments, information and general building or zoning issues.
The following shows the number and types of permits issued from this office in 1993: single family homes
- 34; new commercial - 1; commercial renovations - 9; commercial additions - 1; renovations - 72;
additions - 41; garage and accessory permits - 42; demolition's - 4 and wood stoves - 28.
CABLE ADVISORY COMMITTEE
Leon Weaver, the new Chairperson of the Lunenburg Cable Advisory Committee (CAC), submits the
following report to apprise the citizens of Lunenburg of the Committee's activities.
The CAC is a standing committee of the Board of Selectman. The current members of the Committee
include: Edward Dextraze, Lance May, and Patrick O'Malley. In addition to electing a new Chairperson,
the CAC also elected a new Secretary, Patrick O'Malley. It is our hope to add a fifth member during 1994.
Our mission is to make recommendations to the Board of Selectman to help ensure that the current cable
television license holder, Cablevision Inc., complies with their duties outlined in the existing license. To this
end, the CAC meets on a monthly basis with Cablevision. Inc. During those public meetings, the CAC and
Cablevision, Inc. are available to answer questions associated with cable television service to the citizens
of Lunenburg. It is important to note, that the meetings are designed to address cable television issues
raised by any citizen of the Town, not just cable television subscribers. For example, issues discussed
may include whether Cablevision, Inc. plans to cable certain areas of the community that are not currently
being serviced by the license holder. In addition, other subscriber related issues are also addressed. The
CAC also researches and makes recommendations to the Board of Selectman on other cable television
issues that can have an impact on the citizens of Lunenburg.
In light of the new Cable Act, 1993 was indeed a tumultuous year for the cable television industry and the
viewing public. As a result of this legislation, there are still many issues that need to be resolved. This
legislation, coupled with the fact that Lunenburg's current cable television license expires in 1996, will
make the coming year particularly challenging for the CAC.
We are currently conducting contract negotiations with regard to a new cable television license
agreement. It is this Committee's profound belief that the best way to ensure the continued quality of
service and to shape Lunenburg's new agreement is for the citizens of Lunenburg to clearly articulate the
types of services they would like to see from a license holder. Therefore, we would urge all citizens of
Lunenburg (both subscribers and non-subscribers) to make an effort to attend our scheduled monthly
meetings and let their views be known. This process enables the CAC, Cablevision, Inc., or other future
license holder to clearly understand the cable television needs of the citizens of the Town of Lunenburg.
The date and time of the CAC's monthly meetings are posted in the foyer of the Town Hall.

CONSERVATION COMMISSION
The Lunenburg Conservation Commission is an appointed body of volunteers who have been enabled by
the Commonwealth to administer the Wetlands Protection Act Regulations. There are seven (7) members
all of whom live in Lunenburg. The Commission conducts regularly scheduled meetings on the first and
third Wednesday of each month for the purpose of:
*
assisting applicants with the forms used with the Massachusetts Wetlands Protection Act, Ch.
131, S. 40 and the Town of Lunenburg General Wetlands Protection ByLaw;

===PAGE 9===
conducting public hearings as required for filing Request for Determination of Applicability, Notice
of Intent and Abbreviated Notice of Intent;
work sessions to manage conservation owned property;
planning sessions to address conservation concerns;
informational meetings with interested citizens; and
enforcement of the public interests set forth in the Massachusetts Wetlands Protection Act, MGL,
Ch. 131, S. 40 and the Town of Lunenburg General Wetlands Protection ByLaw.
In 1993 the Commission issued 17 Orders of Conditions, 7 DeterminationS of Applicability, 10 Certificates
of Compliance, 1 Extension Permit and 2 Emergency Permits. These responsibilities include many on-site
inspections.
Secretarial services have been a home based operation. With the on-going renovations of the second
floor at Town Hall the Commission anticipates an office early in 1994.
Ralph Romano served on the Conservation Commission during 1993 and the Commission gives thanks
for his services. Betsy Hall-Weaver was appointed by the Selectmen. Ms. Hall-Weaver has previously
served the Commission as Commissioner and Chairperson. The Commission is pleased to have Ms. Hall-
Weaver back again serving the community.
COUNCIL ON AGING
The Lunenburg Council on Aging and the Eagle House Senior Center could not do what they do if funding
from the Town was not supplemented by volunteers, donations made to "Friends of the Eagle House,
Inc.", State grants, and services provided by other agencies at minimal cost.
Some 4,500 hours of service, worth $37,000, were contributed by volunteers in FY93. These volunteers
deliver Meals on Wheels, help at the congregate meal site, assist with social activities and with Health
Clinics, conduct craft classes, work in the office, act as handymen, train as SHINE Counselors and
represent us at meetings and workshops.
Donations to our "Friends" from appreciative clients, fund raisers at the Eagle House and by the
Lunenburg Rotary Club covered the purchase of a commercial dishwasher to comply with Board of Health
regulations. The COA was the recipient of a grant from the Executive Office of Elder Affairs to construct a
handicapped parking driveway. A contribution from Lunenburg Memorial Fund bought large print
periodicals for our lending library.
COA staff consists of Director Jeanne DeBruin, part-time Secretary Nancy Rimpas, and part-time
Custodian Jeanette Duval, paid from the Town budget, and Meal Site Manager Sandra Cole, paid by the
Green Thumb Elder Employment Program. A portion of Formula Grant funds, allocated to the COA on a
per capita basis, pays for part-time Outreach Worker Nancy Sullivan. Van Drivers Gerald Beloin, Stanley
Howard and Raymond Stone, are paid by Montachusett Area Regional Transit (MART), who also
contribute part of the Director's salary, provide the senior van, and cover all related expenses. MOC
Nutrition Program provides the on-site and home delivered meals. Nashoba Nursing Service conducts
periodic health clinics at the Eagle House, and with Montachusett Home Care Corporation provide
services for elders in their homes. The COA newsletter is published 11 times a year, thanks to support of
local businesses.
For FY93, 25,719 units of service, a 17 percent increase over FY92, were provided for 42 percent of
elders over the age of 60. The COA offers information, referrals, and counseling for elders and their
families. The meals and social programs at the Eagle House are augmented by intergenerational activities
in conjunction with Lunenburg schools, speakers, and programs on medical and other topics of interest to
elders. The Monday through Friday transportation service provides for a small fee rides for shopping,
8
===PAGE 10===
medical and other appointments, and for a fraction of the cost, medical trips to the Boston and Worcester
corridors. An Information Booklet, detailing both local and area elder services and programs, is mailed
annually to residents when they turn age 60. COA staff and volunteers conduct the periodic Surplus Food
distribution to all eligible Lunenburg residents, regardless of age.
In addition to the Outreach Worker, who visits homes of residents to check on their need for services, new
this year at the Eagle House is the SHINE Program. SHINE stands for "Serving Health Information Needs
of Elders". Trained volunteer counselors provide information on health benefits and advise on health
insurance and other benefit programs.
Council on Aging members are Chairman Jean E. Hobbs, Vice Chairman Frieda Jacobs, Raymond
DeLisle, Jane Doyle, Judith Jennison, Nicholas Rimpas, Beatrice Stepton, Frances Therrien, and Agnes
Withington. Volunteer Meals on Wheels Coordinator is Sandy Hartenstein, and Volunteer Trip
Coordinator, Elvira May Kennedy.
All programs and services at the Eagle House Senior Center at 25 Memorial Drive are open to people 60
years of age and older, regardless of residence and income. Anyone in need of elder services or
interested in any activities, or who want to volunteer to assist in any way, are asked to contact the Eagle
House, 582-4166.
FIRE DEPARTMENT
The Fire Department responded to the following calls in 1993: Good Intent - 3; Alarm Activation - 49;
Reset Box - 1; Chimney - 8; Illegal Burning - 10; Structure - 27; Investigation - 24; Outside Electrical - 11;
Brush/Grass - 15; Medical Assistance - 2; Service - 29; Motor Vehicle Accident - 7; False - 3; Hazardous
Material - 12; Ice/Water Rescues - 4; Mutual Aid - 28; Motor Vehicle - 9; Bomb Disposal - 1; Storm
Standby - 1; Rubbish - 1; and Resuce/Ambulance - 324.
At last year's Annual Town meeting, the voters gave us some "seed" money to purchase software for our
computer system that we share with the Police Department. We used that money as the matching portion
of a grant request through the Police Department and ended up receiving four times the original money to
completely update our system in addition to being able to buy our complete software package.
Administratively, this will allow us to be much more efficient. Our thanks to all who supported this request.
As I mentioned in my report last year, we are all still in dire need of some major capital items. Due to the
fact that we lost our reserve pumper at a major fire last year and the fact that building a new station seems
to be a distance away, we have had to readjust our thinking in regards to our requests for purchasing
apparatus. We have been requesting a ladder truck for the last two years with the thought of putting
Engine 4 in reserve. We cannot purchase a ladder truck without doing a major renovation to our station or
building a new one. Aside from this, Engine 4 is deteriorating so quickly that it has become a very unsafe
and unreliable truck to have in operation. Unfortunately, this truck is a very much needed vehicle as it
carries most of our water to fight fires in the outlying areas. This truck is a 1966 F.W.D. and has served
us well, but it's time to be replaced to assure everyone's safety. Given these circumstances, we will ask
for the Engine 4 replacement in lieu of a ladder truck purchase this year. We will propose to fund this
purchase via exempted borrowing on the ballot.
The ladder and station purchases still loom in front of us and they must be addressed in the very near
future. This Town is growing very rapidly and we are finding ourselves dealing with public safety issues
that we have never had to deal with before. We have to keep ahead of these problems or we will
someday soon find ourselves unable to provide adequate lifesaving services that you expect. As the
Town grows, it is paramount that our public safety capabilities expand to keep ahead of this growth.

RITTER MEMORIAL LIBRARY
Automation. That one word represents the major thrust of staff and volunteer efforts at the Ritter Memorial
Library for 1993 and 1994. The Town voted nearly $5000 at the April Town Meeting to start the process of
automating Ritter Library and the same to automate the High School Library. Ultimately, we hope to unite
the two systems in what is called a union catalog.
Ritter has had a strong volunteer program for several years now, but it has more than doubled with the
addition of 14 new automation volunteers each working about two hours every week. The job of
recataloging and barcoding nearly 25,000 books is huge. Most other libraries have their books converted
by professional services at around fifty cents per book. Our volunteers are saving us at least twelve to
fifteen thousand dollars! The Library needs some additional hardware and networking before we can
begin checking out books with a scanner, but it may actually happen in 1994.
Automation means that we can network with other libraries, will have many more access points to our
collection, the staff time saved will lead to better user services, and the people of our Town will see better
and more efficient service.
Ritter was nominated for the Public Library Associations Best Rural Library in America Award". The
nomination states: "In spite of the steady erosion of fiscal support over the last ten years, Ritter has
consistently offered a breadth and quality of library services far beyond what would reasonably be
expected. Given the stress, uncertainty, and frustration of having to constantly battle for budgets, it would
have been easy for the library staff to do the bare minimum. Yet they have consistently demonstrated
commitment, vision, and creativity, maximizing opportunities and mobilizing widespread community
support. Although Ritter Library is small, it is exemplary."

SPECIAL EDUCATION DEPARTMENT
The Special Education Department of the Lunenburg Public Schools provides services to eligible students,
ages 3 to 22 with mild to severe disabilities. Services provided include: academic instruction, academic
support, and learning disabilities tutoring. Related services in the areas of speech and language therapy,
occupational therapy and physical therapy are also provided. There are currently 187 students who
receive special education, representing 11% of the total school population.
The philosophy of the Lunenburg Public Schools Special Education Department is congruous to state and
federal law which states that handicapped students have the right to be educated with non-handicapped
peers. Special Education is seen as a means to support regular education and not to replace it. The
emphasis on special education locally and nationally is on "inclusion". A term that is used to describe the
premise that special education students should not be taught in segregated settings, but rather included in
regular education settings with support from special education staff. In Lunenburg, we have lived by this
philosophy for several years. At all levels, elementary, middle and high school, students are integrated in
regular education classes where and whenever possible. We do recognize that some students still need
separate special education services which are provided; however, the goal is to have our students
integrated as much as possible. This year a team of special educators, regular educators and
administration from the Lunenburg High School will be attending an Inclusion Institute sponsored by
Fitchburg State College which consists of a series of training sessions on the topic of inclusionary
education.
As stated previously, special education is meant to support regular education. For years students all over
the country have been inappropriately labeled as handicapped. The Massachusetts Department of
Education has provided new eligibility guidelines to prevent students from being mislabeled. These
guidelines emphasize that all efforts must be made to meet the needs of students in regular education
prior to a referral to special education. The guidelines also require that students must be found to be
disabled and that the disability prevents him/her from progressing effectively in regular education. The
Special Education Department, in cooperation with the regular education administration, has provided
training and consultation through a state grant to staff. This training will allow staff to implement
strategizing teams to develop effective ways to work with students in the mainstream. In addition, the
special education staff was chosen to participate in eligibility training for the identification process of
disabled students.
The FLLAC Collaborative is an educational collaborative governed by Superintendents, School Committee
members and Special Education Directors. The collaborative develops programs for students who are
unable to be educated in their own communities. FLLAC is able to service students from many
surrounding cities and towns. In keeping with the model and philosophy of inclusion, there are currently 6
FLLAC programs housed in the Lunenburg Public Schools. These programs provide special education
services for moderately to severely disabled students. By having these programs in our system,
opportunities for inclusion exist on a regular basis. FLLAC students can participate in many public school
activities and classes. This kind of collaboration has been mutually beneficial to staff and especially to the
students.

THOMAS C. PASSIOS ELEMENTARY SCHOOL
The Thomas C. Passios School continues to strive toward educating the total child. We utilize a variety
of instructional methods and modalities in our approach and try always to meet the individual needs of
each child. A concerted effort by our staff to continue their professional growth and development is
complimented by the system-wide inservice programs offered annually through the Professional
Development program of the Strategic Plan. We believe that the success of our programs is dependent
upon a healthy and productive partnership between the home and the school. To this end, we make
every effort to communicate on a regular basis with our parents. In addition, parents play an active role
in many of the daily programs and routines of the school.
This year, at the T.C. Passios School, in addition to the core curriculum, we have instituted three new
and innovative programs. These programs are Reading Recovery, Project PALMS, and Preschool
Outreach.
Reading Recovery is an early intervention in reading for selected first grade students who may be at risk
for future reading problems. It provides a planned, systematic, research-based program to address
problems related to early reading failure. Students are seen individually, five days a week, for thirty
minutes a day for an average of twenty weeks by a teacher who has been trained intensively in Reading
Recovery procedures. The Program seeks to prevent reading problems before they even start by
working intensively with targeted first grade students before they begin to meet with reading failure. The
Program was developed in New Zealand by an educational psychologist, Dr. Marie Clay. Since 1984
when it was first brought to the United States, Reading Recovery Programs have been instituted all
across the country. The first Reading Recovery site in Massachusetts to initiate classes was Wareham in
1990. As of this year, there are 60 communities in Massachusetts (including Lunenburg) that have
adopted the program.
This year Lunenburg has two teachers in training for the Reading Recovery Program. These teachers
are involved in an intensive, year-long, graduate program in reading through Lesley College, while at the
same time, working directly with selected first grade students. Each teacher works with two first grade
classes. With full implementation of the Program, that is, enough Reading Recovery teachers to service
all first grade classrooms, the Elementary School can look forward to greatly reducing the number of
students in the school with reading difficulties and cutting down on the number of students referred to
Special Education classes for reading problems.
Lunenburg was recently awarded a grant from the state to implement Project PALMS (Partnerships
Advancing the Learning of Math and Science). PALMS is a five-year, state-wide, systematic initiative that
encourages research-based pedagogical changes that result in increased student learning and
understanding in Mathematics and Science.
Kindergarten Teacher Lisa Stone and Fourth Grade Teacher Larry Bennett were among 120 teachers
state-wide who were trained in the PALMS methodology this past summer at a two week PALMS
Institute. Model classrooms have been set up in which pilot teachers, Larry Bennett and Lisa Stone,
share this new methodology with their colleagues.
Many programs have been made available to the staff through the PALMS grant. A series of
Professional Development courses and in-service sessions are being offered to the faculty by Dr. Richard
Bisk (Fitchburg State College), Noreen Berry (PALMS Specialist), and other PALMS specialists. Fourth
grade teachers have received training and are implementing the National Geographic Kids Net Units.
These Units integrate Mathematics, Science, Technology, and Telecommunications Second and third
grade teachers are receiving training in Science and Technology for Children kits. These kits provide
inquiry-based Science curriculum units that capitalize on children's curiosity about the world around them.

The purpose of the Preschool Outreach Day is to allow children living in the Town of Lunenburg who are

cutting edge of educational reform.
TURKEY HILL MIDDLE SCHOOL
Our student enrollment at Turkey Hill Middle School increased from 524 students during the 1992-93
school year to 549 for 1993-94. This increase is a result of School Choice and new families moving into
Lunenburg. As a result of School Choice, our students live in Leominster, Fitchburg, Shirley, Ayer and
Townsend.
Academic excellence is a high priority of the THMS. The entire staff works very hard to provide the
maximum number of opportunities for students to learn. It becomes the responsibility of the students to do
the rest. It is our hope that parents, students and teachers will work together in making the Middle School
experience a positive one for all.
The four basic curricular areas in grades six through eight are mathematics, social studies, English and
science. In grades five, six and seven, students take reading in addition to the four areas. In grades six
and seven, the reading classes encompass a wide variety of stimulating materials including challenging
novels and short stories. In grade eight, all students have the opportunity to take a foreign language.
Special subjects (art, music, technology education, home economics, health and physical education) offer
a unique element to the Middle School curriculum. These special subjects provide opportunities for
students to explore their individual interests.
Cultural enrichment programs and field trips that enhance the curriculum are provided to students due to
the fundraising by students and their families and with the help of the PTO and Arts Lottery. We are
excited about new programs for our students and parents. Project Thumbs-Up is for our sixth graders and
their parents. The PTO and FSC are co-sponsoring the all day workshop which will provide speakers in
the areas of parenting skills, health topics, self-esteem, etc. Project SOAR is an alternative educational
program for the at risk students. It is designed to help students be more successful in school, improve
academic skills, improve school behavior, improve self-esteem and work to integrate back into the more
traditional classroom. A Community Service program will be piloted this May with our eighth graders.
They will be providing services for our senior citizens in Lunenburg.
We are in our second year of PALMS, Partnerships to Advance the Learning of Math and Science.
Noreen Berry, Lunenburg's PALMS Specialist, is providing programs for parents and training for teachers
in the areas of math, science and technology. The following programs are being implemented through the
involvement with Project PALMS: a math/science resource library has been set up for teachers; Mr.
Bourque's fifth grade students are piloting the National Geographic Kidsnet program, "What's In Our
Water?" This unit integrates math, science, technology and telecommunications. Fifth and sixth grade
teachers are receiving training in "Science and Technology for Children" kits. The seventh grade students
will be dissecting and examining owl pellets.
THMS considers the whole student in its implementation of appropriate programs. Educational decisions
for early adolescents are based on the students' emotional, physical, social and intellectual development.
We look forward to educating the youth of Lunenburg.

HIGH SCHOOL
In October of 1990 Lunenburg High School underwent a rigorous accreditation evaluation by the New
England Association of Schools and Colleges. The first major accounting that subsequently had to be
prepared for NEASC was our "Two Year Progress Report", submitted in December 1992. This was a
comprehensive document which carefully detailed the status of our efforts to implement each of the
Commission's numerous recommendations. The Lunenburg High School community has taken its
responsibility to comply with the letter and spirit of the Commission's 1990 Report very seriously. There
has been a genuine commitment to the process of on-going school improvement and the work of faculty
and administration has been supported unequivocally by the Superintendent and School Committee.
Despite substantial fiscal constraints which our school district, as so many others, has had to contend,
resources essential to compliance have been made available to us. Consequently, very considerable
progress has been achieved during the past three years; indeed 75% of NEASC's 190 original
recommendations have either been fully implemented or are currently in the process of being completed.
By any standard of measure, this is a highly successful compliance rate. The Education Commission
clearly was favorably impressed with our efforts, notifying us in late May 1993 of their decision to award
LHS continued accreditation for an additional three years. The following is the text of NEASC's response
to our "Two Year Progress Report":
The Commission on Public Secondary Schools at its March 21-22, 1993 meeting, reviewed the Two-Year
Progress Report of Lunenburg High School and continued the school's accreditation. The Commission
was pleased to learn of the implementation of a process for the review and refinement of the school's
statement of philosophy and goals, and the restoration of department leadership positions. While the
report described other positive aspects of the school, the Commission was particularly pleased to note:
- the efforts to develop short and long-term plans for curriculum improvement in all academic areas.
- the expanded time that the nurse is in the building to provide services to students.
- the assignment of faculty members to the media center and use of volunteers to assist students and
support the media specialist.
- the implementation of a school-based professional development program.
- the creation of a formal public relations and outreach program.
- steps to increase opportunities for students to become involved in the decision-making processes.
- measures to ensure greater equity and consistency in the enforcement of school expectations for
student behavior.
- increased attention to the use of the results of student testing and performance assessments to modify
and refine the curriculum.
All accredited schools are asked to submit a routine Five-Year Progress Report which, in the case of
Lunenburg High School, is due on March 1, 1995. The report should provide detailed explanations of
action taken to address the following:
- take steps to provide effective and systematic curriculum coordination and articulation within and among
departments and with sending schools.
- provide adequate opportunities for instructional and curriculum supervision in all academic areas.
- develop and implement a plan for the systematic replacement of instructional equipment.
- provide remedial and developmental reading instruction and assistance.

TRUST FUND COMMISSION
The Trust Funds Commissioners, Lee Halloran, Richard Letarte and Richard Storrs, oversee a number of
funds dating back to at least 1931 whose purpose is to assist fellow residents in need and to help defray
the costs of public projects within an mile of the Town Hall. These funds were bequeathed to the Town
with these certain restrictions through direct gifts or living trusts.
During recent years, the trust funds have provided assistance to the Eagle House, purchased a flag used
in the center of Town and will be aiding volunteers to convert the Marshall Park tennis courts into
basketball courts.
If anyone is interested in making a bequest of future interests, please call Town Hall or contact one of the
Commissioners of Trust Funds.

TOWN CLERK,
TAX COLLECTOR
AND TREASURER
It seems that this year has flown by. Does it mean we were having fun or were we just too busy to
notice another year has slipped away? For the first time since taking office the Finance
Committee has not asked departments for budget cuts instead, we have been asked to submit
level services and level funded budget figures. My staff is to be commended for their diligent
efforts in getting the job done in spite of the cuts we have faced over the years. I am truly grateful
for their cooperation and support.
Due to declining interest rates many homeowners have refinanced or purchased new homes.
This has created considerably more work for the tax collector's office as each transaction requires
that a municipal lien certificate be issued.
Quarterly tax billing continues to be a blessing and a burden. We still have not borrowed in
anticipation of revenue since we have been on the quarterly billing system. However, a pattern
has developed where shortfalls in cash flow occur in the last week or so in each quarter with June
being the most significant. It looks as if we will need to borrow in the not too distant future.
As of July 1993, we were able to obtain a 100% collection rate for Fiscal Year 1993 Real Estate
Taxes. This involves either the collection of the tax or filing a tax title lien at the Registry of Deeds
on those properties with outstanding tax balances.
The Town Clerk's office was busy as usual this year with the Annual and Special Town Meetings
and Elections. Excerpts from the January, April and November Town Meetings and results of the
April and November Elections are listed elsewhere in this report.
Many changes are in store for the Town Clerk's office with the new "Motor Voter" law. This bill
when passed, will allow citizens to mail in voter registration forms to the community in which they
live, in addition they will be able to register to vote at the Registry of Motor Vehicles and other
state agencies. The state will ultimately compile a central registry and the voting status will follow
the individual eliminating the need to register to vote in the new community within the
commonwealth.
