Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District
Though school has been designed to house 1,000 students, our day-school enrollment has increased to ap-
proximately 1,200 students. During 1975, over 2,000 adults have been prepared for gainful employment through
our evening Occupational Programs. This past year, 650 students participated in our Summer Program and newly-
instituted Opportunities Program from 3:00 to 5:00 P.M. These programs are designed to provide both exploratory
activities and job-entry skill opportunities to students from the sending district who are not enrolled in this school.
Our goal is to utilize the facilities to the fullest extent possible, since developing new skills has become essential
to employment under present, difficult economic conditions. As in past years, our emphasis with the evening, after-
noon, and summer programs has been to satisfy the needs of local industry by providing occupational competencies
to individuals seeking either new skills or to upgrade themselves.
Our craft Advisory Committees from local industries have provided US with technical assistance which tends to
keep our courses updated, and have also helped in providing Co-op Work Study stations. Their purpose is to review
our courses of study, equipment, and supplies and have continuously made recommendations to update them.
For the 1975-76 incoming High School class, 378 applications were received for the 275 seats available. It
is expected that this number of applications will increase substantially for the 1976-77 school year. In addition, the
young adults who represent the largest unemployed grouping, have graduated from High School unable to enter in-
dustry for lack of skills. A tremendous number of applicants for post High School programs have been processed, with
increased pressure for more students. It is for these reasons that a subcommittee of the School Committee was formed
to recommend various means of adding to the curriculum and, thus, being capable of taking additional students. Plans
that will be considered for increased students will be the most economically feasible method of building utilization
and alteration.
To accommodate friends and relatives, three separate graduation ceremonies were held for High School grad-
uates, Allied Health Careers and other Post-Graduate Programs. Of the 325 total graduates, more than 80% are em-
ployed in the related fields for which they have been trained; approximately 20% have gone on to higher education.
Student activities started off at the very beginning of the year with the annual "Welcome Freshman Day" which
included a student carnival and introduced the new students to the 20 clubs and athletic programs which have been
organized in this school. A special orientation program for new students includeda day designed to acclimate them
to the new environment. Goals and objectives which students should begin to consider were reviewed by the Guid-
ance Department, faculty and student body representatives. Special club activities included. Two Horse Shows, Two
Drama Club presentations Promises, Promises (our first musical), and Arsenic and Old Lace; a Winter Carnival;
Homecoming Weekend; several dances; Student Council collecting coins for underprivileged children; the Yearbook;
Math Club Paper; etc.
Many thanks should be offered to all of our local legislators who participatedwith our student body, Advisory
Committees, and many of the parents of our students, in preventing legislation from seriously inhibiting reimburse-
ments to our school. The effort included visiting the legislature and developing presentations for the Senate Ways
and Means Committee, thus creating affirmative action which avoided reduction of financial State support for this
school.
This past year, our reimbursements have made it possible to reduce our assessment to the cities and towns by
$2,307,467. These reimbursements reduced the budget from $3,994,293 to actual assessments of $1,686,826. In
spite of the spiralling economy and uncontrolled increases in many services, the administration -- in preparing its
budget is mindful of a worsening economy and is making every effort to reduce assessments to the citiesand towns
without impairing the goals of the school and, at the same time, attempting to provide for more students.
After a year of the Self-Evaluation (a requisite to becoming accredited by the New England Association of
Schools and Colleges), a Visiting Team evaluated all aspects of the school, plant, curriculum, teachers, adminis-
trative structure, and educational plan, and measured the school in terms of its stated philosophy and methodology.
As a result, the school was awarded a five-year accreditation with very high commendations. The statement in the
letter from the Association indicated that the school was the first to have attempted accreditation at such an early
stage. Mr. Herbert N. Hoffman is the elected representative from the Town of Lunenburg.

It is a privilege to submit my Sixth Annual
Report as Superintendent of Lunenburg Schools.
This is the 106th such report preserved in the
Town Records and covers the year ending December
31, 1975.
This year I would like to forego the tradi-
tional review of events during the school year and
divide my report into two parts, the first part
dealing with several questions and issues confront-

on the school questionnaire distributed to commun-

PART I - SOME QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS:
1. what are the projected enrollment trends in
Lunenburg Schools during the next five years?
Assuming that net migration, building pat-
terns, and industrial development remain stable,
Lunenburg's pupil enrollment has been estimated to range between 2014 and 2052 in 1980. Enrollment
on October 1, 1975 was 2157 with enrollment declining in the first five grades and our enrollment
bulge occurring in grades 6 through 10 (There are approximately 35 more students in each one of
these grades as compared to other grades). This phenomenon is a result of a linear decline in live
births in Lunenburg since 1965 with the average dropping from 125 to 85 live births per year.
2. Does this anticipated enrollment decline mean any organizational changes in our current grade
structure and building utilization? Does it mean a cut-back in staff?
Yes. A serious look should be made of enrollment patterns prior to the 1978-79 school year. If
conditions remain stable and Lunenburg's pupil enrollment continues to gradually decline level-
ing off slightly above 2000 students, I would recommend reorganizing the school system into a
grade K-5, 6-8, and 9-12 organizational plan. Under this plan grade 5 would occupy the Annex.
Another alternative would be to simply move the 8th grade back into the Middle School converting
that school into a grade 5-8 building; however, this alternative may not be feasible number-wise
until the early 1980's.
It is also expected that with enrollment decline some teaching staff will be cut, In fact, per-
sonnel cuts have been made next year in the elementary grades. Such cuts, however, must be made
sensibly and I would caution those people who automatically correlate the loss of 25 students
with the reduction of one teacher. Mr. Barney and Mr. Ralston speak to this point in their
reports. In addition to the points they make and our commitment to reasonable class sizes and
an individual orientation to learning, I am committed to keeping class sizes at the level we
reached in 1975-76, a level that has taken us several years to attain and a level that a large
majority of the respondents in our school questionnaire did not want increased.
3. What effect would the closing of Fort Devens have on our school system?
Currently we realize about $25,000. per year in Public Law 874 Impacted Area funds. We qualify
for this money because approximately 110 of our students are from federally-connected families
with at least one parent working at Fort Devens or another eligible federal property. If Fort
Devens closes we would lose all of this money and possibly a good portion of the 110 students.

4. The school questionnaire indicated that respondents felt that discipline was the greatest prob-
lem Lunenburg schools face. Do school authorities agree with this opinion?
A qualified "yes" particularly if you are referring to the High School. Discipline is a
tricky word and means different things to different people. We do not consider Lunenburg
schools to have severe discipline problems characterized by rampant drug use, costly vandalism,
assaults, racial tension, or other hard core problems. We do not consider discipline to be a
major problem in our elementary grades.
If, however, you consider "discipline" to be an increasing lack of respect for authority, an
increase in discourteous behavior, increased apathy about learning and about the future, and an
increased lack of respect for oneself and for others then it is understandable why our local
questionnaire and the last two annual Gallup Polls indicated that the public felt discipline
was the greatest problem facing schools. Yes, like many High Schools throughout the country,
we too have had an increase in this type of discipline problem at Lunenburg High School.
Is the discipline problem on the high school level any greater now than it has been in the past?
Yes. Although we are talking about a small minority of students that minority is growing. The
schools reflect society's problems and it is a fact that more parents are giving up or simply
do not have the skills, understanding, or time to properly deal with their children's problems.
In many cases, the schools or the courts are expected to succeed when the parents have failed.
These agencies cannot succeed however without the cooperation and support of parents.
What do we do? One thing is to improve our guidance and special education services with an
emphasis being placed on an early identification of student problems and early and sustained
work with parents particularly when it is apparent that patterns within the family or among
peers may need to be altered or reversed early in the child's school years. Chapter 766, an
improved K-12 guidance program, better coordination with other social agencies, teacher train-
ing and the development of a greater range of options and alternatives for certain high school
students are means for our school system to meet this challenge now.
5. The press has given much attention this past year to the national decline in achievement test
scores. Many claim public schools have been caught "with their test scores down" and should
return to the basics. Where do Lunenburg schools stand on this issue?
Lunenburg administered the 1973 edition of Stanford Achievement Battery this past fall to
grades 3, 4, 6, and 8. In all achievement categories, our students scored above national norms,
a performance we anticipated considering the nature and make-up of our community and school
population. Although above national stanine, we felt our 3rd and 4th graders should have scored
even higher in math computation than they did.
The performance of our juniors and seniors on the Scholastic Aptitude Tests (College Boards)
was also slightly above Massachusetts, New England, and national norms.
However, it must be realized that even though Lunenburg youngsters are scoring above national
norms, these norms are lower than they were 10 years ago. In other words, Lunenburg students
have maintained their relative position scoring slightly above national norms during a general
nation-wide decline in test scores.
There is much speculation on why test scores have declined but as yet no conclusive research
findings have emerged. One of the most popular theories suggest that the student population
taking the tests has changed significantly while the press has had a field day blaming the
public schools for their failure to emphasize the 3 R's. At any rate, the criticism is healthy
and a renewed concern on teaching the basic skills and insuring that certain basic competencies
are learned before a student graduates from High School has resulted in a revision and improve-
ment of our school system's testing and evaluation program. With consultant help, a special
study committee composed of teachers and administrators has developed an updated and system-
atized annual program of measuring student achievement and attitudes. This program, in addition
to providing teachers, parents, and students with valuable achievement data on an individual
basis, will also reveal significant group data about the effectiveness of specific curriculum
areas.

Some relief is in sight. Excluding salary increases for teachers and administrators, the

PART II - WE CONDUCTED A COMMUNITY-SCHOOL QUESTIONNAIRE - So WHAT?
In June of 1975, the Lunenburg School Committee distributed a six-page questionnaire to commun-
ity residents in order to obtain information as to how citizens felt about their schools. The
motivation for such a survey was partially a result of several questions, concerns, and opinions
voiced by townspeople during the Annual Town Meeting in April. The School Committee was interested
in giving the entire community an opportunity to provide input about its schools and in so doing
the representativeness of town meeting opinions could be better evaluated. More importantly, it
was felt that the information gained from the survey would be an invaluable guide to the Committee
in its future decision-making since like most school committees, the Lunenubrg School Committee
tries very hard to be responsive to the needs of the community it serves. Approximately 5,450
questionnaires were distributed to residents over seventeen years of age and 1,028 were returned
for a response rate of 18.9%. Considering the length of the questionnaire the response rate was
relatively good.
Of the individuals responding almost 60% of those surveyed were women. Approximately 52%
currently have children in the Lunenburg School System. When those who have had children in the
school system were added, the percentage is 72.7%.
Concerning the age of survey respondents, there were three times as many individuals over 30
years of age as under 30. Sixty-one percent of the respondents had lived in Lunenburg for over ten
years, and 31% had lived in the area for over 20 years. Interestingly, only 37% of the respondents
had attended last year's town meeting, and a tendency was observed for more individuals who had
lived in Lunenburg a long time to attend town meeting. A separate analysis of sex, age, and number
of children revealed that respondents were proportionally very representative of the entire Lunen-
-91-
===PAGE 94===
burg population with the only slightly skewed component being a heavier response from women, par-
ticularly housewives in the 30 - 49 age bracket who had children in school.
The major occupational areas listed by respondents were housewife (31%), education (15%),
managerial (13.3%), self-employed (9.2%), technician (5.5%), and military (1.2%). Of the women
respondents, 44.7% indicated they had a primary occupation other than housewife.
A significant reflection of interest was indicated by the fact that more than half of the
respondents took time to write additional narrative comments on their questionnaires. The Lunenburg
School Committee and School Administration had these comments categorized and compiled in a booklet
for general viewing. These comments are particularly valuable since they shed considerable light
on the statistical data and are extremely interesting reading.
Finally, in order to obtain an independent objective analysis of the survey data, the Lunenburg
Schools retained Stuart Langton & Associates, a research and planning firm, to undertake an analysis
of the data and prepare a summary report. Copies of this report were distributed to Lunenburg
clubs, organizations, churches, and local governmental bodies. Several copies of the complete
report were made available on a loan basis at the Lunenburg Town Library and at the Superintendent's
office and a separate summary of questionnaire results was sent home to parents.

WHAT FOLLOW-UP HAS OCCURRED?
Too often data from survey questionnaires lie fallow on the office shelves of those individuals
who develop such instruments. In the case of school questionnaires many respondents appreciate the
opportunity to "talk about" or "talk back" to the school system and in this way it can be argued
such questionnaires have therapeutic value. However, what effect does community opinion transmitted
through a questionnaire actually have on school committee opinion and more importantly school
committee decision-making?
In Lunenburg, attempts were made to measure the effect knowledge of community opinion about
its schools had on school committee opinion. Besides being administered to the community, our
questionnaire was twice administered to the five-member school committee, once before the committee
was knowledgeable of community opinion and once after the committee was knowledgeable of community
opinion.
The research done by my assistant, Burt Goodrich and reported in his Annual Report, indicates
that major opinions held by the community did influence school committee opinion.
More importantly though, to date how have questionnaire results directly or indirectly influ-
enced the actions, decisions, or undertakings of the Lunenburg School Committee and School Admin-
istration? In order to answer this question it may be helpful to simply summarize the significant
results of the questionnaire and then follow that listing with several decisions and actions I feel
were a result of major opinions expressed in the questionnaire.
SIGNIFICANT RESULTS OF QUESTIONNAIRE
1. The major problems of Lunenburg schools center around individual student behavior,
i. e., discipline, motivation, alcohol, etc.
2. The most effective means of school communication is through school bulletins.
3. High School guidance particularly as supported by comments in the narratives is not
perceived as an effective element of current school operations.
4. There is a strong feeling and support not to cut back on class size, teacher sal-
aries, extra-curricular activities, enrichment programs, transportation, etc.
5. There is equally strong feeling not to increase or expand school programs beyond
their current levels.

the area of class size where a majority favored canceling small classes.

Howard F. Brock, Chairman of the Lunenburg School Committee and a member of the Committee for
the past fifteen years, decided not to run for re-election and to a sixth term this April.
During his five terms on the School Committee, Howard served as Chairman on five different
occasions. He was also the school committee representative on the Athletic Advisory Council,
served as Treasurer of the High School building addition, and was a key figure on school committee
contract negotiation sub-committees.
Since elected to the School Committee in 1961, Mr. Brock has been an important influence in
the development and growth of the Lunenburg School System. During his tenure, pupil enrollment
increased from 1555 students to over 2200 students while the school system added kindergartens,
built a new Middle School, and greatly expanded its services, staff, and programs in special edu-
cation. Howard, known for his financial astuteness, also is quick to point out that during his
fifteen years on the committee, the per pupil cost has risen from $415. to over $1300. per pupil,
the professional staff has increased from approximately eighty members to one hundred forty, and
the school budget has jumped from $634,621. in 1961 to the FY-1976 level of $2,878,767. He con-
tributes state legislation and mandates requiring new programs, collective bargaining, and the in-
flationary economy of the last few years as the major reasons for spiraling school costs.
Known for his dedication and commitment to his public service responsibilities, Howard has
freely given innumerable hours above and beyond to his school committee role. He is particularly
impressed with the changes in the role of a school committee member during the past fifteen years.

THE INFLUENCE OF KNOWLEDGE ABOUT
COMMUNITY OPINIONS ON SCHOOL BOARD OPINIONS -
As the official controlling agency of a
local school system, the school board has the in-
herent charge of reflecting and responding to
community wishes--- charge that is imbedded in
the New England Town Meeting form of government
and steeped in long tradition. As Dykes states,
"The local school board was developed by
the people to enable them to direct the
educational destiny of their children.
The local school board is the
people's voice and judgment in education-
al affairs. with it lies responsibility
for bringing to bear public wisdom on the
most important of all public concern.
In a period marked by severe economic restraint and increased public scrutiny of governmental
agencies, more and more school boards are making concerted efforts to improve community/board com-
munications. Efforts which they feel will make them more responsive to their publics and more
knowledgeable of what their publics want in and from local schools.
The following report deals with the effort made last Spring by the Lunenburg School Committee
to solicit public opinion in a systematic and scientific way through a Questionnaire to Lunenburg
Residents. The report addresses comments made by several questionnaire respondents concerning the
appropriateness and affect of such a venture and specifically considers the research question--Did
Knowledge of Public Opinion Influence School Board Opinion?
In June of 1975, the Lunenburg School Committee developed and mailed a comprehensive six-page
questionnaire to all residents of the community seventeen years of age and older. The question-
naire solicited demographic data, as well as community opinion about a wide range of topics such
as: (a) major problems with which the local schools must deal; (b) ways in which the local schools
were particularly good; (c) qualities which best characterized the local schools; (d) educational
changes in the local schools; (e) student success in the local schools; (6) student attitudes
toward the local schools; (g) curriculum and methodology in the local schools; (h) classroom cli-
mate in the local schools; (i) reducing costs of local schools; (j) improving local schools in ways
that might mean increased expenditures; (k) responsibilities of the local school board; and (l)
characteristics of "schools with quality education".
Analysis of the questionnaire instrument showed it to be reliable and generally appropriate
for the task for which it had been designed. And, an analysis of the self-selecting sample of
approximately 20% of the community (N = 1028) who responded to the questionnaire showed the sample
to be an accurate representation of the entire community, although response from females was pro-
portionately higher than response from males; and response from those persons with children
currently in school was proportionately higher than those persons with never any children in
school, or those persons who had had children in school at one time in the past.

ATTENDANCE POLICY:
A new attendance policy for students at Lunenburg High School was recommended by a committee
of teachers and Mr. Crouse, Assistant Principal, and adopted by the Lunenburg School Committee on
October 15, 1975 to become effective on November 10, 1975.
The policy was necessary to curb the current trend toward absenteeism among high school stu-
dents and industries everywhere. In all courses at certain times, classroom involvement cannot
be substituted nor can all things done in a class be measured and evaluated for academic records.
Absenteeism is also economically wasteful in that a considerable amount of money is being
spent for services not being received. In some cases, high absenteeism has slowed down the pro-
gress of a class, thus affecting the academic achievement of students who attend school regularly.
There is also the responsibility of the school to teach the values of dependability and punctu-
ality.
Thus, a policy became necessary to emphasize the importance of a students attendance.
The policy states that: "A student who is absent from school for more than 20% of the days
in a marking period will receive an "F" in courses for that period. In order for a student to pass
a course and receive credit for the year, the student must be in attendance a minimum of 150 days.
attendance. Parents will be notified by the fifth absence each marking period of the consequences of poor
"Days missed because of illness which are substantiated by a written doctor's excuse will not
count towards the 20% or 30 days. Death in the family will also be similarly treated."
A review board has been established to consider requested appeals or waivers based on possible
extenuating circumstances. The student and/or family also have the right to appeal to the Princi-
pal, the Superintendent of Schools and the School Committee.
