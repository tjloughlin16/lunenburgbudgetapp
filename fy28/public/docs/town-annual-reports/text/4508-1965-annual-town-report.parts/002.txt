Highways:
Paving the Ways
During the past year, Highway Department work was carried out under the supervision of Richard W. Brown. Born
and raised in Lunenburg, Mr. Brown has worked for the Highway Department for fifteen years, the later years in the
capacity of foreman. He was appointed Acting Superintendent of Streets in December, 1964, and Superintendent of
Streets in March, 1965.
The following is a summary of work accomplished in 1965 on the 77 miles of public roads within the Town, exclusive
of the State Highway (Route 2A).
CHAPTER 81 - (Provides for cooperation by the Commonwealth in the maintenance of Town ways)
Approximately 14 miles of bituminous surface were treated with 35,531 gallons of asphalt with 1, 959 yards of sand
for cover. Extensive gravelling was done on Flat Hill Road #2, Goodrich Street, Northfield Road and Francis Avenue,
for which 1, 673 yards of gravel were used. A total of 1 1/2 milesof gravelled sections of Burrage Street, Flat Hill Road#2
and Goodrich Street were penetrated and sealed. Bituminous patching was done on roads all over town for which 244
tons of patchwere used. Calcium chloride was appliedor Round Street, Watt Street, Flat Hill Road #1, Elmwood Road,
Goodrich Street and Gibson Street. Extensive brush cutting was done on the following roads: Lancaster Avenue,
Reservoir Road, Burrage Street, Flat Hill Road #2, Pine Street, Cross Street, South Row Road, Leominster Road,
Highland Street, Chase Road, West Street, Whalom Road and Whiting Street. Extensive drainage work was done on the
following roads: Drainage ditches installed on Hunting Hill Road, Hollis Road and New West Townsend Road. Catch
basins were constructed on Northfield Road, West Street, Youngs Road, Old Shirley Road, West Street Terrace and
Ramgren Road. Drainage work, including cleaning out catch basins all over town, amounted to $5,267.02. The total
cost of this program was $26,599.72, $20,900 of which was allotted by the State.
CHAPTER 90 CONSTRUCTION (Provides for cooperation by State and County in the construction of Town ways)
Shirley Road construction was let out to contract to Reginald Bates & Sons of Clinton. The cost of this job to date
is $24,030.98. The State contributes one-half and the County and Town each contribute one-fourth of the cost of
roads constructed under Chapter 90.
CHAPTER 90 MAINTENANCE - (Provides for maintenance of roads constructed under Chapter 90)
For the last few years, a program of applying Bituminous Concrete S.T. Mix to Electric Avenue and Lancaster Avenue
has been carried out. This year Electric Avenue was completed and approximately 6500 feet of Lancaster Avenue was
done. The Highway Department plans to continue this work on Lancaster Avenue until its completion. Sections of
Chase Road, about 200 feet, were also treated with this material. Sections of West Townsend Road and Shirley Road
were given applications of tar and asphalt, and hot-top was applied on a part of Whalom Road between the sidewalk
and roadway. The total cost of this program was $10,500 with the State, County and Town each contributing one-third.
GENERAL HIGHWAY MAINTENANCE
Road widenings which have been badly needed for some time were started this year on Burrage Street, Flat Hill Road #2
and Goodrich Street, at a cost of $4,177. It is hoped that the widening of these streets will be completed in
1966. Brush wascut on Burrage Street, Shirley Road, Flat Hill Road #2, Arbor Street, Gilchrest Street, Goodrich Street,
Holman Street, Northfield Road and Whalom Road. Roadside mowing was done on roads all over town at a cost of
$787. Special drainage projects were completed at a cost of $1,971, on Northfield Road, Youngs Road, Turkey Hill Road,
Hillside Drive, Charlton Street, Flat Hill Road #2 and Lake Front. Guard rail was replaced on Whalom Road and Lake
Front. The following roads were given a surface treatment of asphalt at a cost of $3,999.93: Townsend Harbor Road,
Pierce Avenue, Florence Street, Kirby Avenue, West Street, Pleasant Street, Goodrich Street, Burrage Street, Flat
Hill Road #2, Francis Avenue and Northfield Road. The total cost of the general maintenance program was $16,099.85
with $4,000 of this amount being appropriated and expended for road oil and tar.
SNOW REMOVAL
The winter of 1965 was considered a comparatively mild one. Nevertheless, light snow accumulation can be as
expensive to combat as a heavy snowfall, due to large amounts of salt and sand which are used to provide safe highway
travel. During the year, a total of 362.93 tons of salt and 2,339 yards of sand were used in the snow removal program.
The total cost of this program in 1965 was $20,957.13.

ROAD MACHINERY MAINTENANCE
As the Town expands, so necessarily does the work required to keep our roads in good repair. In direct relation to
the volume of work accomplished, is the wear and tear on the equipment. Extensive repairs were made to the 1961
GMC Truck, the 1963 GMC Truck, the 1946 Grader and the 1962 Caterpillar tractor. Rather than discard an old
sander, the body was rebuilt and it was used extensively. Snow plow blades were made for the Cat and the Ford
Tractor buckets, which greatly facilitated removal of snow around the Center, Fire Station, Library and Schools. The
cost of the purchase of gasoline, oil, grease, diesel fuel, acetylene and oxygen, plow blades, chains and tires is
included in the $16,517.58 expended for the maintenance of road machinery during 1965.
SPECIAL ACCOUNTS
Townsend Harbor Road was widened for a distance of about 1200 feet, and 1185 feet of pipe was installed for
drainage purposes. Approximately 3200 feet of pipe was installed to alleviate drainage conditions on Pleasant Street
and West Street. Drainage was installed on Pierce and Kirby Avenues, as part of the Whalom Drainage Program.
Trees were cut, the road straightened and reconstruction commenced on Reservoir Road.
RECOMMENDATIONS
I. Purchase of a power sweeper.
Such a purchase would--
A. Save money on labor in sweeping streets. In the oiling season, streets could be swept the same day
they are to be oiled. Normally, it takes 1 1/2 to 2 days to sweep a street by hand for an average oiling job.
B. Reduce amount expended for catch basin cleaning. Sand and debris could be picked up in early spring
before heavy rains wash material into catch basins. Drainage systems are being permanently damaged by quantities of
sand which escape from the catch basins into drainage pipes.
C. Save money in cutting back roads, releasing manpower for other work. Sweeper could be used instead
of grader, Cat tractor and dump truck to cut back roads and pick up sand, dirt and debris in one operation.
D. Be invaluable in keeping clean, places like the Center, areas around the drive-ins, Lake Front and
school parking lots.
E. Answer numerous taxpayers complaints about sand on roads. At present, we cannot afford to hand sweep
every road. A sweeper could cover many roads and do a less expensive and cleaner job. It has a circular broom which
sweeps debris into center of road, then the revolving brushes pick it up and carry it into the machine. This material could
also be used for fill in certain places.
F. Protect roadside shade trees, shrubs and lawns from salt which works its way into the soil and kills plant
life.
G. The estimated cost of a sweeper is $13,500--that is $1.84 per resident and 18c per resident if amortized
over a period of ten years. The many calls received by the Highway Department would seem to indicate that the Townspeople
are anxious to receive the services offered by this piece of equipment and would consider the purchase of a sweeper a
good investment for the Town.
II. Purchase of a roller.
A roller is absolutely necessary for good road surfacing. At the present time, we have to hire a roller and more
often than not cannot get one. The Highway Department could also construct the Town sidewalks if it had its own roller.
At the present time this work is let out to bid. Most Highway Departments have their own rollers and Lunenburg used
to have one of its own. The Department feels so strongly about the need for this piece of equipment which will cost
about $8,500, that they are willing to forego the purchase of a truck in lieu of the purchase of a roller.
III. Purchase of a saltmizer.
One truck equipped with a saltmizer can cover roads with salt more rapidly and distribute the salt more
efficiently. The big problem in snow removal is to get roads covered with salt before traffic packs the snow down.

Parks:
Getting in the Swim of It
Through the purchase of "Aqualand", a bathing beach located on the northerly side of Whalom Lake, the Town of
Lunenburg will, for the first time, be able to provide a family swimming area for the exclusive use of Lunenburg residents.
This purchase will permit our citizens to avail themselves of a swimming facility which will include supervised swimming
instruction at a guarded, clean, sandy beach.
The Park Department has under its jurisdiction, and is responsible for, the maintenance of six parksand playground
areas, three baseball and softball diamonds, three tennis courts, one ice skating pond, one outdoor basketball court,
a running track and jumping pits. Additionally, it is responsible for providing organized and supervised activities for
the benefit of the residents of Lunenburg.
Adult programs which were sponsored by the Park Department included a softball league, a summer basketball
league, tennis, a golf league, a winter volleyball and basketball program and an open gym period.
The school age programs, both summer and winter, were well attended and received with great enthusiasm. Mr.
Frederick Buckley served as Director of the program. Miss Mary Kukolich supervised the Center Playground and Miss
Wendy Stewart the Wallis Park Playground. Miss Deborah McDonald and Miss Ruth Estabrook conducted the arts and
crafts program both in the Center and Wallis Parks. In addition to the general playground activities, the summer
schedule included artsand crafts, basketball leagues for boys and girls, boys baseball, girls softball, tennis instruction,
track and several special events such as a trip to Pleasure Island and outings at Willard Brook. The winter program
included ice skating, hockey, a Biddy Basketball League and an open supervised gym program.
Special projects carried out with monies appropriated under special articles included the erection of a new flag
pole in the center of town for the sum of $740, the installation of chain link fences at Wallis and Lesure Avenue Parks
at the cost of $475, and the installation of dugouts at the Marshall Park Little League Field at the cost of $380.
Other projects which were completed dur 1965 included the purchase and installation of permanent lights at
Wallis Park and Marshall Pond, the clearing of brush and rebuilding of the infield at the Lesure Avenue Little League
Field and the clearing of an area at the Massachusetts Avenue entrance to Marshall Park for parking.
Plans for the 1966 season include the expansion of the men's golf league, the establishment of an adult evening
tennis league and to continue the work carried on at Aqualand Swimming Area.
Articles being included in the 1966 warrant call for an appropriation of $3500 for the refurbishing of the existing
bandstand and the purchase and construction of a new bandstand, $3500 for the construction of parking areas at Marshall
Park and $500 for the purchase of water safety equipment for Aqualand Swimming Area, and $1800 for the installation
of lights at Marshall Park Tennis Courts.
In 1965 the total cost of carrying out the various Park Department programs and maintaining the several park and
playground areas was $9287.64.

School Building Committee
A new School Building Committee was voted at the Special Town Meeting on September 18, 1965, following the
defeat of the bond issue to build an elementary school.
This Committee has found that a factual study of the situation cannot be accomplished in a short time because of the
dynamic nature of the school situation which altered the conditions in only a few months and is, therefore, requesting
additional time to complete its study. Among the many factors necessary to consider are the following:
1.
The Willis Report was voted into law. This legislation established lower pupil-to-teacher ratios and
authorized the establishment of public kindergartens. Both factors obviously affect Lunenburg school
planning, yet at this writing the State legislature has not voted funds to implement this program.
The most authoritative advice received by this Committee to date is to plan any new school needs
based on the requirements of the Willis Report. The implementation is inevitable.
2.
A Regional Vocational High School is in the planning stages. Area towns have voted to join in this
school project to make it close to reality. However, the bond issue for the proposed Vocational
School will not be voted until after the Lunenburg Building Committee should have completed their
study. Since the potential number of Lunenburg students involved in the new Vocational School
could be from 50 - 100, the Building Committee seems advised to ignore the possible effect. Any
proposed building scheme for Lunenburg wouldhave to be flexible enough to accommodate this small
difference in total enrollment.
3.
Enrollment figures for another school year became available and a new enrollment projection was
obtained from the School Housing Study Committee.
4.
The Elementary Annex was inspected by the State Building Inspector and the pupil capacity reduced
substantially to comply with current safety standards.
5.
This effective reduction in available classroom space plus the ever increasing enrollment in the
Lunenburg Schools create mounting pressure on this Committee and the Town to come up with an
effective solution to our school needs.
6.
Upon suggestion of the Committee and approval of the School Building Assistance Commission, the
School Administration is working up 5 sets of preliminary educational specifications. These speci-
fications cannot be available until February. Review with School Building Assistance Commission
should occur in March, then the Committee can proceed with school designand be satisfied that all
possible combinations have been examined.
Even in a relatively short period of history we all tend to alter or modify our opinions based on new factsand changing
conditions. This Committee hopes to evaluate the school housing requirements carefully and thoroughly and come up
with a recommendation whichwill be the best for the education of the children and within the Town's ability to support.

Superintendent's Annual Report
Upon arrival in Lunenburg in August of 1962 to assume my duties as Superintendent of the Lunen-
burg Public Schools, I took over the direction of a school system of approximately 1650 students. In
the interim, the Lunenburg school enrollment has grown to the present moment where we now have an
enrollment of about 1950. During this period no additional classroom space has been added to the
school facilities. It is obvious to all, I am sure, that simple arithmetic will show that this is the
equivalent of about eleven classrooms if one thinks in terms of a class size of 27 per space. It is
true that an addition to the Jr. -Sr. High School had been opened the previous year in anticipation of
much of this growth. The fact remains, however, that the School System has reached a point where
additional teaching spaces must be provided if we are to maintain pupil-teacher enthusiasma and morale
for the type of dynamic, challenging school system Lunenburg wants and must have in today's world in
order to prepare our youth to compete with other students from all over our country.
It is not my purpose to dwell on this problem
or to reflect bitterness over failure to take steps to
fulfill this need for additional teaching spaces during
the past year. The need, nonetheless, is here now,
a solution is needed as expeditiously as possible if
we are to provide quality education. Continued growth
is not going to stop if Lunenburg is going to be part

So much of our future progress hinges upon additional school facilities that my report must of

"IMPORTANT EDUCATIONAL FACTORS AFFECTING LUNENBURG IN THE
NEAR FUTURE"
First, in December of 1964, the so-called WILLIS REPORT was made public. Everyone knows by
now that this was a complete study of Massachusetts' Public Education System. The recommendations
contained in this study were generally approved and adopted by the legislature and signed by the
Governor. What remains is the implementation of these recommendations and a time-table for com-
pliance. Inevitably, these recommendations must affect Lunenburg as it plans for additional school
facilities.
Among its requirements is the fact that Public School Kindergartens must be provided although
it is not mandatory for parents to send them. In addition, Elementary class sizes must be reduced to
a ratio of 25 pupils to a teacher and the secondary level to approximately an average of 20. At a time
when Lunenburg must comply with these requirements if they are to receive their full share of State
Aid, this can mean the need for an additional seven or eight classrooms. Such requirements must be
taken into consideration in any future building program if we are to be planning properly. There are of
course many other recommendations but none that hit the local level more forcefully than these two.
If the Legislature can ever succeed in passing a tax bill, it is intended that the local communities
would receive greatly increased State Aid to help finance these requirements plus offering some tax
relief at the local level. In Lunenburg, for instance, if the Governor's proposal were approved, our
community would receive $340, 573. in General School Aid under Chapter 70 in 1966, as opposed to the
$92,000. we are scheduled to receive. The provisions of this law then are bound to have significant
effect on future facilities needed and possible sizeable increased financial relief to ease the burden
at the local level.
Second, the ELEMENTARY AND SECONDARY EDUCATION ACT OF 1965 is a major breakthrough
in Federal Aid to Education and its provisions will certainly have profound effect on our educational
offerings in the very near future. Federal projects under this Act have already been submitted in the
BUSINESS, SCIENCE, GUIDANCE, and LANGUAGE areas and we are about ready to submit a sizeable
project for complete Federal funding for more intense help in the areas of MATH, READING, and
SPEECH THERAPY. This latter project should enable us to give more intensive help to the under-
achiever who has ability yet does not meet his potential.
In addition, the Lunenburg Public Schools have been working in conjunction with the Mental
Health Center in developing a RESEARCH PROJECT with complete Federal Funding designed to work
with the PRE-SCHOOL CHILD in an attempt to discover and correct problems which might develop in
youngsters which would inhibit their learning progress at an early enough age before the problems
become too solidly entrenched. Work has been going on for several months now in this project which
is now about ready to submit. Hopefully, it will be approved. This would mean thousands of dollars
to Lunenburg and should enable the schools to help prevent serious learning blocks before they have
taken root.
There are other areas of this Act, particularly in the LIBRARY AREA, which remains to be explored
as soon as guidelines are received from the State. Unquestionably, Federal Aid is going to have pro-
found effect upon our educational offerings of the future and we must continue to be alert to capitalize
on its advantages.
