ROBERT LOGAN
Robert Logan was born in Glasgow, Scotland on August 23, 1889, moved to
Fitchburg in 1911 and moved with his family to Lunenburg in 1932. He served
as a member of the Board of Registrars and as an official Town census taker from
1958 unti his death and in this capacity became well known to a large segment
of the citizenry. Townspeople will remember Bob for his dedication to his
Country, his Town, his Church, and his Family, for he made outstanding
contributions to many phases of community life--the Schools, the Library the
Grange and the 4-H Clubs. This report is respectfully dedicated to his memory.

Board of Selectmen:
The Chairman Reports
Lunenburg continues to grow at a greater rate than the towns which surround it. The official state census figures
for 1965 indicate that in the last five years our Town's population has increased 15% and that our rate of growth has
been just twice as great as the average growth rate of all of the cities and towns in our area of influence.
All growing towns experience growing pains and like many other Towns, as Lunenburg has continued to grow
our tax basehas not broadened enough to allow our tax income to keeppace with rising costs. Nothing is more important
than the education of our children. We do, however, view with apprehension the high percentage of our tax bill
which is being located for schools, and conversely the low percentage which is being allocated for all other municipal
purposes. Without some form of tax relief, as school budgets continue to rise, the only way that higher and higher
taxes can be avoided is to curtail other municipal services.
It must be emphasized that programs and planning by all Town Departments should be designed within the ability
of the taxpayer to pay. In our State, the local real estate owner bears the brunt of taxation. It becomes more and more
evident that the cities and towns will be in serious financial trouble if the Massachusetts Legislature does not, in the near
future, take affirmative action with respect to providing substantial new revenues to take the pressure off local property
taxes. Therefore, as it is the only feasible plan insight, your Board of Selectmen unanimously endorses the 3% limited
sales tax on non-essential items which is currently being proposed by the Governor of our Commonwealth and urges
Townspeople to take a similar stand.
Although at first glance the December 31st balance sheet might appear to contain an unusual number of unexpended
balances, by and large, the proposals outlined in last year's report have been successfully carried out and many of the
balances reflected as outstanding on December 31, 1965, have now been expended. Included in the Town's accom-
plishments can be listed the following:

An innovation in the Assessing and Tax Collecting Departments which was instituted in 1965 is the running off of
tax lists and bills by an outside computer system. In addition to allowing faster and more accurate processing of tax
accounts, this systemwill allow comparisons which will in the future be of considerable value to the various Town Boards
and Study Committees. A Committee, which was authorized by the Annual Meeting to look into the advisability and
feasibility of contracting for an expert appraisal of taxable real estate and personal property, has carried out its charge
and a full report of its findings is commenced on Page 7 of this Report.
A Wage Classification and Salary Plan for municipal employees was adopted and a Personnel Committee appointed
to administer such plan. Another innovation was the establishment of a Development and Industrial Commission whose
accomplishments are listed on Page 59 .
The new fire truck, which was ordered after the last Annual Meeting and is being custom built, was scheduled to
be delivered on or about February 22, 1966. Although work on the truck is running a little behind schedule, it is
hoped that the Fire Department will have their new addition early in the Spring.
The Town Hall roof was replaced thus eliminating the danger of falling slates. Although renovations to the interior
of the Town Hallwere considerably delayed because of a problem regarding the heating system, practically all of the
work to be accomplished with funds appropriated in 1965 has now been completed and the rear of the Town Hall has
been converted into four attractive and functional offices. The Welfare Staff has moved into the Town Hall and expansion
of the Police quarters, which will include space previously occupied by the Welfare Department and triples the area
available for police purposes, has commenced.
Richard Brown, who was appointed Acting Superintendent of Streets in December of 1964 and who has been a
member of the Highway Department for 15 years, was appointed Superintendent of Streets. Our Board has been pleased
with the volume and quality of work accomplished by the Highway Department under Mr. Brown's supervision. Over
$13,000 appropriated for drainage in previous years, plus the $10,000 appropriated for this purpose in 1965, was used
to complete the Whalom Clearing Drainage program and correct major drainage problems which have existed on Pleasant
Street and Townsend Harbor Road. A portion of Reservoir Road was straightened and reconstructed and the reconstruction
of Cross Street Bridge was commenced. It is hoped that engineered plans will be made available early in 1966, so that
the latter project can finally be completed. Contracts were let and sidewalks were extended on Lancaster Avenue and
Whalom Road. A dangerous section of the Shirley-Leominster Road commencing at the Leominster-Lunenburg line and
continuing to a point below the intersection of Lancaster Avenue, was relocated and constructed with Chapter 90 funds.
Details with regard to an extensive Highway maintenance program carried out during 1965 can be found in the Highway
Superintendent's report.
Although the purchase of radio equipment for the Highway and Public Safety Departments was delayed pending
approval by State and Federal Civil Defense Agencies, who are sharing in the cost of same, our Civil Defense Director
has received word that approval has now been given and the equipment has been ordered. Another accomplishment
during 1965 was the purchase of "Aqualand", a bathing beach for the exclusive use of Lunenburg residents.
The focal point of Lunenburg's 1965 municipal calendarwas unmistakably the consideration and reconsideration of
whether or not a 16 room elementary school, as proposed by the School Building Committee, should be constructed in
the Pratt Street area. After two special meetings failed to give the necessary two-thirds vote, the matter was placed
on a referendumwhich afforded 2398 voters the opportunity to express their opinions with regard to this very important
matter. As it cannot be denied that additional classroom space is needed, it is hoped that the new School Building
Committee will come in with a plan which will be acceptable to two-thirds of the voters.

Assessors:
Revaluation Recommended
In the past year, two matters of concern to the taxpayerhave dominated somewhat the news at the state and local
level. We refer to the Sales Tax debate in the Legislature and to full-fair assessments combined with the revaluation
of all real estate. These matters involve the taxpayer and certain assessing practices, therefore, it is felt that some
comment thereon by the Assessors would be timely and appropriate. It is also hoped that the comments given will be
informative to our citizens.
The Sales Tax is a revenue raising proposal introduced by Governor Volpe and others in an attempt to support the
State budget and to aid cities and towns by reducing the tax burden to the property owners, In years past, the Legislature
has enacted laws which have "cost" the taxpayers a considerable amount of money. Among these laws which must be
offset by the local property tax, are those which have granted tax privileges to certain persons. As examples we cite
the exemptions granted to certain qualifying veterans and elderly persons. In 1965 in Lunenburg the total of these
combined exemptions was $49, 177, representing $4.25 of the $89.00 tax rate. We cite also Lunenburg's share of the
County expenses which in 1965 was more than doubled, the amount having been in excess of $33,000, and having
represented $3.00 on the tax rate. These but few of the many tax burdens which have been passed on to the cities
and towns by the enactment of laws, and which must be paid for through a tax on the property owner. The Board of
Assessors believes that through an enactment of a Sales Tax, the tax base would be broadened and property owners
would be relieved of some of the tax burden they now assume. In January 1965 when the Sales Tax was first introduced
the proponents showed that under the formula for distribution of the revenues obtained therefrom Lunenburg's tax rate
would be reduced $22.00. this would have obviously given relief to the property owner, in 1965 the Assessors urged
that a Sales Tax be adopted in Massachusetts. In 1966 the Board will continue in its efforts in support of a Sales Tax
and again asks other town officials and the citizenry to join in this endeavor.
Full and fair value assessments have been in the news because of a series of rulings handed down by the Supreme
Court of Massachusetts in some recent court cases. In the past it has been the state-wide practice to assess property
at a fraction of its market value. The State Supreme Court in its severalrulings now reaffirms what was originally stated
in the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, namely, "that all property shall be assessed at its full and
fair market or cash value". To make the change from fractional assessments to full-fair assessments the Assessors feel,
as does the Department of Corporations and Taxation, that it should be preceded by a program of revaluation of all
property instituted by a firm of experts. We prefer to call such a program "An Equalization Program" because it will
put all property on an equalized basis prior to the change to full-fair assessments. It is noted that the Honorable Justices
of the Supreme Court of Massachusetts also recognized such a method as a sensible and desirable procedure to follow
prior to applying full-fair assessments, for the Court, in the case of Cohen VS. the Assessors of Beverly, ordered that
the Assessors arrange with a firm of experts for an equalization program and submit to the Court for its approval. In
some cases involving full-fair assessments the Court has enjoined the Tax Collector from issuing tax bills basedon
fractional valuations or assessments. One town in Worcester County was involved in such a case, and that town incurred
expenses of about $100,000 in fighting the case.
Faced with these rulings and wishing to make the transition to full-fair evaluations in the least "disturbing" manner
last year your Board of Assessors asked for the appointment of a "Citizens Study Committee on Revaluation" to study
this question and to report back to the voters. Through this study it is hoped that the voters will receive an unbiased
report and then have the opportunity to discuss and vote on the Committee's recommendations. Your Assessors in this,
their annual report, have attempted to clarify certain points so that the citizens may vote intelligently on matters
involving assessing practices and their tax bills.

Revaluation Study Committee:
How the Study Committee Views It
The Revaluation Study Committee was appointed by the Selectmen and Moderator to look into the advisability and
feasibility of contracting foran expert appraisal of the taxable real estate and personal property in Lunenburg, for the
purpose of enabling the Assessors to revalue such real estate.
Practically everyone is familiar with the terms fair value, valuation, revaluation, appraisal and reappraisal. However,
since the Massachusetts Supreme Court's recent mandate that assessments not only be proportional and fair, but that
within a reasonable length of time they must also be brought up to full value, a considerable amount of confusion has
arisen as to the meaning of these terms and as to their interrelationship.
It is nothing new that Boards of Assessors are now and always have been obliged to assess property proportionately
and at cash value. Although most cities and towns have through the years, within their ability to do so, made every
effort to spread tax levies equitably among property owners, during the same period few, if any, municipalities attempted
to place full, cash or market values on their real property.
We feel at this time it is necessary to emphasize that the Revaluation Study Committee is strictly concerned with the
relative equality of values placed on Lunenburg real estate. We are not, and were not chosen to be, concerned with
the ratio of assessed value to true market value. It is not necessary to revalue or reappraise real estate prior to its being
brought or near, full market value. In a letter to our Committee dated May 26, 1965, Edward C. Wilson, Associate
Commissioner of Corporations and Taxation, wrote, If there has been an equalization study in the Town of Lunenburg
within a reasonable period of time and there is equalization as to valuation, the Assessors couldwell adopt a factor for
increasing the value as represented by their present level of assessment as compared to current full and fair cash value."
There is no reason why our Board of Assessors cannot, and in the opinion of this Committee they should, immediately
adopt sucha factor and increase the value of Lunenburg's real property to a level which would satisfy the Supreme Court's
mandate. This can and should be done regardless of whether or not our Town does choose to participate in a full scale
revaluation program.
In 1954 the professional firm of Cole, Layer & Trumbull completed a revaluation of all buildings located within the
Town. At the same time, a land committee was appointed to act as advisors to the Board of Assessors in arriving at new
land values. Services rendered by the above firm at a total cost of $6500 included the following:
I. Establishing of values of all buildings in Lunenburg based on 1952 building costs.
II. Furnishing Town with individual lot cards which contained--
a. Complete description of buildings on lot.
b. Description of lot.
C. Record of ownership including date and book and page of deed recording.
d. Sketch of buildings.
e. Replacement value of buildings together with amount of physical and functional depreciation.
f. Sound value of buildings.
III. Furnishing Town with dwelling specifications; measuring procedures; dwelling grade schedules; field pricing
schedules; commercial schedules and land pricing methods and charts to be used by the Board of Assessors for the purpose
of keeping assessments up to date and equitable with original revaluation schedule.

===PAGE 10===
Land values were then established by the Board of Assessors based on recommendations by a Land Committee established
in accordance with recommendations by the then Revaluation Study Committee. Values of land werethen entered on the
individual lot cards and added to sound value of buildings, and assessed values of land and buildings were established by
taking 45% of the latter total. (It is estimated that 45% of 1952 values represents approximately 35% of the 1965 values.)
It then became incumbent upon the Assessors to each year, 1. Determine value of any new buildings, using the materials
and guides furnished by the professional firm and still based on the 1952 values; 2. Determine any changes in value of
old buildings based on additions, renovations, etc. and 3. Revalue land which is subdivided by plan or deed.
The Committee was particularly pleased to note that the Lunenburg Assessors' Office is as well equipped as, if not
better than, any other Assessors' Office which they came in contact with. In addition to the card system described above,
this office is equipped with a complete set of aerial photo maps on which lot lines have been plotted and lot dimensions
and names of record owners recorded. These maps were originally plotted by Alden Marble and Associates, Civil Engineers,
and are kept up to date by the same firm who from time to time amend the maps from photostatic copies of actual deeds
which are also secured by and maintained in the office of the Board of Assessors. Map numbers are cross-referenced to
individual lot cards.
During the course of our study the following observations were made:
A. One of the most important contributions of a professional revaluation study is to furnish the Assessors with
the proper tools to work with. As can be seen by the above, the Lunenburg Board of Assessors already has these tools.
B. Another benefit associated with a full scale professional revaluation is amap system which usually results
in the "discovery" of taxable real estate which had not previously been taxed. Lunenburg already has an excellent map
system and at the time of acquisition of same a considerable amount of taxable real estate was "discovered". Because,
as was pointed out earlier, our maps have been kept up to date by trained and skilled professional engineers, there is
no reason to believe that a Revaluation Program at this time would "discover" any appreciable amount of previously
untaxed parcels of land.
C. Land values have become disproportionate (although it might be pointed out that the values would also appear
to be low, it should again be emphasized that regardless of the system used to correct inequities the ratio of assessed
values to true market values is again the strict domain of the Board of Assessors). A land map supplied at the time of
the previous revaluation is outmoded and practically useless. A new land map could easily be supplied by local civil
engineers and values upgraded by the Assessors. Again, a system for maintaining uniform land values was supplied at the
time of the previous revaluation and it would appear that such system, if revised, would still be entirely adequate.
D. In most cities and some towns a major factor in a professional revaluation is commercial and industrial
values. There is very little commercial and industrial property in the Town of Lunenburg and much of that which we have
is fairly new. Particularly because the Assessors have made it a practice to solicit professional help in placing values
on these buildings, such values would appear to be reasonably equitable.
E. Inequities in values of Idings do exist. The majority of such inequities would appear to relate to additions
and renovations to existing dwellings which are not being assessed and, to a lesser degree, to the fact that depreciation
and grade factors have not been uniformly applied. The Revaluation Study Committee is not convinced that a professional
revaluation firm is needed to correct these inequalities. Certainly without a better maintenance program than has been
carried out in the past, an expenditure of approximately $23,000 to retain a professional firm would be a poor investment.
F. A system provided by the Commonwealth of Massachusetts known as the "State Assessment System" could
be made available to the Town of Lunenburgat an annual cost of approximately $250. This system offers the following
services:
1. Constant assistance in supervision of assessing procedures.
2. Experienced staff of appraisers for assistance when needed for special appraisals.
3. Training of personnel in assessing procedures.
4. Periodic surveys of assessing conditions including the furnishing of specific recommendations.
- 8 -
===PAGE 11===
The Study Committee sent out twenty-seven questionnaires to various towns within the State who are already members
of this system. Of the twenty-four replies received, twenty-one favored the system, the other three stated that they had
not belonged long enough to judge the results. It is strongly recommended that the Town join the State Assessment System.
G. Some of the advantages of having inequities which exist corrected locally, perhaps under the supervision
of the State Assessment System, are as follows:
1. Considerably less cost.
2. Once trained, local "listers" could be retained on a part-time intermittent basis to maintain and
upgrade existing work cards.
3. Local assessors would possess knowledge of local conditions and changes both present and anticipated.
4. Provide continuity of supervision.
5. Render sounder decisions on abatements.
6. If a maintenance program was successful, tax benefits would be derived on a yearly basis rather than
waiting for a complete revaluation to be done every ten years or SO.
7. Any adverse effect on taxpayers would be more gradual.
In view of all the above facts and findings the Revaluation Study Committee does not recommend that a complete
revaluation by a professional firm be made at this time. The Committee does, in addition to recommendations given
throughout this report, further recommend: 1. That a committee be appointed to review the present assessing practices
and methods and recommend changes which would permit the correction of inequities which already exist and which
would provide a continuing maintenance program and 2. That a valuation book be printed and made available to
property owners. It is felt that such a publication would have a lingeffect on inequities and keep citizens more
aware of abatements granted.

A Combined Effort
The unusually large number of independent boards, commissions and departments which characterize the adminis-
trative structure of most Massachusetts Towns has been described as an administrative weakness. In order to overcome
this weakness more andmore towns are consolidating administrative service functions into one department for efficiency
of operation, more effective use of personnel and as a convenience to the townspeople. In Lunenburg the administrative
function of the Board of Selectmen, Board of Health, Zoning Board and Licensing Authority are combined with those
of the Town Clerk, Tax Collector, Treasurer and registrars of Voters.
One of the greatest benefits of the system in our Town has been a coordinated system of permits. The filing of an
application to do some particular job automatically alerts the administrative officer or Town employee to the probability
of the need of permits for related matters. Thus, an application for a building permit immediately signals the need for
permits in all areas of zoning, plumbing, and sanitary disposal. The requirements of a fulldisclosure of intentions and
plans as to the particular project, together with a follow-up inspection, is of tremendous help in controlling and
preventing future problems for the Town. The general public is becoming more familiarwith these requirements as time
goes on. Generally there is a realization of the personal as well as community need for these regulations and therefore,
a willingness to comply. The Nashoba Associated Boards of Health places a trained person at the Town's disposal once
a week, regularly, and more often if needed, to advise and aid US in handling problems within the areas of sanitation
and general health.
The full time Town Office Staff consists of Mrs. Mary Padula, who is elected as Town Clerk, Tax Collector, and
Treasurer and appointed as Secretary of the Board of Selectmen, and Agent of the Board of Health, Licensing and
Zoning Authorities, Mrs. Sylvia Oksanen, Mrs. Ine Pihlaja, Mrs. Edice Gaffney and Mrs. Francelina Vaughan. In
addition Mrs. Margaret Goodell and Mrs. Joyce Moisan are employed on a part time basis.

Town Counsel:
The Public Defender
At the close of 1964, one law suit in which the Town was a party, was pending in Superior Court. This suit was
an appeal from a decision of the Board of Appeals granting a special permit pursuant to the zoning by-law. The case
was tried out during 1965 with the Court upholding the decision of the Appeals Board. Although my appearance was
entered in the case as Counsel for the Board of Appeals, the burden of defending against the appeal was borne by the
person who received the special permit. This may serve to illustrate a point worth noting.
Where the Board of Appeals grants a special permit or variance, and an appeal is taken contesting the Board's
action, it is expected that if the person receiving the permit or variance is interested in protecting it, he will undertake
to bear the burden of defense. If it is an appeal from the Board's denial of a petition for variance or special permit or
other similar quest, then the burden of defending against an appeal should be ours.
There were several instances during 1965 of actual or threatened violations of the zoning by-law or the sanitary
code. In a majority of these cases a satisfactory disposition was arrived at without the necessity of court action. At
other times the Selectmen have been presented with applications for permits for particular uses of landor buildings
which posed difficult questions of legal interpretation of the zoning code to the particular matter at hand. Written
opinions were given in some of these instances after painstaking study.
The Chapter 90 project effecting the widening, relocation and repair of the Shirley-Leominster Road necessitated
the acquisition of easements for highway and drainage purposes from the abutters. Entailed in this effort were several
visits to the locus with the Selectmen and Department of Public Works engineers, conferences and consultations with
abutting owners, and in one case at least, negotiating a settlement of a damage claim with an attorney representing
one of the abutters. In addition it was necessary to examine titles to abutting lands and draft and execute easements.
Substantial legal work was done for the Conservation Department in pursuance of this department's important work
of acquiring available lands for conservation purposes. The striking thing about this endeavor is that title to vast areas
of land has been vested in the Town with comparatively little cost and in some cases, with no cost. The land acquired,
while of little tax value and ill-suited for most purposes, is highly desirable for conservation purposes.
During the early part of every year, upto the Annual Town Meeting day, considerable time and thought is devoted
to preparation for the Annual Town Meeting. The Salary Administration Plan was inaugurated in 1965. Much was
spent by the Personnel Study Committee in putting the by-law together. Correspondingly, much time and study was
invested by the Town Counsel studying the proposed by-law and meeting with and advising the Committee with respect
thereto.
The extension of sidewalks along Whalom Road was not accomplished without some difficulty. Some abutters,
disturbed in the use of their frontage, challenged the project. This resulted in some exploration and affirmation of the
respective rights and responsibilities of the Town and the abutters.
Considerable time was spent in reaching an agreement with the City of Leominster regarding the use by certain
Lunenburg residents of the 's sewage system. A new updated contract was prepared and entered.into by and between
the two municipalities. The lowering of the waters of Shirley Reservoir raised the ire of many residents of the lake
area. Appeals were made to the Selectmen in the wake of the dispute which erupted, to stay the draining of the lake.
Advice and counsel as to the Town's jurisdiction was furnished to the Selectmen as well as the parties concerned.
Of particular consequence during the year was the issue of the proposed new school in the Whalom area. The
unprecedented series of Town Meetings culminating in a so-called referendum on the question produced. a maze of
perplexing legal problems, eventually involving legal counsel for both branches of the legislature. A great many hours
of time were spent by me in research, conferences, consultations, phone calls and correspondence in order to insure
the legality of any action which might be taken by the Town.
In addition to these specific matters I was in attendance at most of the regular meetings of the Board of Selectmen
and rendered advice and opinions on routine matters coming before them. My advice and counsel were frequently sought
and given to the Town Treasurer and Clerk and just about every other Board and Department Head in the Town.

Police Department:
Bigger Quarters or Smaller Men
As the Town of Lunenburg continues to grow, naturally the work load of the Police Department reflects this growth.
The amount of space allocated to the Department to carry out the increased demands put upon it has not grown. In
recent years additional space has been my repeated request. You read in the newspapers not too long ago, what I
considered to be a possible solution to the Police Department housing problem. I feel that the time is past for merely
considering these requests, but to regard the problem as an emergency, to be acted upon immediately. Only I, as
Chief of Police, can be in a position to judge the quality of the work done by my men. Up to now I feel their work
has been excellent, under the existing conditions. How long this will continue with the lack of proper facilities, I do
not know. I would not hesitate to say that I feel the record of our Department is in jeopardy unless something is done
immediately.
To go back a bit in the history of the growth of the Lunenburg Police Department will help you to understand our
problems. When the State Police left Lunenburg, the Town appointed its first full time Police Chief. This was about
1947, and for all practical purposes the Chief's home was the Police Station. By 1951 the Department had grown to
two full time men, and the Town realized that this housing was most unsatisfactory. As a temporary answer to the
housing of the Police Department, one room was made available on the second floor of the Fire Station. This room
measured 10 ft. by 13 ft. In 1953 a communications system was installed in the Fire Station and it was felt that for
economic reasons the police should remain in the "temporary quarters". About this time, Lunenburg, like the whole
country, was beginning to show an increase in crime activity and the duties of the Department were increased to the
extent that a third man was added to the Department in 1956, and a fourth man in 1964.
The following chart compares increases in (1) The number of full time members of the Police Staff, (2) Population
and (3) Arrests, during the last ten years:

It is, however, still felt that for economic reasons, for the present the Police Department should be kept in close

addition as proposed above will meet our demands for many years if the move is made in the near future. I do hope the
Townspeople are in agreement.

Fire Department:
Citizens Exercise Care
In spite of the drought there were thirty-five fewer brush and grass fires in 1965 than in 1964, The Fire Chief
attributes this decrease to the townspeople who have obviously been more careful with outside burning. During the
year there were one thousand three hundred seventy-seven permits issued for this purpose. Townspeople are reminded
that a permit, which is required for all outside burning, may be obtained by calling the Fire Station (582-4206).
The Fire Department answered a total of one hundred and ninety-four calls, two less than in 1964. Included were
ninety alarms, a decrease of thirty-six from the previous year. A breakdown of the calls is as follows: building - 14;
brush or grass 48; service calls - 42; town dump 30; car or truck 11; accident 12; resusitator 10; electrical - 6;
mutual aid 6; needless 5; stand-by 4; false 3; rescue 2; cooking - 1.
Service calls almost tripled in 1965. These calls included checking on the malfunction of oil burning equipment
and electrical or gas service and locating odor of smoke in homes and wooded areas. The Town Dump will continue to
be a problem until a different method of disposing of rubbish is used.
Ten members of the Fire Department attended the Meadowood Fire School held in Fitzwilliam, N.H. Thisschool
is run by Chief Holbrook of Fitzwilliam and all instructors are approved by the Massachusetts Fire Department Instructors
Association. Our own drill program, at our bi-monthly meetings was also conducted by instructors from the State
Association as well as by our own men. With all the new hazards and new technology connected with fire fighting
it is felt that this is one of the most important functions of the Department.
The new truck, the purchase of which was authorized at last year's Annual Meeting, will be delivered some time
in March. This truck is expected to be a big help in our overall operation. Civil Defense approval of matching funds
on our radio equipment has been applied for and we are confident that such approval will be given.
The addition to the Fire Station that is being considered to accommodate the growing Police Department could
solve two problems at once. In addition to giving the Police Department the space it has needed for the past few years,
it will also give the Fire Department storage space that will be needed in the near future. The Fire Chief has recommended
that the Town give serious consideration to the installation of a fire alarm system, with street boxes and facilities at
the station for direct connection to all schools, Town buildings, and to commercial and industrial buildings if the owners
so desire. The alarm system would be one more step toward reducing the insurance rate in Lunenburg.
The Chief has also recommended that the Town consider handling its own ambulance calls. Last year there were
sixty-eight known emergency calls for ambulance service in Lunenburg. There were also several other requests that
would have been handled by an ambulance if the Town had owned one.

Civil Defense:
Alert Today Alive Tomorrow
The importance of Civil Defense Communications was brought home during the recent Blackout when His Excellency
Governor Volpe waited for and received his assurance through thismedium that the power failure was not an act of war.
It should be reassuring to the citizens of Lunenburg to know that when the new Base Station is completed it will afford
one hundred percent emergency power at any given time.
During the past year, our communications system has received most of our attention. Although the Emergency
Operating Shelter, which is located in the basement of the High School and houses the Base Station, is seventy-five
percent operational at the time of this writing, it is expected to beone hundred percent efficient by the time this report
is made available to the readers.
In December of 1965, the Civil Defense Office was relocated from the Fire Station to the Emergency Shelter.
Drills on communications were held on the first Monday of each month in conjunction with the State Civil Defense Program.
The Town of Lunenburg is divided into four radiological monitoring areas, each with its own monitoring team and
equipment. They are located as follows: Area 1. Shirley Reservoir - bounded by Massachusetts Avenue, Lancaster Avenue,
Lancaster and Shirley Town Lines; Area 2. Whalom Park - bounded by Massachusetts Avenue, Lancaster Avenue, Leominster
and Fitchburg Town Lines; Area 3. - bounded by Massachusetts Avenue, Holman Street, Townsend Town Line and Fitchburg
and Ashby Town Lines; Area 4. Hickory Hills - bounded by Massachusetts Avenue, Holman Street, Townsend and Shirley
Town Lines.

Tree Warden:
The Ravages of Tree Disease
The Elm Leaf Beetle has nothing to do with Dutch Elm Disease except to the extent that severe defoliation may
weaken the overall vigor of the tree thereby making it more susceptible to bark beetle attack. Dutch Elm Disease is
instead caused by a fungus which is spread by the Elm Bark Beetle. Since there is no recognized method of killing the
fungus the beetle must be killed. Although the Elm Bark Beetle spends most of its life beneath the bark of weakened
or dead elms it has two weaknesses: 1. As in thespring it comes out and feeds on the tender young twigs of elms it can
at this time be destroyed by an insecticide. 2. Because it breeds and lays its eggs only in dying parts of elms it can
also be eliminated by removal of the wood it needs for breeding.
There is nothing that adds more to a Town's attractiveness than tree-lined streets and by-ways. The ravages of
various tree diseases such as the Dutch Elm Disease have made serious inroads in the number of our shade trees. Every
year a variety of trees are purchased and planted by the Tree Department to offset this loss. It is felt that by varying
the species we notonly add interest and beauty but, because a concentrationof any single plant species invites disaster
by setting the stage for an epidemic, we also insure that future generations will not be faced with the aesthetic and
monetary losses that some communities have borne when blight has struck the particular type of tree to which their
planting program has been confined.
The control and suppression of public nuisances including Dutch Elm Disease is a major task of the Tree Department.
In 1965 approximately one hundred and twenty-nine trees were cut and removed - twenty-three samples sent to the
University of Massachusetts for testing were all declared positive. Thirty-two trees were cut by the Department of
Natural Resources, who gave our Town ten days free tree removal work amounting to one thousand dollars.

Sealer of Weights and Measures:
Watch Your Weights
Today has been described as the atomic age, the jet age and the age of new frontiers. To the weights and measures
official it has become the pre-packaged age. In view of the changes which have taken place in the fieldof buying and
selling, weights and measures supervision is indispensable.
While the large dollar values of seemingly small errors are understood by weights and measures officials, the great
importance of accurate weighing devices should also be stressed to all who buy or sell. Every scale in commercial use
is a money machine, and if accurate, it is either taking money away from the customer or giving away profits of the
businessman. It has been pointed out that an ounce is worth $1875. That is a lot of money for an ounce of anything,
but a one ounce error on a scale making 100 weighings a day, 300 days a year at $1.00 per pound will result in an
error of $1875 in one year's time.
Scales, gasoline pumps and fuel oil truck meters and other commercially used weighing and measuring devices are
regularly tested for accuracy once a year, and unannounced spot checks are frequently made. Of the 158 weighing
devices inspected in 1965, 136 were found to be correct and sealed, 20 were adjusted and sealed and 2 condemned.
Retests were made on 6 gasoline pumps and on 3 sets of scales.
After three hundred years of being confused with Arctic Seal Hunters and more recently, with the men who apply
chemicals to cellar foundations to prevent water from entering, Legislation has been passed which permits the title of
"Sealer of Weights and Measures" to be changed to "Inspector of Weights and Measures", which title is more apropos.
Other legislation which is being proposed to protect the consumer includes: (1) An act prohibiting anyone from adjusting
or readjusting the speedometer or odometer of a motor vehicle for the purpose of misrepresentation of the mileage covered
by such vehicle; (2) An act concerning the selling of motor vehicles that cannot pass state inspection standards; (3) An
act regulating minimum specifications for tires and the sale of retreaded tires; (4) An act to further regulate the identity
and quantity of commodities in package form. An Article is being included in the 1966 Annual Meeting Warrant which,
if passed, will make our Sealer, Mr. Raymond F. Butterfield, an Inspector.
In accordance with the provisions of Section 41, Chapter 98 of the General Laws of Massachusetts, all inhabitants
or persons having usual places of business within the Town using weighing or measuring devices for the purpose of buying
or selling goods, wares or merchandise, for public weighing or for hire or reward, are hereby notified tobring them in
to be tested, adjusted and sealed.

Cemetery Commission:
Improvements Cited
Many improvements were made in the two Town cemeteries during the past year. The old section of the South
Cemetery was fertilized, and all low spots filled and seeded. The improved sod condition of this section has greatly

Superintendent Earle A. Brown has continued the plotting of grave sites, drawing of prints and numbering of lots
for the newly developed areas. In addition to his many other duties, Mr. Brown has kept the machinery and equipment
in excellent condition, ready for use when needed.

Conservation Commission, Town Forest Committee:
For d Better Tomorrow
It has been written that every user of productive land is in fact a trustee, responsible for the lives and the future
of the human race, which must always depend upon the land for support. The air we breathe, the water we drink, the
food we eat, the clothes We wear and even the automobiles drive are all furnished by our natural resources. Experts
in this field believe that within a relatively short period of time - some say within fifty years - there will not be
enough natural resources to support the ever increasing population of this earth. We are told that half of the peoples
of the world already suffer from mal-nutrition and several million men, women and children die each year actual
starvation. It is predicted that great famines willoccur different parts of the worldwithin the next five or ten years.
CONSERVATION CANNOT WAIT.
Fortunately, through the use of what is known as Multiple Purpose Resource Planning, we can promote effective
use of the physical environment today, satisfying as many needs as possible, and still maintain the inherent productivity
of the physical resource base for the future. A forest resource, such as Lunenburg's Cowdrey Nature Center, has
wide array of possible uses timber production, watershed protection, wildlife protection, recreation and scenic
splendor. The development of the Cowdrey lot for these purposes is an excellent example of Multiple Purpose Resource
Planning and gives further testimony to the fact that land set aside for the benefit of future generations can, at thesame
time, be used for the enjoyment of the present generation.
The Conservation Commission and the Town Forest Committee have been very pleasantly surprised with the popularity
of the Cowdrey Nature Center. The trails, the bridle paths and the old wagon and sled roads have been used by many
people for hiking, horseback riding and picnics. This refutes the idea that people will go only where an automobile
will take them. The Girl Scouts, Boy Scouts, 4-H Clubs and the Grange are among the organizations using this area.
The Conservation Commission offers its apologies to the Grange for the reception given to them by a nest of bees and
is considering importing some friendly bees.
During the past year four hundred Boy Scouts from the Watatic District used the Cowdrey lot and an adjacent
field, owned by Ralph E. Frost, for a very successful three-day Camporee. The 4-H Clubs built a bridge over Mulpus
Brook, where the old Tote Road crosses, which is substantial enough for horses, automobiles and trucks. The Girl Scouts
have offered to identify the various kinds of trees with name plates. The Boy Scouts have also offered their services.
Many improvements are planned for 1966. It is hoped to clear Flercum Brook from Massachusetts Avenue to Mulpus
Brook, thus allowing canoeing, boating and fishing for more than a mile. Mulpus Brook flows for a mile above the
Beaver Dam, thirty or forty feet wide, through a beautiful swamp where many flowers, wild ducks, herons and other
kinds of birds can be seen. It is a unique boat ride during which a mink, beaver or a muskrat may surprise the riders.
A beaver house six feet above water, ten feet below water and twenty feet wide at the base is well worth seeing. As
the boat goes by Beaver Island one can see where the beavershave cut down frees, some of which are more than a foot
in diameter. Beaver Dam is also well worth seeing .It is a wonderful piece of workmanship and engineering that would
behard to equal. This entire area abounds with wildanimals, birds, flowers, mountain laurel and beautiful trees, and
contains almost every kind of natural resource.
The Conservation Commission and the Town Forest Committee are dedicated to protect, preserve and increase the
natural resources of Lunenburg. Included in Lunenburg's resources are wild birds, both song and insectiverous; wild
mammals and game; fresh water fish of every description; forests andall uncultivated flora; public shade and ornamental
trees and shrubs; land, and soil resources; lakes, ponds, streams, underground and surface waters and minerals and
natural deposits. Our program can be carried out only by setting aside areas within the Town suited for conservation.
Stone walls in the woods and in the fields and along the public highways are among Lunenburg's most precious
land marks but like natural resources they are fast disappearing and like our natural resources they should be protected
and preserved. These walls are an impressive Lunenburg heritage, a monument to the men and women who in the pioneer
days arose before dawn and worked until after dark with no help or aid except neighbors getting together and with no
breaks except back breaks. Many thousands of feet of stone walls in Lunenburg bordering public streets and also not
on public streets, have been taken and carted off by people who have no right to them and probably it is still being
done (police take notice).

Planning Board:
Immediate Needs and Long Range Solutions
The growth of Lunenburg over the past decade has resulted in many problems, the major of which is the need for
expanded and improved services and major improvements in community facilities.
Our long range "General Plan" for Lunenburg contains scheduled program for these improvements. The objective
of this program, generally called the Capital Budget Program", is to provide a tabulated list of the major capital outlay
requirements of our Town and a schedule for the orderly implementation of them. This program is for use of the various
Town agencies and is intended to be a framework upon which to build the future development of our community. It is
intended to be flexible and subject to some revision and updating from year to year as may be required.
Because of the drastic effect the school system growth has had upon our Town's economy, all but the most urgent of
other capital improvements have had to be postponed. It is realized, however, that improvements of vital concern to
our Town's future growth cannot be put off indefinitely and must be scheduled according to a priority of need consistent
with the Town's fiscal ability to pay.
Among the items of high priority requiring Planning Board study were investigations relative to a public sewerage
disposal system' and new and enlarged highway department facilities.
Investigation relative to the sewerage problem was divided into two phases: one concerning an immediate relief of
our problem, the other a long range solution. Concerning our immediate needs, inquiries were made to Fitchburg and
Leominster regarding the possibility of an arrangement whereby a Lunenburg collector system could be tied into either
or both of their disposal systems. Replies from these inquiries offered no encouragement for relief through these means.
Our long range approach was aimed primarily at doing sufficient work on the problem at this time to enable US to decide
upon the most suitable location and size of site for a system to satisfy our needs. The Board engaged a Boston firm as
sewerage consultants to give them professional assistance in their studies and to prepare recommendations as to the proper
type of treatment and the location and size of site to meet our needs and satisfy current state and federal requirements.
They are currently taking preliminary steps for acquiring the recommended site, either by option or outright purchase,
so that the land for a treatment plant will be available at such time as it is decided to build a central sewerage system
for Lunenburg.
The Board studied various locations throughout the Town relative to a new site for expanded highway department
facilities and has forwarded its recommendations to the Selectmen.
Planning Board members met with representatives of the Soil Conservation Service of the United States Department
of Agriculture regarding a comprehensive soil study and interpretive soils report for Lunenburg. The information available
from this study would be valuable to individual property owners and Town officials, and particularly to the Board of
Health in determining adequacy of soils for sanitary disposal systems in sub-divisions as well as on individual lots.
The maps furnished with the studies would also be of value in determining the suitability of sites for other types of
development. The total cost of this study including reports and maps of soil conditions throughout the Town would be
$4679.60, payment of which could be spread over three years. It is the Board's opinion that the Town should take
advantage of this federal program and an article will be inserted in the Annual Meeting Warrant requesting an appro-
priation of $1600 for this purpose.
Three Preliminary Plans of Proposed Subdivisions were submitted to the Planning Board during the year. The Board
had two meetings with the Industrial Development Commission and several meetings with the Board of Selectmen. The
Board was represented at the two state-wide meetings and the seven regional meetings of the Massachusetts Federation
of Planning Boards, on the School Housing Study Committee and the Transportation Coordinating Commission.
The Planning Board continues to oppose the Massachusetts Home Builders Association proposed bill (House Bill 643)
which would give the State control of specifying zoned lot sizes and we urge all residents to contact their State
Representatives and Senators on this same basis. We strongly feel that the control of lot sizes should remain with the
people of each individual town and city.

Industrial Development Commission:
Groundwork Laid
At the Town Meeting in March of 1965, it was formally voted to establish a "Development & Industrial Commission"
for the Town. Prior to this time there had been an Industrial Study Committee which presented a report to the same
Town Meeting, recommending that the Commission be established. In compliance with the vote taken under Article 8
of the Annual Meeting Warrant, a seven member committee was chosen as follows: Mendall Colburn - one year term,
John Rafferty - two year term, Almon Ruggles - three year term, William Allen - four year term, Andrew Bonnallie -
four year term, Rodney Karlson - five year term, Norman Cross - five year term. At the Organizational Meeting in
April, Andrew Bonnallie was elected Chairman, and Norman Cross, Secretary. Meetings are scheduled for the first
Tuesday of each month, but may be cancelled by the Chairman if necessary.
During 1965, the members of the Commission have tried to familiarize themselves with the Town's Protective Bylaws.
They have also been on the lookout for any commercial or industrial concerns which might be interested in moving to
Lunenburg. Their constant concern is, of course, for the firms which are already here.
The Commissionhas continued to use Mr. Thomas J. Early, a general representative of the Department of Commerce
& Development for the State of Massachusetts, as its advisor.
During the year, the Commission has been concerned with the most effective way for it to assemble information,
facts and figures about the potential of Lunenburg for Industrial or Commercial development. In this connection,
Mr. Bancroft Hall was invited to join the Commission as a consultant. The Commission members are divided into four
committees: 1. Finance Committee; 2. Publicity Committee; 3. Power & Light Committee and 4. Land and Sites Committee.
A further objective of the Commission has been to work with the local Planning Board in order to take advantage
of its knowledge and to become acquainted with that Board's projections of the Town's future,
At the present time, the Commission is involved in the project of inventorying all present commercially zoned, and
unused areas in the Town, as well as those areas that might be zoned commercial or industrial in the future.
As the year progressed, Commission members became aware of three weaknesses far as industrial development in
Lunenburg is concerned. The most significant is the fact that although there seems to be an adequate supply of water,
there is a very inadequate storage of same. Water is one of the first things industries are interested in when moving into
a new area. Secondly, there is very definitely a shortage of available property adjacent to a railroad in Lunenburg.
Thirdly, there is nothing in the Protective Bylaws to permit any land to be set aside for industry per se, The Town has
no industrial zoning as such--whether this will ever be a handicap or not remains to be seen.
The Commission's future plans are to continue cataloguing and inventorying the available property in Lunenburg,
and to present it in some readable, attractive form to have on hand for prospective customers. The Commission will
also continue to be most interested in Lunenburg's present industries, to help them to grow and prosper in a friendly
climate. Lunenburg will never be an industrial town and nobody wants it that way, but there is a great deal that can
and will be done in the future towards getting the "right kind" of business to settle within our Township.

Town Nurse:
Health is Wealth
The Town of Lunenburg Nursing Service is provided by two full time nurses who administer both School and Visiting
Nursing. The schools continue to consume a large portion of the nurses' time. A report of the School Nursing Program
will be found on Page
The year 1965 was a busy one for the nursing staffas indicated by a total of 1955 nursing visits made, classified as
follows: Bedside Nursing - 1,007 visits; Visits to School Children -447; Preschool Children Visited - 312; Infant and
Premature Visits - 103 and Tuberculosis Visits (including transportation to Worcester County Sanitorium for follow-up) - 29.
Bedside Nursing is the only service for which a fee is charged, depending on the patient's ability to pay. No person is
ever refused care because of inability to pay.
During the year the services of the Nutritionist and Social Worker from Nashoba Board of Health were used to help
several families with diet problems and also social and medical problems. "Flu Shots" were made available to all Town
employees for a small fee. One hundred and ten persons took advantage of this Nashoba Board of Health Program. The
nurses attended lectures at the Central Health Office in Rutland and also regular nurses' meetings at Nashoba.
Two 'Well-Child Conferences" were held each month in the High School Nurse's Office under the direction of the
Nashoba Board of Health. We have been fortunate in having either Dr. Nancy Hendrie or Dr. Alexandra Pittelli, both
pediatricians, in attendance. Any child, six weeks to school age, is eligible for periodic physical examinations, health
supervision, immunization against diptheria, tetanus and whooping cough. Oral polio, and the latest immunization,
measles vaccine, are also offered. Our clinics were well attended. Appointments may be made by calling one of
the nurses.
Once again, the Town is indebted to the Community Service Department of the Woman's Club which supplied
transportation for our 'Well-Child Clinics" and made layettes, mittens, sweaters and other useful articles for distribution
to our patients.

One for the Benefit of All
The sixteen member towns of the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health are: Ashburnham - Ashby - Ayer - Berlin-
Bolton - Boxborough - Dunstable - Groton - Harvard - Lancaster - Littleton - Lunenburg - Shirley - Townsend-

minimum standards of community health. The wisdom of the original decision of the member towns to form the Nashoba

Welfare Department:
Medicare Program has March 31 Deadline
The Local Agency administers five programs of assistance: Old Age Assistance; Aid to Families with Dependent
Children; Disability Assistance; Medical Assistance for the Aged and General Relief. Cost for these programs is shared
by the Federal, State and Local Governments. This office is also available to assist families in meeting a social need
by consultation or by referral to other social agencies in our area.
The majority of men and women, receiving assistance on our programs, also receive an OASI (Old Age and Survivor's
Insurance, otherwise referred to as Social Security) check each month, but the amount of the check isnot enough to cover
their living expenses and their medical needs. The 1965 Amendments to the Social Security Act increase the benefits
of current OASI beneficiaries and broaden the eligibility for OASI to new groups of individuals. Since many persons
were affected by both sets of changes, caseloads in all the categories were reviewed for adjustment of grants. Individuals
who may now be eligible for OASI benefits were referred to the local Social Security office in Fitchburg for a review
of their records. That office, which is located at 154 Main Street, near Stuart's Department Store, can be contacted
by calling 345-4183.
According to the latest figures, more than 14 million Americans who have reached their 65th birthday are now
entitled to monthly Social Security benefits. An additional 1. million persons, 65 years of age or over, are insured
for these benefits, but have not as yet filed their claim. If you are 65 years of age or older and are not now receiving
OASI benefits, contact the Fitchburg Social Security office as soon as possible to learn about the 1965 changes in benefit
rights and to file an application for same.
Even if you are not entitled to a regular monthly check, you may be eligible for the health insurance plan under
the new Medicare program. As the Medicare program does not become operative until July 1, 1966, your current health
insurance should be kept in force until that date. Application must be made before March 31, 1966 in order to receive
the medical insurance benefits offered by the Medicare plan.
In the coming year many recipients now being aided exclusively by the Welfare Department will become eligible
for many of the services covered by the Medicare plan. To what degree this willaffect the expenses of the local department
is not evident at this time, however, the Town has continuing liason with the State Department of Public Welfare and
the local Social Security office and will continue to keep abreast of the Medicare program in order to assure maximum
coverage for Lunenburg citizens.

Ritter Memorial Library:
Library Facility Praised
The year 1965 marked the first full year of operation of the expanded library. The Trustees and the Staff have
been well satisfied with the results and feel that the addition and renovations which were completed in 1964 have more
than adequately met the requirements of the Town, and if they were to plan it again, would do exactly as was done.
Comments of outsiders have been most gratifying and when the annual meeting of the Wachusett Library Association was
held in Lunenburg on October 14th, with over seventy persons in attendance, Lunenburg received many compliments
on its fine facilities and the appearance of the building.
Circulation again reached a new high, with a total of 55,873, an increase of nearly 10 percent over a year ago.
The breakdown is as follows: Periodicals 1030, Adult 17,754 and Children 37,089. Constant use by students of the
reference works in the library accounted for many thousand study hours.
Throughout the year Library Staff members and Trustees attended a considerable number of meetings. The principal
speaker at the Wachusett meeting was Dr. Lorraine Tolman, Librarian of the School of Education, Boston University.
Miss Phyllis Gilman of Campbell & Hall, book distributors, gave book reviews and a luncheon, catered by Mrs. Priscilla
Heslam, was served at the United Parish Building. Mr. Cook and the Staff attended the annual meeting of the North
Central Regional Library in Worcester, and Mr. Cook served as a panel member for the program. Miss Proctor attended
a meeting in Lancaster on Regional procedures, Mrs. Whitcomb attended a meeting in Gardner on a discussion of worth
while books to add to the juvenile collection and Mrs. Cheesman attended a meeting in Lancaster on young adult books,
as well as a meeting in Gardner as secretary of the Wachusett Library Association.

the year, and three hundred forty-seven (347) were received as gifts. To keep the collection current, two hundred
fifty-three (253) adult books and one hundred sixty-seven (167) children's books were discarded.
Eight hundred twenty-six new library cards were issued for a total of thirty-one hundred fifteen (3115) borrowers.
The use of the Regional Library facilities is also increasing. During the year three hundred (300) children's books and

Highways:
Paving the Ways
During the past year, Highway Department work was carried out under the supervision of Richard W. Brown. Born
and raised in Lunenburg, Mr. Brown has worked for the Highway Department for fifteen years, the later years in the
capacity of foreman. He was appointed Acting Superintendent of Streets in December, 1964, and Superintendent of
Streets in March, 1965.
The following is a summary of work accomplished in 1965 on the 77 miles of public roads within the Town, exclusive
of the State Highway (Route 2A).
CHAPTER 81 - (Provides for cooperation by the Commonwealth in the maintenance of Town ways)
Approximately 14 miles of bituminous surface were treated with 35,531 gallons of asphalt with 1, 959 yards of sand
for cover. Extensive gravelling was done on Flat Hill Road #2, Goodrich Street, Northfield Road and Francis Avenue,
for which 1, 673 yards of gravel were used. A total of 1 1/2 milesof gravelled sections of Burrage Street, Flat Hill Road#2
and Goodrich Street were penetrated and sealed. Bituminous patching was done on roads all over town for which 244
tons of patchwere used. Calcium chloride was appliedor Round Street, Watt Street, Flat Hill Road #1, Elmwood Road,
Goodrich Street and Gibson Street. Extensive brush cutting was done on the following roads: Lancaster Avenue,
Reservoir Road, Burrage Street, Flat Hill Road #2, Pine Street, Cross Street, South Row Road, Leominster Road,
Highland Street, Chase Road, West Street, Whalom Road and Whiting Street. Extensive drainage work was done on the
following roads: Drainage ditches installed on Hunting Hill Road, Hollis Road and New West Townsend Road. Catch
basins were constructed on Northfield Road, West Street, Youngs Road, Old Shirley Road, West Street Terrace and
Ramgren Road. Drainage work, including cleaning out catch basins all over town, amounted to $5,267.02. The total
cost of this program was $26,599.72, $20,900 of which was allotted by the State.
CHAPTER 90 CONSTRUCTION (Provides for cooperation by State and County in the construction of Town ways)
Shirley Road construction was let out to contract to Reginald Bates & Sons of Clinton. The cost of this job to date
is $24,030.98. The State contributes one-half and the County and Town each contribute one-fourth of the cost of
roads constructed under Chapter 90.
CHAPTER 90 MAINTENANCE - (Provides for maintenance of roads constructed under Chapter 90)
For the last few years, a program of applying Bituminous Concrete S.T. Mix to Electric Avenue and Lancaster Avenue
has been carried out. This year Electric Avenue was completed and approximately 6500 feet of Lancaster Avenue was
done. The Highway Department plans to continue this work on Lancaster Avenue until its completion. Sections of
Chase Road, about 200 feet, were also treated with this material. Sections of West Townsend Road and Shirley Road
were given applications of tar and asphalt, and hot-top was applied on a part of Whalom Road between the sidewalk
and roadway. The total cost of this program was $10,500 with the State, County and Town each contributing one-third.
GENERAL HIGHWAY MAINTENANCE
Road widenings which have been badly needed for some time were started this year on Burrage Street, Flat Hill Road #2
and Goodrich Street, at a cost of $4,177. It is hoped that the widening of these streets will be completed in
1966. Brush wascut on Burrage Street, Shirley Road, Flat Hill Road #2, Arbor Street, Gilchrest Street, Goodrich Street,
Holman Street, Northfield Road and Whalom Road. Roadside mowing was done on roads all over town at a cost of
$787. Special drainage projects were completed at a cost of $1,971, on Northfield Road, Youngs Road, Turkey Hill Road,
Hillside Drive, Charlton Street, Flat Hill Road #2 and Lake Front. Guard rail was replaced on Whalom Road and Lake
Front. The following roads were given a surface treatment of asphalt at a cost of $3,999.93: Townsend Harbor Road,
Pierce Avenue, Florence Street, Kirby Avenue, West Street, Pleasant Street, Goodrich Street, Burrage Street, Flat
Hill Road #2, Francis Avenue and Northfield Road. The total cost of the general maintenance program was $16,099.85
with $4,000 of this amount being appropriated and expended for road oil and tar.
SNOW REMOVAL
The winter of 1965 was considered a comparatively mild one. Nevertheless, light snow accumulation can be as
expensive to combat as a heavy snowfall, due to large amounts of salt and sand which are used to provide safe highway
travel. During the year, a total of 362.93 tons of salt and 2,339 yards of sand were used in the snow removal program.
The total cost of this program in 1965 was $20,957.13.

ROAD MACHINERY MAINTENANCE
As the Town expands, so necessarily does the work required to keep our roads in good repair. In direct relation to
the volume of work accomplished, is the wear and tear on the equipment. Extensive repairs were made to the 1961
GMC Truck, the 1963 GMC Truck, the 1946 Grader and the 1962 Caterpillar tractor. Rather than discard an old
sander, the body was rebuilt and it was used extensively. Snow plow blades were made for the Cat and the Ford
Tractor buckets, which greatly facilitated removal of snow around the Center, Fire Station, Library and Schools. The
cost of the purchase of gasoline, oil, grease, diesel fuel, acetylene and oxygen, plow blades, chains and tires is
included in the $16,517.58 expended for the maintenance of road machinery during 1965.

Townsend Harbor Road was widened for a distance of about 1200 feet, and 1185 feet of pipe was installed for
drainage purposes. Approximately 3200 feet of pipe was installed to alleviate drainage conditions on Pleasant Street
and West Street. Drainage was installed on Pierce and Kirby Avenues, as part of the Whalom Drainage Program.
Trees were cut, the road straightened and reconstruction commenced on Reservoir Road.
RECOMMENDATIONS
I. Purchase of a power sweeper.
Such a purchase would--
A. Save money on labor in sweeping streets. In the oiling season, streets could be swept the same day
they are to be oiled. Normally, it takes 1 1/2 to 2 days to sweep a street by hand for an average oiling job.
B. Reduce amount expended for catch basin cleaning. Sand and debris could be picked up in early spring
before heavy rains wash material into catch basins. Drainage systems are being permanently damaged by quantities of
sand which escape from the catch basins into drainage pipes.
C. Save money in cutting back roads, releasing manpower for other work. Sweeper could be used instead
of grader, Cat tractor and dump truck to cut back roads and pick up sand, dirt and debris in one operation.
D. Be invaluable in keeping clean, places like the Center, areas around the drive-ins, Lake Front and
school parking lots.
E. Answer numerous taxpayers complaints about sand on roads. At present, we cannot afford to hand sweep
every road. A sweeper could cover many roads and do a less expensive and cleaner job. It has a circular broom which
sweeps debris into center of road, then the revolving brushes pick it up and carry it into the machine. This material could
also be used for fill in certain places.
F. Protect roadside shade trees, shrubs and lawns from salt which works its way into the soil and kills plant
life.
G. The estimated cost of a sweeper is $13,500--that is $1.84 per resident and 18c per resident if amortized
over a period of ten years. The many calls received by the Highway Department would seem to indicate that the Townspeople
are anxious to receive the services offered by this piece of equipment and would consider the purchase of a sweeper a
good investment for the Town.
II. Purchase of a roller.
A roller is absolutely necessary for good road surfacing. At the present time, we have to hire a roller and more
often than not cannot get one. The Highway Department could also construct the Town sidewalks if it had its own roller.
At the present time this work is let out to bid. Most Highway Departments have their own rollers and Lunenburg used
to have one of its own. The Department feels so strongly about the need for this piece of equipment which will cost
about $8,500, that they are willing to forego the purchase of a truck in lieu of the purchase of a roller.
III. Purchase of a saltmizer.
One truck equipped with a saltmizer can cover roads with salt more rapidly and distribute the salt more
efficiently. The big problem in snow removal is to get roads covered with salt before traffic packs the snow down.

Parks:
Getting in the Swim of It
Through the purchase of "Aqualand", a bathing beach located on the northerly side of Whalom Lake, the Town of
Lunenburg will, for the first time, be able to provide a family swimming area for the exclusive use of Lunenburg residents.
This purchase will permit our citizens to avail themselves of a swimming facility which will include supervised swimming
instruction at a guarded, clean, sandy beach.
The Park Department has under its jurisdiction, and is responsible for, the maintenance of six parksand playground
areas, three baseball and softball diamonds, three tennis courts, one ice skating pond, one outdoor basketball court,
a running track and jumping pits. Additionally, it is responsible for providing organized and supervised activities for
the benefit of the residents of Lunenburg.
Adult programs which were sponsored by the Park Department included a softball league, a summer basketball
league, tennis, a golf league, a winter volleyball and basketball program and an open gym period.
The school age programs, both summer and winter, were well attended and received with great enthusiasm. Mr.
Frederick Buckley served as Director of the program. Miss Mary Kukolich supervised the Center Playground and Miss
Wendy Stewart the Wallis Park Playground. Miss Deborah McDonald and Miss Ruth Estabrook conducted the arts and
crafts program both in the Center and Wallis Parks. In addition to the general playground activities, the summer
schedule included artsand crafts, basketball leagues for boys and girls, boys baseball, girls softball, tennis instruction,
track and several special events such as a trip to Pleasure Island and outings at Willard Brook. The winter program
included ice skating, hockey, a Biddy Basketball League and an open supervised gym program.
Special projects carried out with monies appropriated under special articles included the erection of a new flag
pole in the center of town for the sum of $740, the installation of chain link fences at Wallis and Lesure Avenue Parks
at the cost of $475, and the installation of dugouts at the Marshall Park Little League Field at the cost of $380.
Other projects which were completed dur 1965 included the purchase and installation of permanent lights at
Wallis Park and Marshall Pond, the clearing of brush and rebuilding of the infield at the Lesure Avenue Little League
Field and the clearing of an area at the Massachusetts Avenue entrance to Marshall Park for parking.
Plans for the 1966 season include the expansion of the men's golf league, the establishment of an adult evening
tennis league and to continue the work carried on at Aqualand Swimming Area.
Articles being included in the 1966 warrant call for an appropriation of $3500 for the refurbishing of the existing
bandstand and the purchase and construction of a new bandstand, $3500 for the construction of parking areas at Marshall
Park and $500 for the purchase of water safety equipment for Aqualand Swimming Area, and $1800 for the installation
of lights at Marshall Park Tennis Courts.
In 1965 the total cost of carrying out the various Park Department programs and maintaining the several park and
playground areas was $9287.64.

School Building Committee
A new School Building Committee was voted at the Special Town Meeting on September 18, 1965, following the
defeat of the bond issue to build an elementary school.
This Committee has found that a factual study of the situation cannot be accomplished in a short time because of the
dynamic nature of the school situation which altered the conditions in only a few months and is, therefore, requesting
additional time to complete its study. Among the many factors necessary to consider are the following:
1.
The Willis Report was voted into law. This legislation established lower pupil-to-teacher ratios and
authorized the establishment of public kindergartens. Both factors obviously affect Lunenburg school
planning, yet at this writing the State legislature has not voted funds to implement this program.
The most authoritative advice received by this Committee to date is to plan any new school needs
based on the requirements of the Willis Report. The implementation is inevitable.
2.
A Regional Vocational High School is in the planning stages. Area towns have voted to join in this
school project to make it close to reality. However, the bond issue for the proposed Vocational
School will not be voted until after the Lunenburg Building Committee should have completed their
study. Since the potential number of Lunenburg students involved in the new Vocational School
could be from 50 - 100, the Building Committee seems advised to ignore the possible effect. Any
proposed building scheme for Lunenburg wouldhave to be flexible enough to accommodate this small
difference in total enrollment.
3.
Enrollment figures for another school year became available and a new enrollment projection was
obtained from the School Housing Study Committee.
4.
The Elementary Annex was inspected by the State Building Inspector and the pupil capacity reduced
substantially to comply with current safety standards.
5.
This effective reduction in available classroom space plus the ever increasing enrollment in the
Lunenburg Schools create mounting pressure on this Committee and the Town to come up with an
effective solution to our school needs.
6.
Upon suggestion of the Committee and approval of the School Building Assistance Commission, the
School Administration is working up 5 sets of preliminary educational specifications. These speci-
fications cannot be available until February. Review with School Building Assistance Commission
should occur in March, then the Committee can proceed with school designand be satisfied that all
possible combinations have been examined.
Even in a relatively short period of history we all tend to alter or modify our opinions based on new factsand changing
conditions. This Committee hopes to evaluate the school housing requirements carefully and thoroughly and come up
with a recommendation whichwill be the best for the education of the children and within the Town's ability to support.

Superintendent's Annual Report
Upon arrival in Lunenburg in August of 1962 to assume my duties as Superintendent of the Lunen-
burg Public Schools, I took over the direction of a school system of approximately 1650 students. In
the interim, the Lunenburg school enrollment has grown to the present moment where we now have an
enrollment of about 1950. During this period no additional classroom space has been added to the
school facilities. It is obvious to all, I am sure, that simple arithmetic will show that this is the
equivalent of about eleven classrooms if one thinks in terms of a class size of 27 per space. It is
true that an addition to the Jr. -Sr. High School had been opened the previous year in anticipation of
much of this growth. The fact remains, however, that the School System has reached a point where
additional teaching spaces must be provided if we are to maintain pupil-teacher enthusiasma and morale
for the type of dynamic, challenging school system Lunenburg wants and must have in today's world in
order to prepare our youth to compete with other students from all over our country.
It is not my purpose to dwell on this problem
or to reflect bitterness over failure to take steps to
fulfill this need for additional teaching spaces during
the past year. The need, nonetheless, is here now,
a solution is needed as expeditiously as possible if
we are to provide quality education. Continued growth
is not going to stop if Lunenburg is going to be part
of the dynamic growth forecast for America. It is not
idle rumor that we are currently using improvised
spaces such as the Teacher's Workrooms, the Home -
making room, the High School Auditorium and others
to attempt to carry on the type of educational program
the School Committee has set as the Community's
goal. It is not only pupil growth which contributes to
this need to improvise, it is as well, extension of
program offerings and more specialized types of teach-
ing than have been characteristic of times past.
A STUDY GROUP IN THE AUDITORIUM
So much of our future progress hinges upon additional school facilities that my report must of
necessity dwell somewhat upon the problem. Your School Committee has had to grapple with the pro-
blem for next September as we look forward to a growth of about 80 or SO more students with the open-
ing of schools in September, 1966. The Committee during its budget deliberations has determined that
the School System must have a minimum of four additional classrooms in September 1966. At least

"IMPORTANT EDUCATIONAL FACTORS AFFECTING LUNENBURG IN THE
NEAR FUTURE"
First, in December of 1964, the so-called WILLIS REPORT was made public. Everyone knows by
now that this was a complete study of Massachusetts' Public Education System. The recommendations
contained in this study were generally approved and adopted by the legislature and signed by the
Governor. What remains is the implementation of these recommendations and a time-table for com-
pliance. Inevitably, these recommendations must affect Lunenburg as it plans for additional school
facilities.
Among its requirements is the fact that Public School Kindergartens must be provided although
it is not mandatory for parents to send them. In addition, Elementary class sizes must be reduced to
a ratio of 25 pupils to a teacher and the secondary level to approximately an average of 20. At a time
when Lunenburg must comply with these requirements if they are to receive their full share of State
Aid, this can mean the need for an additional seven or eight classrooms. Such requirements must be
taken into consideration in any future building program if we are to be planning properly. There are of
course many other recommendations but none that hit the local level more forcefully than these two.
If the Legislature can ever succeed in passing a tax bill, it is intended that the local communities
would receive greatly increased State Aid to help finance these requirements plus offering some tax
relief at the local level. In Lunenburg, for instance, if the Governor's proposal were approved, our
community would receive $340, 573. in General School Aid under Chapter 70 in 1966, as opposed to the
$92,000. we are scheduled to receive. The provisions of this law then are bound to have significant
effect on future facilities needed and possible sizeable increased financial relief to ease the burden
at the local level.
Second, the ELEMENTARY AND SECONDARY EDUCATION ACT OF 1965 is a major breakthrough
in Federal Aid to Education and its provisions will certainly have profound effect on our educational
offerings in the very near future. Federal projects under this Act have already been submitted in the
BUSINESS, SCIENCE, GUIDANCE, and LANGUAGE areas and we are about ready to submit a sizeable
project for complete Federal funding for more intense help in the areas of MATH, READING, and
SPEECH THERAPY. This latter project should enable us to give more intensive help to the under-
achiever who has ability yet does not meet his potential.
In addition, the Lunenburg Public Schools have been working in conjunction with the Mental
Health Center in developing a RESEARCH PROJECT with complete Federal Funding designed to work
with the PRE-SCHOOL CHILD in an attempt to discover and correct problems which might develop in
youngsters which would inhibit their learning progress at an early enough age before the problems
become too solidly entrenched. Work has been going on for several months now in this project which
is now about ready to submit. Hopefully, it will be approved. This would mean thousands of dollars
to Lunenburg and should enable the schools to help prevent serious learning blocks before they have
taken root.
There are other areas of this Act, particularly in the LIBRARY AREA, which remains to be explored
as soon as guidelines are received from the State. Unquestionably, Federal Aid is going to have pro-
found effect upon our educational offerings of the future and we must continue to be alert to capitalize
on its advantages.

School Nurse
The School Nursing Program is performed by the Town Nurses, Mrs. Doris M.
Lahti and Mrs. Gladys V. Barney, who also are responsible for the Town's visiting
nursing services.
During 1965, 447 visits were made to school children for: follow-up on some
defects found by the School Doctor, vision and hearing failures, health supervision
and absence from school. Some transportation was supplied to doctors and clinics
for needed attention.
The present school year has presented problems, particularly in the vision testing,
which is a very important part of any school health program. Each year many new
children are picked up asvision failures and many others with glasses are referred
back to their eye doctor. Because of lack of proper testing space at the High School
and Annex, the testing has consumed a great deal more time than in the past. Our
present vision machine requires twenty feet of testing space. We are in hopes that
next year we will have one of the new vision machines which can be used in nurses'
offices, not requiring any space except a table.
We are able to keep our school immunization program at a very high level with
the programs offered by the Nashoba Board of Health, as follows:
Diptheria and Tetanus Booster and re-vaccination against small-pox for Grades 1,
6, & 11.
Sabin Oral Polio to new students who have not had immunization and booster
doses to children in Grade 1 who previously completed oral series.
Tuberculin Tine Testing was administered to students in Grades 1 and 9, with
follow-up on any positive reactors and their families.
Dental flouride treatments for Grades 2, 5, and 8 given by the Dental Hygienist.
The above programs require written permission from parents and have certainly
been well accepted with very few refusals.
In closing, we wish to thank Superintendent, Mr. Brown; Principals, Mr. Carey
and Mr. Passios; school faculty and personnel, without whose support and coopera-
tion we would not be able to carry out successfully any of our school health programs.
Respectfully submitted,
DORIS M. LAHTI, R. N.
School Nurse

School Lunch Program
This is my Third annual report of the Lunenburg School Lunch Program and is
for the school year, September 1964 to September 1965.
During this period we fed 194,640 students the Type "A" lunch. Of these,
6,015 were free lunches served to high school workers and the needy. In addition to
these, we served 11,459 lunches to adults including the staff. There were 175 lunch
days for the Elementary School and 177 for the Jr. -Sr. High School. These amount to
an estimated 1,171 lunches served per lunch day. We sold 164,387 half-pints of
milk under the United States Department of Agriculture Special Milk Act Program as
well as 5,245 half-pints to adults.
Financially, we fared very well and there was an opening balance of $10,106.50
for the beginning of the school year in September 1965 after all bills were paid in June.
Commodities in the form of butter, flour, rice, rolled wheat dried milk, beans,
peanut butter, ground beef, cheeses, corn meal, lard, turkeys, chicken, roast beef,
canned fruits, etc. donated by the U. S. Department of Agriculture were generous in
amount and contributed greatly to the financial success of the program. It is note-
worthy to point out that the School Lunch Program is self-supporting with the excep-
tion of the School Lunch Supervisor's salary, fuel and electric costs. These com-
modities are available during different months and the quantity allotted is based on
the average number of students fed. The total valuation of these donated commodities
was $20,561.50.
Participation percentage-wise for the year in the Jr. -Sr. High School was 75%
according to attendance and 71% according to membership. In the Elementary School,
participation according to attendance was 68% and 64% according to membership. Such
percentages represent what is considered to be very fine participation.
Personnel in the Jr. -Sr. High School consist of Mrs. Lucy Chartrand as the
Head Cook and Mrs. Gertrude Solovei as the Baker, while the general workers are
Mrs. Ethel Nolan, Mrs. Mary Thibault, Mrs. Gloria Beaulac, Mrs. Mary Haueisen,
Mrs. Anita Forest and Mrs. Ella Nicholson.
Personnel in the Elementary School are Mrs. Rita Seguin, Manager; Mrs. Hazel
Whitney, Head Cook and Mrs. Bertha Geigner, Baker. General Workers are Mrs. Gen-
evieve Nass, Mrs. Annette Thibodeau, Mrs. Phyllis Colello, and Mrs. Ellen Brown.
The success of our program depends upon these people, many of whom have
been with Lunenburg for some years as faithful and conscientious workers.
At the Thanksgiving dinner this past November, we served 1,601 "Type A"

lunches, while for Christmas dinner, we served 1,552 lunches, both featuring turkey
and gravy with ice cream for dessert. As always at the Elementary School, the tables
were appropriately decorated by the students and teachers.
The Annual Spring School Lunch Workshop was held in Holden for three days
during our April vacation. Mrs. Rita Seguin from the Elementary School and Mrs.
Lucy Chartrand, Mrs. Ethel Nolan and myself, attended from the Jr. -Sr. High School.
Mrs. Seguin and I attended the Supervisors and Managers Conference at the
University of Massachusetts, June 28th - July 2nd, 1965, the theme of which was
"FUNCTIONS OF SCHOOL FOOD SERVICE MANAGEMENT". Lectures and courses were
given by the staff of the School Lunch Office in Boston, as well as by the staff at the
University.
On Friday, November 8th, in conjunction with the Teachers' Convention, the
personnel of both schools attended the School Lunch Convention for Worcester County
held at the Tahanto Regional High School in Boylston. It was a most successful meet-
ing and featured bread and roll making.
I wish to express my thanks to you, Mr. Brown and to the School Committee;
also, the Principals of the schools and to the Town Personnel and Boards. Especially
do I wish to thank Mr. Archambault and his custodians for the assistance we receive
throughout the year and my own personnel for the hard work and dedication to the
lunch program.
