HAROLD D. HARLEY
1891 - 1980
Harold was very active in the affairs of the Town during his entire life and gave many
years of dedicated service. not only to the Planning Board, Conservation Commission
and Town Forest Committee, but also to the entire Town. Harold's recognition of the
importance of land-use planning was visionary. His early achievements in leading
Lunenburg into the adoption of Subdivision Control and Zoning, and the acquisition
of public lands for conservation and park use, will no doubt be honored by the ever-
increasing gratitude of generations of Lunenburgers yet to come.

Board of Selectmen: left to right - Rudolph Masella, Chairman Ann P. Hall, and August N. Misner.
As the new year began and a year of celebration in honor of Lunenburg's 250th birthday came to a close, the
Board of Selectmen looked forward to a return to normalcy in the Selectmen's office. It was not to be.
Tax Cap Fever was upon US 0%?; 4%?; who and what to cut?; can we spend more if the people vote to in-
crease?; how much more? The questions flew around Town Hall, the State House, and everywhere in between. The
problem: Lunenburg's Annual Town Meeting was scheduled to take place long before those on Beacon Hill were ready
to make up their minds. However, under the very capable leadership of Joe Giovino and the Finance Committee,
Town Counsel Bob Larkin, and Town Treasurer Mary Padula, we held our Town Meeting on schedule and managed to
carry law. out the will of the voters in a manner which met all the requirements of the legislation which eventually became
The voters at the Annual Town Meeting made the decision to purchase land andoperate a Town-owned sanitary
landfill as the solution to the serious problem of solid waste disposal. The land has been purchased, the preliminary
plans approved, and assignment of the land was made by the Board of Health after a public hearing in December.
Final plans are being prepared by the engineer; George Munyon, Highway Superintendent, has been named Opera-
tions Manager; and the Board fully expects to open the new facility next summer and simultaneously to begin closure
procedures on the facility on Townsend Harbor Road.
The Annual Town Meeting of 1979 was notable in at least two other respects as well. First, attendance at the
meeting was the highest in recent memory: 808 according to the official tally by the Town Clerk. Last but no
means least was the retirement from public office of one of Lunenburg's most dedicated and tirelesspublic servants --
Sherwood Winchester. After thirty-two years of service as Forest Warden, Police Chief, Fire Chief, Constable, and

Selectman, "Winch" is missed around Town Hall. However, he is still verymuch to be seen around Town behind the
wheel of his school bus, and his big smile is always a welcome sight.
May and June were quiet as new Selectman Gus Misner joined the Board in carrying out the routine duties of
the office.
Then came JULY! "Contaminated Wells, Don't Drink the Water!", said the State.
Then came AUGUST! "Laboratory Error", said EPA.
The summer of '79 will long be remembered in Lunenburg as the summer of the "water problem". The Town
struggled through five very long weeksof a nightmare involving an effort to find the answer to questions about what
had happened as the result of the illegal dumping of chemicals in a sand pit off Townsend Harbor Road. Hickory
Hills Lake was threatened, as well as all the private wells around the lake and all the way to the Shirley town line.
We learned that medical science did not know what effects consumption of the chemicals supposedly involved would
have on the human body. We learned that there was no established procedure to provide potable water to homes
deprived of the use of their wells by contamination. We learned there was no established procedure for cleaning up
a chemical spill which obviously needed to be cleaned up before it spread further. We learned that financial assist-
ance for even such an immense disaster was by no means assured. No one had the answers to the hard questions we
had to ask. However, many people did make an extra effort in what was a time of crisis for Lunenburg and the fol-
lowing are deserving of a vote of thanks from US all: All Town departments, the Lunenburg Water District Commiss-
ioners and staff, Mr. Kendall Crocker of Ashby, Mayor David Gilmartin of Fitchburg, all our elected legislators
both state and federal, and lastly the many individuals who as members of the oft maligned bureaucracy made an
extra effort to assist US in whatever ways they were able.
On August 16th at a meeting arranged by Congressman Drinan to bring together all the federal agencies who
might be able to help the Town, an abrupt change in direction took place. Dr. Thomas Spittler of the Environmental
Protection Agency announced that previous tests conducted by the state laboratory on Lunenburg wells were "erro-
neous", and that the warning of contamination of drinking water was based on false reports due to laboratory error.
The Department of Environmental Quality Engineering is currently supervising a hydrogeological study at the
site of the chemical dump; and until the Board receives some definite report on the results of that study, including
the extent of the contamination and probable future impacton the area, no specific actionis planned. It is possible
to report that testing of selected wells is continuing by DEQE and that all tests to date have shown the water to be
safe for drinking purposes at this time.
September found the Board doing the work necessary to submit a Pre-application to HUD for a Community Dev-
elopment Block Grant to install water mains in the Townsend Harbor Road-Hemlock Drive area. The grant was sub-
sequently denied.
November brought a Special Town Meeting to deal with the recommended purchase of salt spreaders which
would reduce the use of salt on our roads in winter icing conditions. The new awareness of the precious and pre-
cariousness nature of water quality made this a popular issue and the spreaders have been purchased. Also at this
time-omen of what is to come additional funds were to various accounts because of the unprecedented
rise in the cost of oil and gasoline.
In December the Board and the Police Union reached agreement on a new contract after many months of ne-
gotiations. Also, preliminary budget meetings were held with major department heads.
It is plain that the closing year of the seventies was a precursor to the eighties and the problems we are all to
face in the next decade the danger to drinking water supplies was vividly experienced here at home; with mixed
horror and apprehension we watch the news from the Mid-East especially focusing on Iran and Afghanistan; the spi-
raling cost of oil and the realization that the supply is finite at any cost; the near miss at Three Mile Island. As we
prepare both Town and family budgets for the coming year and wonder what path lies ahead, we are again reminded
that although the aforementioned problems are national in scope, they are very personal in effect; and it is each of
US singly and together in every city and town across the nation who are the United States of America. To paraphrase
Robert Frost, we have miles to go before we sleep, miles to go before we sleep.

Board of Health
The following is a brief resume of services receivedby our Town through Nashoba Associated Boards of Health:
In the Nashoba Nursing Service, the term "health promotion" is used to describe activities that relate to dis-
ease prevention. As this term implies, the Nashoba Community Health Nurses, such as Mrs. Shirley Carpenter, work
to promote the highest level of health for the citizens of the community. Some examples of these activities include:
home visits to expectant mothers, to newborns, especially those of high risks; to elderly citizens requiring health coun-
seling or assistance from available community resources; and to the chronically ill in need of assistance. Other ac-
tivities include screening for early detection of disease and communicable disease investigation and control. The
Nursing Service also serves as the Certified Home Health Agency (VNA) for 14 Nashoba towns.
Nashoba's Nursing Service also provides home care services in conjunction with the area's home care corpora-
tions (the agencies serving the needs of the elderly), with the statewide Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) Pro-
gram, and the State's Lead Poisoning Project. Nursing Service activities during 1979 were: Nursing Visits, Health
Promotion - 287; Nursing Visits, Disease-Related - 986; Physical Therapy Visits - 47; Home Health Aide Visits - 889
(hours expended in Home Health Aide Visits were 2,006). Also, 28 Well Adult and Oldster Clinics were held in
Lunenburg attended by 723 persons.
One of the least obvious, but most important prevention services by Nashoba is in the area of Environmental
Health. This Division is responsible for the prevention of disease and injury in the areas of: food service, such as
markets and restaurants; housing; recreational facilities, such as camps, beaches and swimming pools; sewage disposal;
wells; public water supplies; nuisance conditions. An additional benefit is in the improvement of environmental
quality. In 1979, some of the Environmental Health Services included: Food Service Inspections - 14; School Sur-
veys - 7; Well Inspections/Samples - 167; Bathing Beach Inspections/Samples - 52; Nuisance Investigations - 40;
Housing Inspections - 4; Sewage Disposal System Inspections - 302; Sewage Disposal System Permits - 33.

In the continuing process of working to maintain a logical, moderate growth pattern for the Town, the Planning
Board has concluded another intense year of work. Projects for the year ranged from the approval of a new eleven
lot subdivision off Arbor Street, to the bi-weekly process of approving new lots not requiring subdivision approval, to
our current task of reviewing the sequence of updating the Town's Master Plan, last prepared twenty years ago.
Currently, areas of greatest concern are being pinpointed for study and consultants are being reviewed to en-
able the Planning Board to present to the Town a proposal to prepare a new Master Plan which will address the future
effect of areas such as:
(a) water quality and use.
(b) the extension of I-190 and its effect on the Town.
(c) the impact on Town services of extended or accelerated growth.
As this process continues, the Planning Boardwill be seeking large amounts of input from the Town Boards, Commis-
sions and its citizens to insure a complete and well rounded plan with direction.
This year the Planning Board accepted with regret the resignation of Ronald D. Colvin. This vacancy was
filledby Charles H. B. Arning who was selected by the unanimous joint vote of the Board of Selectmen and Planning
Board to fill out the year's term.

On January 1, 1980 your Boardof Assessors will increase all valuations by 10%. This increase will include all
land, real estate and commercial property in the Town. This action is necessary to comply with State law, that all
property must be valued at full and fair cash value. This is part of a program started in 1975 by your Board of As-
sessors to maintain the revaluation of the Town done in 1969 and 1970 by the firm of Whipple, Magane and Darcy.
When the new revaluation was put into effect in 1970, the Town was at 100% full and fair cash value. The Assessors
were given guidelines by the revaluation firm for the purpose of maintaining the new system. Each year from Sep-
tember to March, we view every piece of property in Town and record any changes made. By following these prac-
tices, we have been able to maintain accurate data on all property in Town. When the State Department of Cor-
porations and Taxation informed US in 1975 that Lunenburg (due to rapidly increasing Real Estate values) wasno longer
at full and fair value and must submit a plan to the State Department of Corporations and Taxation on how we were
going to correct this situation or face court action, we were able to submit the following plan that was approved by
the State.
1. The Town had been revalued in 1969 and 1970 by the firm of Whipple, Magane and Darcy.
2. The Assessors and staff were thoroughly instructed in the operation and use of the Whipple, Magane and
Darcy evaluation and assessment system. This included field work and record keeping. We were left with
the firm's manuals, guides and forms to maintain the system in equity.
3. All property is viewed on a yearly basis to insure up-to-date detailed drawings on property record cards.
4. All assessing courses offered by the Commonwealth of Massachusetts are attended by all Assessors and clerks
to maintain knowledge of the law.

The Board of Assessors increased all property in Town 10% in 1976 and another 10% in 1977. However, due to
a slow down in construction and uncertain economic conditions, we didnot increase valuations in 1978 or 1979. Now
as we enter 1980 and check the real estate sales for the past year, your Assessors find that we have property valued
at only 60% of full market value. Therefore, we find it absolutely necessary to increase all valuations in Town by
another 10% to stay within the guidelines set down by the State Supreme Court that all property must be valued at
full and fair market value. This action will have the effect of increasing the total valuation of the Town by more
than seven million dollars, however, it will also lower the tax rate if the Town budgets were to remain the same as
last year and the total amount of money a person would pay for taxes would also remain the same.
In 1980 your Assessors find there is a new law on the books called classification. This law divides the Town
into four different classes for taxation and allows each class to be taxed at a different rate. The four classes are as
follows: Homeowners, Industrial, Commercial and Open Spaces. The Town will be required to increase the valua-
tions of all property to 100% full and fair market value and then have this valuation certified by the State. At this
time with the advice and consent of the Selectmen, the reduced classification values will be instituted and values will
be maintained by factoring every two years on the advice of the State Department of Revenues. Another new law re-
quires Assessors to undertake studies sponsored by the State and pass examinations which will assure some standard of
competence of individual Assessors and standard assessing practices throughout the Commonwealth. Your Assessors
have three years to comply with this new law, and only certified Assessors will be allowed to take office in the fu-
ture.

Library Trustees
Library Trustees: Seated left to right - Janet M. Cragin, Beverly A. Mulholland, Chairman Barbara T. Murray and

What can you buy today for $7.61? Two movie tickets, four pounds of hamburger, seven gallons of gasoline
or the complete range of services of the Ritter Memorial Library, which includes borrowing books, records, au-
diovisual materials and equipment, usingreference sources and the help of the Library staff, the photocopier, meet-
ing room and children's room, and enjoying many kinds of activities and programs. The Library budget, about one
percent of the total Town budget, is the best investment per capita that Lunenburg residents made in 1979.
Adults borrowed 18, 473 books (86 on interlibrary loan), 599 records, 1, 291 magazines, 68 pamphlets, and 58
regional library system films, for a total of 20,489 items. Children tookout 19,986 books (4,268 to the children at
the Primary School), 77 magazines, 18 puzzles, 152 filmstrip kits, and 384 records for a sum of 20,617. The grand
total for materials borrowed was 41, 106. If there were no library service available here, each of US would have had
to pay $440 for these materials at today's prices, instead of the $7.61 we actually spent!
Because the price of books has risen so high, the Library has adopted a "get tough" policy for retrieving long
overdue materials. After a well-publicized amnesty period last spring, a blitz of phone calls, notices, and certi-
fied letters resulted in 99 of 129 items either being returned or paid for a savings of almost $700. This retrieval
policy will continue, resorting to court action if necessary, to prevent the loss of expensive Town resources and to
keep all materials available for your use.
Wehave recently completed another important economizing measure a three-year program to make our sev-
enty year old building more energy efficient. This effort has involved tuning and readjusting the heating system,
lowering thermostat settings, and installing storm windows on all three floors. These actions should translate into
fewer dollars being wasted on ever-increasing fuel and utility costs.

Historical Commission
The newly formed Historical Commissionheld its first meeting in June for the purpose of undertaking a survey
of the architecturally and historically significant areas, buildings, monuments, archeological sites, cemeteries and
structures in Town. They met with Liz Durfee, Inventory Coordinator for the Massachusetts Historical Commission,
to discuss the functions and survey process of the local Commission. Presently, the Commission is photographing,
researching and processing information relating to the Center of Town and the surrounding buildings. The following
is an excerpt from a booklet on Historical Commissions published by Office of the Secretary of the Commonwealth,
PURPOSE OF A LOCAL HISTORICAL COMMISSION
The local historical commission is the agency responsible for identifying and recording the historic assets
of its community and for developing and implementing a program for their preservation. The commission
cooperates withother municipal agencies such as the planning board and conservation commission to in-
sure that the goals of historic preservation are considered in planning for future development of the com-
munity. The commission also coordinates the activities of local groups whose interests involve history
or historic preservation, such as local historical societies and private preservation organizations, serving
as the liaison between these groups and the state, and Federal agencies and preservation organizations.
The inventory, which is the primary responsibility of the local historical commission, provides the basis
for all preservation planning in the community. It provides the Massachusetts Historical Commission
with the information necessary to assist communities in protecting historic properties from adverse effect
caused by publicly funded or licensed projects. It also provides the basis for evaluation by both the
local historical commission and the Massachusetts Historical Commission of properties suggested for nom-
ination to the National Register of Historic Places. The Massachusetts Historical Commission receives
National Register suggestions submitted by the local historical commission based on a locally conducted
inventory of the community's historic assets. The State commission seeks the advise of the historical com-
mission in local allocation of Federal historic preservation grants administered by the Massachusetts His-
torical Commission. For these reasons it is important for each city and town to have such a Commission.

Personnel Committee
The Town of Lunenburg is fortunate in having in its employ many extremely conscientious and capable people,
at all levels. They make a significant contribution to the quality of life in our Town, It has been and is the inten-
tion of the Personnel Committee to deal fairly with these people. However, the Committee must also work within
the framework of what is fair to the taxpayers of Lunenburg, upon whom falls the burden of paying the salaries of
Town employees,
Over the past several years, the Committee has felt obliged to limit salary increases for Town employees, so
as not to place an unfair burden on the taxpayers when they could ill afford it. This has, of course, resulted in a
falling-behind of wage levels in the Town. The Committee feels that this year, something should be done to bring
things back into line. We are, therefore, proposing a somewhat larger than usual percentage adjustment in the
overall Salary Schedule. To compensate for this, our Department Heads have been asked to curtail other increases
wherever possible, so that the adjustment can be made without inflating the total budgets more than is absolutely
necessary.
Aside from the overall percentage increase, the amended Classification and Salary Schedule reflects a nec-
essary increase in the mileage allowance, from 12c to 17c per mile, and a small number of adjustments to particular
salary categories, where inequities were brought to the Committee's attention. We feel that the resultant proposed
plan is as equitable as we can make it, both for the employees and for the taxpayers who will be passing judgment
on it at the coming Annual Town Meeting.

Conservation Commission
The Conservation Commission met twenty-three times in regular and special meetings during 1979. Six public
hearings were held on thirteen requests for determination of applicability to the Wetlands Act. This means that out
of twenty-three meetings, nineteen were spent primarily on the Wetlands Act. This activity is the result of the
dwindling number of good building lots. This increases the demand for marginal lots most of which are considered
wet lots. The building of homes on wet lots located in the watershed serving the Water District is a distinct threat
to the quality of our drinking water. Vigilance by the Board of Health, Planning Board and Conservation Commission
will ensure that our water supplies are not endangered by helter-skelter building in the watershed area.
The Conservation Commission strongly endorses the purchase of the development rights of the so-called Levin
Farm at the intersection of Massachusetts Avenue and Electric Avenue. This 175-acre tract would then remain ag-
ricultural land from this time on. The enormous amount of Massachusetts farm land given up for development will
hurt US in the future. If we act now in unison with the state "Agricultural Preservation Act" this prime agricultural
property will be saved from development.
In Memory
As 1980 began, a valiant fighter for the cause of conservation quietly faded from
the scene. A few hours after being stricken at hishome on Sunnyhill Road, Harold Harley
passed away on January 3, 1980.
He will be sorely missed by those of US who worked with him on the Conservation
Commission. The wisdom of his counsel, the sharpness of his wit, and his selfless dedi-
cation to the Commission and its work will long be remembered by those who follow in
his footsteps.
Footsteps that began before conservation became fashionable. Footsteps that
bore the vision of Marshall Park, Marshall Pond, Lesure Park, the Town Forests, the
Cowdrey Nature Center and Laurelbank Farm. Footsteps that carried the Town into sub-
division control and zoning laws that protected the Town from over development and
helped maintain its pastoral beauty.
The footsteps have stopped, but thanks to Harold Harley the roadway to conserva-
tion is broad and inviting to those who want to keep Lunenburg a beautiful Town to
live in.

Sewerage & Drainage Investigative Committee
In view of its findings that sewerage and drainage problems do exist in certain areas of Town, the Sewerage
and Drainage Investigative Committee recommended to the 1979 Annual Meeting that $18,000 be appropriated to
match a Federal EPA Grant of $54,000. The $72,000 is to be used to engage an engineering firm to make a detailed
study to update the 1973 Morgenroth Report by identifying existing problem areas, determining future needs, and to
prepare a plan for reasonable, more cost effective alternatives to an extensive sewer system and treatment plant.
Following a favorable vote under Article 13 of the April 7, 1979 Annual Meeting Warrant, this Committee
interviewed engineering firms and selected Dufresne-Henry Engineering Corp. of Springfield, Vermont to prepare
an engineering service contract for the Step I Wastewater Facilities Planning work and to prepare an application for
funding to the Massachusetts Division of Water Pollution Control and request that the United States Environmental
Protection Agency approve the scope of services fees. Although it was agreed that upon approval and award of the
Grant by EPA the Town would finalize a contract with Dufresne-Henry, it was also agreed that in the event the EPA
Grant should not be received, the Town would have incurred no financial liability.
The agreement with Dufresne-Henry was executed on May 21, 1979, and Step I Facilities Plan wassubmitted
to the State on July 31, 1979, followed by supplementary information submitted on October 12, 1979. Finally, on
January 23, 1980, a Staff Engineer from the Division of Water Pollution Control contacted the engineers and in-
dicated that except for a few minor changes, the plans met with the State's approval. On January 28, 1980, the
engineers were provided with an outline of the required minor changes, the changes have been made, and the plans
and completed grant applications will have been executed and submitted to the State and EPA by the time you read
this report. Although the preliminary State review took longer than anticipated, Dufresne-Henry is of theopinion
that the Project can still commence in the Spring of 1980, in accordance with the original timetable.

shield wings, four-wheel brakes and colors other
than black. These were the formative years of our
love affair with automobiles.
Gasoline and oil were usually dispensed by

stood proudly in front of the establishment, topped
George G. Munyon, Jr., Highway Superintendent

gravity into the tank. One watched the merchant
very closely to sure he didn't pump "short". Gas was selling for 20 cents per gallon; oil was pumped from a drum
into quart measures at 10 cents. Some stores and shops carried a premium grade oil at 15 cents per quart.
In 1920 the first white lines were painted down the center of a road in Wayne County, Michigan. The idea
soon spread across the country, along with yellow lines showing that it was unsafe to pass. Stop signs, which told
drivers who should wait and who should go, began to appear at crossroads. Automatic traffic signals first appeared
in Detroit in the early 1920's.

Most of the motor vehicles stolen in Lunenburg were taken from driveways of private homes, and in many in-
stances, the keys had been left in the vehicles and the doors were unlocked. Residents are cautioned to be sure that
keys are taken from ignitions and that vehicles are locked, even on their own property.
Loitering, liquor, lighting and littering on the Lakefront all contribute to Leominster and Lunenburg's law en-
forcement problems. The so-called "bottle law" adopted at the last Annual Town Meeting has been an effective
tool in attempting to control some of the problems in this area. As a result of the newly formed Lake Whalom Lake-
front Association of Concerned Citizens, other control measures under consideration are: making the Lakefront one
way; restricting public use during certain hours; restricting parking and making a tow-away zone; and, ultimately,
closing the Lakefront off completely to motor vehicle traffic.
Generally speaking, there was an indication of crime reduction in Lunenburg during 1979. Statistics for the
year show that the breaks into private homes and businesses were down to 71 as opposed to 119 in 1978, with 65%
of all stolen property recovered. The dollar value of property stolen during 1979 did increase to $145, 412.00 of
which $94,740.00 was recovered. Larcenies remained about the same, at 158; but as previously mentioned, motor
vehicle thefts rose to 52 over the 33 stolen in 1978. Total calls for service this past year were 5,270 including 117
complaints for acts of vandalism, 15 assaults, two armed robberies, one bomb scare, and there were 81 persons taken
into protective custody as part of enforcement of the drinking laws.
Other activities within the Department included the investigation of sudden deaths by drowning and suicide,
responses to 370 alarms (false or otherwise), the issuance of 238 gun permits, the making of 672 house checks and
the serving of 239 summons.

Dog Officer
The Dog Officer received and investigated five
hundred calls since taking over this position as of
July 1, 1979. At this time there are approximately
1,250 dogs licensed in Lunenburg - due in large
part to summer and fall notices being mailed out and
numerous telephone calls. Fine books have been
printed and citations are being issued at the time of
any violation of the Restraining By-law which was
votedon and passed at the 1979 Annual Town Meet-
ing. The fines are: $10. for 1st Offense and
$25.00 for each subsequent offense.
In seven instances, the Dog Officer, after due
investigation, found it necessary to ask that Perma-
nent Restraining Orders be placed on these dogs due
to the serious problems they had created in Town.
The Dog Officer in afew instances had to remove a
few dogs from the Town completely in order to insure
the safety of the citizens of Lunenburg.
Also, 118 stray dogs were picked up in Town
since July 1st. Four dogs were destroyed due to un-
treatable illnesses, 14 dogs were claimed by owners,
and the remaining 100 dogs were given away to per-
sons who can provide good homes for these animals.
The Dog Officer works diligently in finding the right
home for the right dog and truly believes that every
good dog deserves the chance for a good home and
that good old standby - Love.
There is still a continuing problem with dogs
Kathleen M. Comeau, Dog Officer
whose owners work and allow their dogs to run loose
and unattended. These dogs cause frequent aggrava-
tion to other residents, and in many ways become public nuisances. Often some of these dogs are picked up by the
Dog Officer severely injured and in some cases the dogs are found dead in the roadway.
Dogs running loose in the school yard have continued to be a very disturbing problem. It has now been decided
by School officials and the Dog Officer that no dogs will be allowed in the school yard at any time. The safety of
all our children is vitally important, and since this has become an almost uncontrollable problem, the rule will be
strictly enforced at all times. The Dog Officer checks the school grounds twice a day, seven days a week, and
school officials and personnel are also assisting in this matter. There will be no exceptions.
The Dog Officer wishes to remind all dog owners that should your dog become lost, there is no possible way for
anyone to know who the owner of the dog is unless the dog has proper tags on its collar. If all dogs were licensed
and wore identification tags, there would not be one lost dog in Town. Such a dog could be promptly returned to
its owner and tax dollarswould be saved as the Dog Officer would not have to spend many hours feeding and caring
for stray dogs. Should your dog's license tag become lost, you may purchase a duplicate at the Town Clerk's office
for ten cents. The State passed a law requiring dogowners to present a Rabies Shot Certificate to the Town Clerk's
office at the time of purchasing or renewing a dog license.
The Dog Officer wishes at this time to say thank you to the Board of Selectmen, Town Clerk and the Town Hall
staff for all the assistance given her, and also to the Police Department for their assistance. She also wishes to thank
the peopleof Lunenburg who have been very understanding and helpful. The Dog Officer is available at all times no
matter how small the problem may be and will do her best to help each and every resident in Town.

A different type of report this year one of pro-
gress. We now have a caseload of five cases per month
compared to fifteen to twenty cases per month in the past,
and we are recovering more money from various sources
as explained below:
1. A cost-of-living increase (4.8%) in the overall bud-
get for all recipients and retroactive to July 1, 1979
under MGLA Chapter 115.
2. Veterans' Improved Pension (VIP).
3. Notice of intent (to each applicant when applying
for assistance).
4. Computer Program Sheets (reported quarterly).
5. Voluntary Assignments (sources of income).
6. Notice of Action (Agent's alternative).
Items #2 through #6 are briefly explained below:
Item #2 - Another new pensionplan has been passed and
has decreased our rolls because it offers more of an in-
come than other plans, i.e. single veterans under the old
plan received a pension of $197.00 monthly, but when
they apply under the Veterans' Improved Pension plan,
this changes to $295.83 which would put the veteran in
a status of self-support without our assistance. Not all
Alfred H. Gauthier, Veterans' Agent
persons eligible under this plan are referred to said plan
as they could lose some incomes. Therefore, they are
advised as to which plan would be most beneficial to them. After receipt of an application, the Veterans' Adminis-
tration sends a letter to the veteran indicating one of the following - Unadvantaged Election - Election Deferral -
Election Award. This office is then notified as to what procedure has been followed.
Item #3 - Notice of Intent - Each applicant is to receive a letter from this office in that effective January 1, 1979
we must provide the State Commissioner of Veterans' Services with a quarterly list of recipients on the rolls, and this
information is fedinto the State computer for cross-indexingwith all departments in the Commonwealth, so that wages
or benefits can be counter-checked against lists for accuracy of reported incomes, and appropriate action can be
taken against any person who is not entitled. This new law comes under MGLA Chapter 367, Acts of 1978.
Item #4 - Computer Program Sheets - In #3 above whereby we notify the recipient of our intent, in #4 we must sub-
mit a quarterly Computer Sheet to the State with all necessary data for a three month period to be fed into the com-
puter and balanced against reports submitted by Welfare, Employment Security, Social Security, etc. so that the
recipient cannot receive monies fraudulently.
Item #5 - Voluntary Assignments - We now have authority to regain any monies paid to a recipient who would get a
future income from any and all State departments, sick benefits, retirement, etc. This will assist US in helping others
when we get some of our expenses back.
Item #6 - Notice of Action - At the discretion of the Veterans' Agent, a recipient can be - Suspended Terminated -
Amended - Reduced Stipend. However, a letter indicating action taken must be forwarded to the recipient and the
State as to reasons for action taken, and further indicating that the recourse of the recipient is to write a letter over
his signature within thirty days to the Board of Appeals and said Board will notify the recipient and the Agent of the
date scheduled for a review of appeal.
With the combination of all of the above, we are able to weed out and help those that are in need, thus saving
the Town money and many hours of paperwork. At this time I wish to thank everyone who has assisted me during the
past year, especially while I was incapacitated, and trust that I may call on them during the coming year.
