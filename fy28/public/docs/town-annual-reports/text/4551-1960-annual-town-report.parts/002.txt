The first meeting of the Building Committee was held on April 4, 1960. At the Special Town Meeting in August
the voters approved preliminary plans and specifications for a twelve-roomaddition to the Junior-Senior High School
and appropriated $345,000 for its construction. Preliminary plans were approved by the State Building Assistance
Commission in September and final approval was given by that board on October 18, 1960. Approval was also re-
celved from the Emergency Finance Board to borrow up to $227,500 of the total funds appropriated.
On October 21, 1960, bids were opened and three days later the general contract was awarded to H. V. Lindberg
Company, the contract price being $35,000 less than the figure estimated. Mr. Lindberg has proven to be an ex-
cellent contractor, and his company has been ahead of schedule since they moved onto the building site. In view
of the quality of workmanship, the Building Committee decided against engaging a Building Inspector, thereby sav-
ing the taxpayers an additional $4,000.00
Bids for equipment are to be opened in early February and will be awarded in ample time to assure delivery be-
fore school commences in September. Needless to say, the Building Committee has had to meet a very tight sche-
dule in order to assure occupancy in September. However, barring labor strikes or unforeseen emergencies, the
building will be completed on schedule.
The Building Committee has recommended that the voters approve an article which is to be inserted in the 1961
Annual Meeting Warrant and which calls for approval of the enlargement of the locker room in the Junior-Senior
High School. The cost of this addition has been fixed at $6,905, as alternate bids were secured together with the
bid for the building addition. Additional money will not have to be raised as, in view of the savings mentioned in
connection with the main bid, the amount appropriated in August will more than cover the cost of this locker room
addition.

I herewith submit my Eleventh Annual Report (Seventh since
withdrawal from School Union, Ashby-Lunenburg-Townsend) as
Superintendent of Schools.
Many answers to the requests and alternatives in my report
for 1959 are completed or in correction. We expect to move in-
to the expanded facilities, added to the Junior-Senior High
School, September 1961. About half of this 12-room addition will be needed by some
of the Elementary pupils--probably five rooms of Grade VI. Some of this arrangement
is not educationally the best but will do very well until the next facilities are
voted. New building is projected for about 1965 for which survey and preparation
must be made. All projection is dependent on the birth rate and census variables.
"Perhaps the greatest problem facing American education is the widely held view that
all we require are a few more teachers, a few more buildings, a little more money
It will not be enough to meet the problem grudgingly or with a little money. The
nation's need for good education is immediate; and good education is expensive If
the public is not prepared for this, then responsible educators, business leaders,
political leaders, unions, and civic organizations, must join in
campaign to prepare
them
"
Battle weary school board members and school administrators who have fought hard
to get present budgets may shrink from looking squarely at the financial problems of
tomorrow. "America's destiny in a very great measure is in the hands of school board
members and superintendents.
The nation has indicated that "we can afford the education we need" and this
seems very true when we make some comparisons to available statistics on non-produc-
tive expenditures that even exceed school expenditures. The choice we must make,
therefore, is whether education will rank high or low in our scale of priorities.
Salaries for Lunenburg personnel have become less than competitive in many areas
of work over the past few years. Some partial corrections are attempted by the
School Committee in the present budget now under analysis. In many respects, teachers
belong to a second class profession. They have been grossly underpaid as evidenced
by the critical shortages, especially in certain subject areas. Their ranks have,
too often, attracted less than the best college graduates; in fact a Harvard statistic
reports that about twenty-five (25) percent of United States elementary teachers are
not college graduates.
The competition that characterizes industry's search for talented undergraduates
is lacking in the field of education. Everyone seeks the brilliant physicist of to-
morrow. Who seeks the brilliant teacher or is willing to pay for the services if
discovered? In contrast to other professions, teaching makes little provision for
the advancement of its most gifted specialists. We talk about merit but salary
questions have dominated the hue and cry about the teacher shortage so much so that
the result is as many drop merit as try to initiate it.
Two forces will shape American education in 1961 and the years to follow. One
is a legacy of the Eisenhower administration; the other a promise of the Kennedy
administration. In Elsenhower's, "Commission on National Goals", it recommends rais-

Report of Lunenburg Junior-Senior

It is with pleasure that I herewith submit my Annual Report
as Principal of the Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School. The Admin-
istration, Faculty and Student Body continue to work toward one
united goal: Excellence in Education. No society can be content
with the "status quo"; each group must continue to seek new ideas
and accept new ideas and new trends as it moves forward to meet the
challenge of contemporary times. Not only must our school be fully
prepared to face the pressure of the educational demands of today;
but we must be ever alert to the envisioned future.
Lunenburg citizens want excellence of education. Our community has seen fit to
provide excellent facilities and have established a committee for "long range school
housing" consideration. It is a pleasure to attempt to meet the desired standards of
a demanding community. We believe that the Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School is a
good school which is ever striving to "move education forward".
GROWTH
As our community continues its growth, the size of the Lunenburg Junior-Senior
High School reflects this community expansion. It is estimated that about 700 stu-
dents will register in Grades 7-12, in September of 1961. On the opening day of
school this past year, the Junior-Senior High School enrolled 602 students. This
number increased to 622 by October first.
The citizens of Lunenburg are to be congratulated for the foresightedness
which has led to the current construction of the twelve-room addition to our school.
The existence of The School Housing Committee is to be congratulated for the hours of
sincere and patient study which have produced recommendations designed to meet Lunen-
burg's school housing needs in the most economical and soundest manner possible.
There were 76 Seniors graduated from Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School in
1960, and of this number, forty have gone on to higher education, four are in the
armed forces, five are married, three have returned to school for Post Graduate work,
eleven are employed in various office jobs and thirteen are engaged in general type
employment. The present Senior Class now numbers 82.
SCHOOL PROGRAM
The quality of education offered by Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School must
meet the needs of the youth of our community for successful living in a complex
world. Our graduates who go on to "higher education" have a right to expect "a
solid preparation" for their collegiate endeavors. Our school must always be alert
to the everchanging demands of modern education. We must remain dedicated to the
task of "assisting each individual" to make the fullest possible use of his abilities
and talents.
We believe that Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School is an excellent secondary
school. Our faculty is ever mindful of its collective responsibility to furnish edu-
cational materials in keeping with the challenges of the age of space.

Report of Lunenburg Elementary Schools
for the Year 1960
In this day of moving forward in education, we must con-
tinuously be on the alert before we are swept up, on the one hand
by the nostalgia for the "good old days", and on the other, by the
cry for change. Much is done in reminiscing about the way things
were done "when I went to school", but much more must be accom-
plished to determine what must be done for children now.
In planning for the future, a school must always accept
the best of the past and keep it. But it must, more vigorously,
improve upon, or eliminate, theories and practices of the past
which have not stood the test of time. The main objective for our
schools is to maintain constant surveillance over our curriculum
and teaching practices, as well as our organizational and administrative theories and
practices, to the extent that we provide the best education within our power for the
young people of our community.
As has been stated in past reports, our school, in all this effort, is primarily
interested in the quality of its education. In this concept of quality, we are at
the same time very much concerned with the breadth that this education takes. Our
school believes in broad curriculum offerings, including development of each child's
knowledge, attitudes and concepts in sciences, history, geography, music, art, current
affairs, and social problems. However, we do not lose sight of the important notion
that, in broadening our curriculum, we not forget that the traditional reason for the
existence of the elementary school is the cultivation and improvement to the extent
of each child's potential, of the basic skills in the fields of primary concern-
reading, language, arithmetic, spelling, and penmanship. In our schools we are try-
ing to maintain a valid balance between the traditional fundamentals and the addi-
tional areas of knowledge of American cultural heritage in the fields of social
studies, science, music, art, and other subject areas. In recent years, it has fallen
upon our schools to assume the responsibility for many areas which were not as neces-
sary in times past when the home and community were the extent of a child's horizons.
Today, when we talk of the community, we think of the world. The scientific advance-
ments, the modern means of communication, the people of other nations are all now
almost as intimate to the child as his home and the nearby city were a half century
ago.
Our Lunenburg elementary schools are continuing to maintain a high level of
attainment in the fundamental skills area. This is noted very pointedly in the results
of the Iowa Tests of Basic Skills which indicate that our elementary school as a whole

interest in good education on the part of our citizenry.
From an educator's point of view, constructive criticism in the general litera-
ture of the day is valid and serves a useful purpose, but if any school or school
system had to follow the many ideas advanced by innumerable critics, many of whom
have no basis for their conclusions, the school would be swinging from "pillar to

IMPROVEMENTS AND CHANGES IN ORGANIZATION AND PROGRAM
Some of the changes and improvements in both organization and program will be
discussed briefly here.
In the area of reading, we have taken certain steps to improve the program. In
grades four and six a new method of regrouping students has been adopted. We have
taken a segment of these pupils in grades four and six and regrouped them according
to their reading level, using one teacher per reading group and adding the reading
supervisors, allowing for an additional group. By regrouping in this manner, we are
providing a more concentrated and longer period of individually supervised reading
since each teacher becomes responsible for only one reading level rather than the two
or three groups among which she must divide her time under the normal classroom read-
ing arrangement. This plan is proving to be very effective and will be expanded to
other grades when it becomes feasible.
On the first grade level also we are regroup-
ing pupils during their reading period in order to
lessen the number of groups with each teacher,
in
thereby giving students more continuous time in
reading under the direct supervision of the teacher.
In a number of individual cases in other grades,
pupils are sent to a room other than their own for
special help in reading at their own reading level.

being used in grades four, five and six. These
reading laboratories provide materials and exer-

so that every child in a class can simultaneously
work at his present level of ability. Each selec-

For a few years corrective speech help has been offered through a nearby school
system, with tuition being paid by parents, for those pupils who had speech defects
or deficiencies which were deemed correctible. To this date no service of this type
is available locally, though our remedial teachers and regular teachers do work on
speech improvement and simple speech correction.
Our special areas continue to be expanded. The music department, providing an
opportunity for our teachers to increase their competence in the teaching of music,
offered a fifteen-week in-service course in Music Education. Some twenty-five of our
teachers took this course, displaying a high level of professional interest, and re-
sponsibility. In the regular music program, we are placing special emphasis on the
listening phase of our program. To this end, listening periods have been scheduled
for every grade level, approximately once a month. This program is designed to pro-
vide listening experiences through which our children may become more aware of music,
its rhythm, melody, tempo, intensity, mood, structure and style. The Elementary
School Chorus, composed at that time of some eighty-five voices, were invited to per-
form at the annual conference of elementary school supervisors and principals of the
State Department of Education last April. The Art Department began during the present
school year a curriculum guide to serve as a guide for a graded program in all media
of art. The Physical Education program continues to show great strength and quality.

The use of audio-visual materials has increased considerably. The greater use

Team teaching, a technique in organization within a grade, whereby all teachers
cooperate in the formulation of the planning of an extended unit of work and join to-

in this area.

in science, mathematics and geography under the National Defense Education Act, under
which the Federal Government contributes one-half the cost.

FACILITIES
This school year finds the present elementary school facilities occupied. About
900 pupils occupy the 24 rooms in the Elementary School, 8 rooms in the Annex, and 2
rooms in Brooks House. For the next school year it is anticipated that we will move
our sixth grades to the new high school addition, allowing us more rooms for the next
two or three years in the present elementary school facilities. Under present condi-
tions with present elementary school facilities, and the inevitable increase of the
junior-senior high school population, serious pre-planning and consideration must be
given to elementary school facilities in about 1964. It is fortunate that the town
has seen fit to continue its school housing investigating committee. It is recommend-
ed that this committee be continued SO that it can continue to advise on the school
housing program.

Report of the School Nurse
for the Year 1960
During the year, the school nurses have assisted Dr. John DeLeo, our School
Physician, doing physical examinations on students In grades 4, 7, and 10. Also, the
High School students who participate in the various athletic programs. Children in
grade one have a physical examination during the spring and summer before entering
school. Forty of these children were examined here at school by Dr. DeLeo at two
special clinics set up for this purpose. Thirty-one children had boosters for diph-
theria and tetanus, and thirty-one children were vaccinated for smallpox at this
time. The rest of the children entering first grade were examined by their family
physicians, who made out a form and returned it to school and becomes part of the
child's permanent medical record.
The following programs are under the auspices of the Nashoba Associated Boards
of Health:
Dental Fluorine in which grades 2, 5, and 8 participate. Three hundred forty-
three pupils took advantage of this service. Of this number, one hundred ninety-
three were referred to their dentist for further dental care. Mrs. Darrah was the
dental hygienist in charge.
Mantoux Testing Program, which includes routine testing of first graders and also
new children who have not previously been tested and members of the school personnel,
Two hundred sixteen such tests were done this year, One child had a positive reaction
to the test and her family were rounded up and all had chest x-rays, which fortunate-
I y were negative. One of the adults also had a positive reaction but her chest x-ray
taken later was also negative.
Two Polio Clinics were held during the past summer. A number of school children
attended for their fourth polio shot. I am certain there are still a number of
school children who have not had this added protection. We are planning to send
questionaires home so as to find out where we stand and then be able to take steps to
have these children protected by a fourth or booster shot.
Vision testing has been completed. We are getting good cooperation from families
regarding corrections. Hearing test are not done as yet as we have not had an audio-
meter available.
One nurse is always on duty at the school during school hours to take care of
any emergency. Also, we arrange for transportation If a child becomes 111 and fre-
quently we take these children home If no transportation is available.
Weighing and measuring is done once in all the grades and a second time on grades
one, two, and three. Periodic check-up is done at the time of weighing and other times
when for various reasons it seems necessary.
In closing, we wish to thank you, Mr. Billings, the principals, the teaching
staff and the school personnel for your courtesy and cooperation.
Respectfully submitted,
SIGNE E. SCHULTZ, R. N.
School Nurse
January 23, 1961.

Report of the School Lunch Program
for the Year 1960
Dear Mr. Billings:
This is my Fifth Annual Report of the Lunenburg School Lunch Program and is for
the school year, September 1959 to September 1960.
During this period we fed 136,332 Type "A" lunches to students and 10.134
lunches to adults for a period of 175 lunch days, which is an average of 837 lunches
a day in both schools combined. Beyond these figures, we sold 146,947 half-pints of
milk under the special milk program, as well as 4,239 half-pints to adults. That is
a total of 297,652 half-pints of milk purchased in all schools during the year.
Financially, this past year was better than previous ones. This fall (1960),
many more products are available on the U. S. D. A. commodity lists. We have re-
ceived butter, cheese, corn meal, dried eggs, white flour, dried milk, rice, dried
pea beans, frozen hamburg, peanut butter, canned apricots, canned peaches, canned
cherries, canned tomatoes, and frozen turkeys. Many of these items are available in
quantities but others are restricted as to number of students participating in the
program for the month.
Participation percentage-wise for the year in the Junior-Senior High School was
75% according to attendance and 71% according to membership. In the Elementary
School, participation according to attendance was 60% and 56% according to member-
ship.
The personnel in the Junior-Senior High School consists of Mrs. Dorothy Maki as
the Head-Cook, and Mrs. Myrtle Eastman as the Baker. The general-workers are Mrs.
Myrtle Dickey, Mrs. Edna Huff, Mrs. Ethel Malone, Mrs. Fanny Ovaska, and Mrs. Emelian
Perry.
In the Elementary School, Mrs. Phyllis Prouty is the Manager; Mrs. Hazel Whitney
the Head-Cook;Mrs. Helen Hebert as Assistant-Cook, and Mrs. Lucy Chartrand the Baker.
The general-workers are Mrs. Bertha Gelgner, Mrs. Elizabeth Gilchrest, and Mrs. Rita
Seguin.
The success of a program depends so much on these people, and the ones listed
are wonderful workers and do many things beyond their duties and expectations of
them. Without them I could not carry on a good program.
At the Thanksgiving dinner this November 22, 1960, we served 1,518 Type "A"
lunches. The Elementary Annex pupils were transported to the high school to enjoy
the meals with us. At Christmas, we served 1,394 lunches, The tables in the Elemen-
tary School were appropriately decorated by the students and teachers, and the High
School Cafeteria by Mrs. Drumm and her art students.
I continued to attend the Equipment Seminars which began in the Fall before.
These were held at Lexington, Dorchester, and Shrewsbury.
The Spring annual workshops were held in Boston and Springfield during our
April vacation. The lunchroom personnel drove to the Cathedral High School in
Springfield for one day and I attended the conference for the three day period.
The annual Summer Workshop held at the Fitchburg Teachers' College (July 18-22,
1960) was attended nearly 100% by our personnel. Lunenburg was host for these days
