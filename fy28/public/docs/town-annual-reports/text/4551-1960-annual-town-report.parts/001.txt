SELECTMEN
David A. Eyles, Chairman
Howard A. Hamel
John C. Shields
The record for 1960 shows many tangible accomplishments. Some pro-
blems were still confronting us at the end of the year even though consider-
able effort was exerted by the Board of Selectmen. The substantial comple-
tion of West Townsend Road adds to the miles of highways which, from
year to year, have been brought up to the requirements of modern day
vehicular traffic. A long-standing drainage problem which plagued Gra-
ham Street and its residents was finally eliminated. While much remains
to be done, the preliminary work was started on White Street and Shirley
Road. The same is true of other roads, and the year 1961 will see more
progress in that direction. It should be noted that a program of road im-
provement often necessitates the taking of land. The amount taken or
needed is mostly inconsequential. Nevertheless, there seems to be an
increasing reluctance on the part of the citizenry to forego damage claims.
Often, the demand is all out of proportion to the value of the portion
needed. The cost of meeting these demands and the costs of litigation, if
such becomes necessary, when added to the construction costs, tends to
make a proposed project too expensive to consider. This situation was
true in at least one case during the year.
Additional sidewalks were constructed along Whalom Road and
Leominster Road, thus providing greater safety for the walking public.
During the summer months a major road oiling program was carried
out. Although somewhat costly, this work pays for itself by greatly re-
ducing road maintenance costs which would otherwise become necessary.
A milestone was reached when, at the special meeting in August,
the voters adopted a Protective By-Law, sometimes called a Zoning Law.
Under the law, the Board of Selectmen are responsible for administering and enforcing it. Follow-
ing its adoption, the Selectmen appointed a Board of Appeals consisting of five members and three
associate members. While the by-law may step on a few toes, the passage of time will make mani-
fest its importance to the general welfare of our community. Along with the majority who voted for
it, we urge strict compliance with its provisions in order to derive maximum benefit.
While this is essentially a report of your Selectmen, it is difficult not to allude to problems
which come under the Board of Health. It is clearly apparent that the lack of a central system of
water drainage and sewerage is the underlying cause of the seemingly never-ending complaints
from residents with water and sewage problems. We realize that there is no solution that money
could not buy. With that realization in mind, however, it is necessary to adopt measures which
will be consistent with our means as well as the proper safeguarding of the public health and wel-
fare. Therefore, while endeavoring to be considerate and lenient, we are oftentimes faced with
the necessity of denying sewerage permits, restricting the sale of subdivision lots, requiring mini-
mum lot sizes, and from time to time, adopting more stringent health regulations. For obvious
- 5 -
===PAGE 8===
reasons we do not advocate a public sewerage system.
Therefore, we do not think it amiss to point out the reasons for the Town's admitted inability
to provide a satisfactory solution to every such complaint. It is no secret that the cost of town
government continues to go up. As the budget demonstrates, the schools get the lion's share of the
tax dollar, and that share increases each year. It is unfortunate that the Town's expenditures can-
not somehow be tailored more carefully to the Town's collective pocketbook. Although we have
more new homes and, fortunately, the new C. A. Cross Company plant, plus two new bowl-
ing alleys, our tax base has not broadened enough to allow our tax income to keep pace with a ris-
ing budget. The inevitable result of this pattern is a higher tax rate. We must learn to differen-
tiate between what we would like to have and do, and what we can afford to have and do.
As in other years, we invite your attention to the reports of other Town departments, andtoall
other interesting and informative material contained within these pages.
We wish to extend our sincere thanks to all who have in any way contributed to the welfare of
our Town.
LEGAL
Robert J. Larkin, Town Counsel
During the early part of 1960, the Town Counsel attended several
meetings with the Selectmen and various other Committees and Town
officials which had to do, generally, with the preparation and framing of
articles for presentation to the voters at the annual meeting. In the pro-
cess, depending upon the subject matter, research and study was quite
often required. Apart from the articles, many questions on procedure and
administrative matters bearing on the conduct of the meeting were received and answered.
In addition to these seasonal matters, the routine business of the various Town boards and commit-
tees came in for a share of attention.
In the wake of the annual meeting, a great many matters required follow-through treatment.
A good example of this was the vote to construct an addition to the Junior-Senior High School.
The Town Counsel met several times with the Building Committee and the Architect, and reviewed
technical bid procedure, proposed contracts and other documents necessary to an undertaking of
this magnitude. Considerable time was spent in pursuing a bill through the Legislature enabling
US to vote money out of the Stabilization Fund, at the special meeting in August. Much time was
spent prior to the special meeting in August on matters to be considered at the meeting.
Due to some changes in the election laws, it was necessary to meet with the Town Clerk and
the Registrars prior to the recent election. There were also a few claims against the town, several
hearings of one kind or another before the Board of Selectmen and Board of Health, some land
purchased, and a number of agreements, contracts, easements and permits were drawn.

Veterans' exemption has been extended to those veterans who have been awarded the Con-
gressional Medal of Honor, the Distinguished Service Cross, or the Navy Cross, and if they other-
wise qualify under Clause 22 of Section 5 of Chapter 59 they are now exempt to the amount of
$4,000 of value on real estate.
A maximum rate on motor vehicle and trailer excise has been established at $66 per $1,000
valuation. Had the excise been based, as in the past, on the average of all of the real estate tax
rates in the Commonwealth, the 1961 excise rate would have been $69 per $1,000 valuation.
A question frequently asked is, "How much will my valuation be increased if I landscape my
lot, or paint my house?" The answer is that no increase is ever made for these reasons. The
Assessors feel that a well-kept house and grounds is an asset to the Community and should be
encouraged.

TOWN CLERK
Mary L. Padula, Town Clerk
The Town Office swarmed with activity during 1960. The Annual
and Special Town Meetings, as well as the Presidential and State Prim-
aries and the State Election on November 8th, involved a considerable
amount of preparation and included the registration of 484 new voters.
During the month of January the 1959 Town Report was prepared. Throughout the year, a record
number of births, marriages, deaths, pole locations, grants of easement, agreements, contracts,
etc. were recorded and, the dog, fish and game, annual, seasonal and Sunday licenses issued total-
led seventeen hundred seventy-three.
Applications pertaining to bui Idings, including notifications of intention to bui Idand sanitary,
zoning, plumbing and driveway permits, were received and processed in cooperation with the
Board of Selectmen, Nashoba Board of Health, Planning Board, Plumbing Inspector, Board of
Appeals, and Highway Superintendent. A total of 467 applications were received and processed.
Keeping abreast with new laws, particularly those affecting taxation, bond issues, invest-
ments, elections and vital statistics, by studying advanced copies and through frequent contacts
with the Town Counsel, involved a considerable amount of time, as did the preparation of numerous
statements and reports for various State agencies, realtors, industries, banks, etc.
The search of old Town records in connection with inquiries regarding family histories and
genealogies has more than once resulted in the Town Office personnel becoming engrossed in
Lunenburg's historic past. Frequent comments by genealogists indicate that the Cunningham History
of the Town of Lunenburg (1719 - 1886) is far more complete than historic data available in most
cities and towns.
Although unable to answer every one of the many and diversified questions which were asked
at the Town Office, such as, "What is the proper attire for the roller skating rink ?", "What is the
best method of disposing of swarming bees?", "What do you do with dead skunks in the road?",
and "Is deer meat which has remained in a freezer for two and one-half years still edible?", we
were able to ascertain that a permit is required to keep a racoon, did endeavor to supply the
names of President-elect Kennedy's newly appointed cabinet members, and trust that, in general,
we were able to satisfactorily dispose of the majority of the problems with which we were confronted.
Helping to carry out the duties of this office, which also includes the work of the Treasurer
and Tax Collector, as well as clerical work connected with the Board of Selectmen and Boardof
Health, are Mrs. Sylvia Oksanen and Miss Joyce White who are employed on a full time basis and
Mrs. Ine Pihlaja who averages twenty hours per week.
Any person conducting a business in the Commonwealth under a title other than his real
name is required by law to file a business certificate in the office of the Town Clerk. As many
inquiries are received conceming the various commercial enterprises located within our Town, it
is felt that businessmen will benefit greatly by complying with this law.
Fees received by the Town Clerk for the year 1960 totalled $1,206.75.

Sherwood G. Winchester, Chief
Frederick B. Hobbs, Sergeant

The continuing growth of our Town once more accounted for asizable
increase in the activity of the Police Department, with motorvehicle vio-
lations still topping the list. Larcenies, liquor violations, assaults and
vandalisms also increased and continue to be of great concern to this de-
partment. These violations involved many hours of investigation and were
usually followed by court cases or some kind of disciplinary action.
Highway safety becomes more important each year in view of the
greater number of vehicles on the road. The one hundred fourteen acci-
dents which occurred during 1960 exceeded the previous year's total by
five. As included in this figure were four fatalities, it is obvious that
more emphasis must be concentrated on highway safety and patrol. Chief
Winchester, therefore, strongly recommends additional manpower for
traffic control purposes.
The Annual Bicycle Safety Program was conducted with the cooperation of the school officials
and the Parent-Teachers' Association. Inspections were held at the Elementary School and at
Wallis Park in Whalom. Approximately one hundred fifty children presented their bicycles for
inspection, with police officials and parents making adjustments, and awarding safety stickers
to those who passed.
Juvenile problems, including teenage drinking seem to be on the increase in Lunenburg, as
well as in other communities. Definite action has been taken where minors are involved in the use
of liquor. Through the cooperation of the Registry of Motor Vehicles, minors found drinking and
driving, lose their license to operate and may face court action. Parents of teenage children
should be acutely aware of the seriousness and extent of this problem and are urged to do every-
thing in their power to discourage drinking by minors. School officials and the Parent-Teachers'
Association have cooperated with the Police Department in helping to solve this problem.

Control of Public Nuisances
Richard W. Brown, Superintendent
The Dutch Elm disease is the most serious shade tree problem facing our Town today. This
disease cannot be eradicated from the United States. It can, however, be controlled at the com-
munity level. This control can only be accomplished after a sincere effort by local officials and
a desire by the citizens to preserve those elms remaining. Your local moth superintendent cannot
perform his duties as outlined in Chapter 132 of the General Laws without your full cooperation.
When certain basic steps are followed to completion, excellent control of the disease can be
maintained. These steps are as follows: (1) A good sanitation program which means the complete
removal and destruction of all dead and dying elm wood including recently dead or dying whole
trees or parts of trees, such as broken branches, elm wood piled on private property forfuture
fireplace use, or elm wood left lying around for various other reasons; (2) A good sampling pro-
gram in order to determine the extent of spread of the disease within your community; (3) The
prompt removal and destruction of the confirmed Dutch Elm diseased trees as determined by the
sampling program; and (4) The dormant spraying of the valued trees as a supplement to the sani-
tation program.
Pursuant to the requirements of Chapter 132, Section 14 of the General Laws, $1,802.51 has
been established by the State as the financial liability of our municipality for insect pest and
Dutch Elm disease control.
Although the above financial liability represents the amount required by law, it does not re-
necessarily reflect the amount needed to effect good control. The Moth Superintendent is, therefore,
requesting that $2,600.00 be appropriated for these purposes in 1961. Expenditures over the
financial liability will entitle Lunenburg to aid from the Bureau of Insect Pest Control.

DOG OFFICER
Elwood C. Erickson, Dog Officer
The owner or keeper of a dog three months old or older must license
it annually, on or before March 31st, and shall cause it to wear a collar
or harness with dog tag attached. If the tag is lost a substitute tag must
be secured from the Town Clerk. Any dogs acquired after April 1st must
also be licensed as well as those which become three months of age after
that date.
The fee for every dog license is $2.00 for a male and $5.00 for a female. If a female dog
has been spayed it can be licensed for $2.00 providing a proper certificate from a registered
veterinarian is filed with the Clerk. Although a dog license is valid throughout the State, in case
of permanent removal to another Town, a transfer license must be secured from such Town within
thirty days. A fee of $.25 is charged for a transfer license. Special licenses are issued to owners
or keepers of kennels.
During 1960, one hundred nineteen complaints were received and disposed of by the Dog
Officer, sixteen dogs were hit and/or killed by motor vehicles, twenty dog bites were reported,
twenty-four dogs were destroyed, and six were sold and the money turned over to the County. The
Dog Officer worked eighty-seven hours and traveled six hundred forty-eight miles.
TOWN FOREST
Richard C. Hayes, Chairman
During the past year your Town Forest Committee, in cooperation
with the Planning Board, has devoted further study to our natural re-
sources in the light of what should be done to prepare for the future
growth of our Town. Fortunately, a readymade plan is now available to
US through the Self-Help Conservation Program of the Massachusetts De-
partment of Natural Resources.
The initial step of this program entails the appointment of a Town Conservation Committee and
would enable US to receive State aid up to fifty per cent of the cost of any suitable property ac-
quired. Practically all of any such land acquired would be woodland and swamps and would have
negligible assessed valuation so virtually no loss of revenue would result. The long range bene-
fits to Lunenburg are immense.
As of December thirty-first of this year, forty-two cities and towns in Massachusetts had al-
ready joined this program and fifty others have it under consideration. We urge you to vote in
favor of the article which has been inserted in the Warrant for the 1961 Annual Meeting calling
for the acceptance of certain Chapters of the General Laws which would permit the establishment
of a conservation committee.

Sealer of Weights and Measures
Raymond F. Butterfield, Sealer
Today has been described as the atomic age, the jet age and the age
of new frontiers. To the weights and measures official it has become the
pre-packaged age. In view of the changes which have taken place in the
field of buying and selling, weights and measures supervision is indispensable.
Scales in Lunenburg are regularly tested for accuracy once a year, and unannounced spot
checks are frequently made. This is true too of gasoline pump meters and fuel oil truck meters and

In our Town the Board of Selectmen serves as the Board of Health. A generalized Public Health Nursing Pro-
gram is carried out by your Town Nurses. This includes School Nursing and Community Nursing, as well as bedside
care of the sick in their homes, infant and pre-school health supervision, supervision of tuberculosis cases and con-

During the year, 1,040 visits, classified as follows, were made: 814 for bedside care; 168 to infants and pre-

further check-up and booster shots during the pre-school years. Dr. Eleanor H. Smith, Medical Director of the

cost of $.25 per person. This service was offered the member towns by the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health.

per-visit fee is charged, based on an ability to pay. No request for service is ever refused on the basis that the
person is unable to pay, but all persons requesting service must be under medical care. Fees collected are turned
over to the Town Treasurer each month.

the monthly staff meetings held at Nashoba Health Center in Ayer.

WELFARE
Thelma LeBlanc, Director
The Board of Public Welfare now adminsters five programs, Old Age
Assistance, Medical Assistance for the Aged, Aid to Dependent Children,
Disability Assistance and General Relief. The category of General Re-
lief is a program that is supported entirely by local funds. The other four
programs are Federal-State programs of financial aid for needy people.
The Town receives reimbursements for these four programs from the Federal
and State Governments according to an established formula.
The Federal-State programs were first established through the Social Security Act of 1935.
At that time there were two programs, Old Age Assistance and Aid to Dependent Children. In
1950, the category of Disability Assistance was added. This year, a new program entitled "Medi-
cal Assistance for the Aged", was enacted and became effective as of October 1, 1960. The Old
Age Assistance Law, Chapter 118A, was amended to include this section to provide medical care
for the aged in cases where only medical help is needed.
- 44
===PAGE 47===
The basic principle underlying these programs is that government should provide protection to
Individuals and families against the social risks that are inherent in an industrialized society.
Social insurance has extended protection to more and more working people. The payment of
Social Security benefits has solved the problems of income maintenance for many, however, the
benefits are not sufficiently high to meet living costs and many people are still in need of public
aid and related social services. High living costs have also made it necessary for many persons,
particularly the elderly who are drawing Old and Survivors Insurance benefits, to seek supple-
mentary assistance.
Old Age Assistance is available for elderly persons who are in need. The person may have no
income or he may have a small income and needs supplementation to meet maintenance costs. In
addition to financial assistance, other health and welfare services are made available to recipients.
Aid to Dependent Children is one of the provisions that the Social Security Act made for the
care and protection of children. One principle established was that children should be
guaranteed the protection of being reared in their own homes in the eventof the loss of the bread-
winner. Many of these families receive financial help and important related health services. The
relatively large number of families receiving aid because the father has deserted, gives evidence
of social problems on which further help is needed.
Disability Assistance is a program that covers persons 18 years of age or over, who are in need.
Need is determined in accordance with budgetary standards. This program covers either a physical
or mental disability.
In the past twenty-five years we have come a long way toward the goal of providing economic
protection for all people. We live in a world of rapid movement, one in which social and industri-
al changes take place daily. Our programs must and do respond to these changing conditions. The
new program of Medical Assistance for the Aged is an illustration of this response. It is an effort to
provide more adequate aid to meet the needs of people living in a modern society. The budgetary
standards are constantly being changed to meet different needs. Effective July 1, 1960, payments
on all categories of assistance were increased 5.2% because of the increase in the cost of living
index, as reported by the Division on Necessaries of Life.
The existence of such an agency as the Board of Public Welfare, is an expression of communi-
ty concern that its human resources be conserved. It is a recognition of the importance and worth
of the individual. Need is not necessarily linked with personal inadequacy, but is usually created
by failures of society and of the economic system, over which the individual has no control. The
provisions of the Public Assistance programs contribute to the stability of the American family.
The financial help given to the needy aged, blind, or disabled individual withinhis family setting,
often has enabled him to remain at home without interruption of family life. Efforts to rehabilitate
needy disabled persons have helped some of them to gain greaterself-sufficiency, and others to re-
sume responsibility for the care and support of their families. Perhaps most important of all is the
contribution of public assistance to the morale of the family. Many times financial assistance has
enabled the needy family to preserve, in the face of adversity, the continuity of close family ties
and other strengths of family living.

4-H CLUBS
In 1960 the Lunenburg 4-H Clubs had a very successful year. The January Rally was a sliding
party and cookout at the home of Mrs. Stillman. Other Rallies during the year consisted of a
Safety Program and Talent Night. "Safety" was also the theme used in the window display for
National 4-H Week, for which the Stitch Wits Club was awarded 2nd prize.
In June the 4-H'ers furnished the entertainment at a meeting of the Lunenburg Grange. The
Grange also gave two half-camperships to 4-H members. Other camperships were won by club
members for selling cookies. This year a new product, a cranberry table syrup, was soldexclusively
by the 4-H clubs of Massachusetts to help the county clubs, the National Foundation and also the
cranberry growers.
Worcester County is divided into three districts. Lunenburg is in the Northeast District with
Mrs. Marguarite Patton of Sterling, as Assistant County Agent. On June 4th Lunenburg was host
to our district for Junior Achievement Day. A large number of 4-H'ers exhibited their completed
projects and many took part in the dress revue as well as in the demonstrations and public speaking
contest.
Members of the Dairy Club, under the leadership of George Mason, showed their animals at
eight different fairs. One of the outstanding events of the year was the Worcester County Dairy
Tour, at which 4-H'ers from all over Worcester County participated in the judging of cattle.
Gregg Stillman placed first out of over 100 contestants and was given an Ayrshire calf. Scott
Penniman placed third and Roger Abell placed 4th.
The Senior 4-H'ers took part in many events. Some members attended the Junior Leaders Con-
- 54 -
===PAGE 57===
ference at the University of Massachusetts and also took part in the 4-H Pageant at the Eastern
States Exposition at Springfield and the Senior Girls Dress Revue at Worcester.
In October a new club was organized by Joann Rinki. On November 10th the Annual Banquet
was held in the Parish Building with about 80 club members and parents present. Mrs. Patton pre-
sented pins and awards to the 4-H'ers who had completed their projects.
Lunenburg made a splendid showing at the 13th Annual Medal Winners Banquet held in Sutton
on December 13th. The Dress Revue and Clothing Award was presented to: Carol Hastings, Lois
Dahlgren, Susan Hastings, Helen Rinki, Doris Geigner, and Caroline Brown. Sheron Stillman re-
ceived the Recreation Award and the 4-H Key was awarded to Doris Geigner and Joann Rinki.
HOME DEPARTMENT
Approximately twenty local homemakers belong to the Lunenburg Extension Group. Morning
meetings are held on the second Thursday of every month, usually in the members' homes.
Early in January Miss Alice Bair of the Worcester County Extension Service gave an interesting
talk to the group on "Facts, Frauds and Deceptions". Other winter activities consisted of a de-
monstration entitled "Brighten a Corner in Your Home" by leader trainers Mrs. Howard Hamel and
Mrs. Arthur Popp, and included three meetings at the United Parish House on "Community Meals".
In the Spring, Mrs. Adelard Charron and Mrs. Egide Mathieu taught "Project Closet Capers"
and "Use of Sewing Machine Attachments" to groups of interested homemakers. In May the officers
attended a Recognition Luncheon held in Worcester, and Mrs. Mathieu and Mrs. Gordon. spoke
over radio station WFGM regarding the various projects and the purpose of extension work.
Summer activities included "Outdoor Cookery" with Mrs. Gordon and Mrs. Mathieu teaching
the latest methods of preparing picnic foods, and the Annual Field Day in Sutton. A luncheon
served to 13 local members by Mrs. Robert Garneau and Mrs. Edgar Letarte was a practical de-
monstration of "Cooked and Prepared Foods for the Freezer". Recipes and household hints were
sent in for publication in cookbooks which will be sold to aid the Mildred Thomas Scholarship Fund.
On October 13th, "Chair Caning" was taught to a group of local women by Mrs. Gordon and
Mrs. Mathieu. "Understanding Life Insurance" was discussed in November, with Mrs. Russell
Travis and Mrs. Arthur Popp as leaders.
Christmas Open House in Worcester on December 6th was well-attended. One of the main
attractions was a "Children's Centerpieces" contest which various towns entered. A Minature
Marshmallow Castle Centerpiece, planned by Mrs. Betty Thomas and her five helpers, won honor-
able mention.
Future projects include: Making Children's Coats, Chair Caning, Modern Vegetable Cookery,
Yeast Breads, and Understanding Clothing Labels.
New members are welcomed by the Extension Group. Anyone who is interested in the Ex-
tension Program should contact the Chairman, Mrs. Egide Mathieu.
