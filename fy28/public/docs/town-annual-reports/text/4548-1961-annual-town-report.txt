EINO O. ToKo
1905 - 1961

in General Court
1952 - 1954
Selectman of Lunenburg
1947 - 1954
Finance Committee

1960 - 1961
With the untimely death of Eino O. Toko, January 6, 1962, Lunenburg
lost a good friend and a fine citizen. Although a relatively short span of
years was allotted to him in this life, the hallmark of those years of his
public life was his regard for the well-being of the community he loved.
His aims and his accomplishments echoed the principle to which he sub-
scribed - that the fullness of human living is possible to mankind only
through the cooperation of all in the pursuit of the common good. His
years of public service in his own town of Lunenburg and as a member of
the Great and General Court of the Commonwealth were guided by that
principle. So too, were those years he so ardently devoted to active
leadership and zealous membership in so many organizations having,
significantly, the same fine principle as one of their objectives. Always
willing to help a worthy person, or promote a good cause, he never
allowed political expendiency to obscure the public interest. While a warm,
kind and friendly person, he expounded or defended his principles with
vigor. His strength, courage and perseverence inspired the same qualities
in others. The loss to the community and town government is even greater
than that to US who called him our friend.
This report is respectfully dedicated to his memory.

SELECTMEN
Howard A. Hamel, Chairman
John C. Shields
David A. Eyles
Now and again many are heard to inquire about the duties and respon-
sibilities of selectmen. In looking back over the reports of past years,
while they constitute a resume of each year's doings, they do not directly
answer the oft repeated question - "What do selectmen do?". In setting
down this, our Annual report for 1961, we will strive to weave into it some
sort of an answer. We say - "a sort of an answer" - because, although the
only real way to describe what a selectman is and what he does is to recite
his numerous duties and responsibilities, varied and unrelated though they
may be, the space alloted prevents such a detailed recitation.
In colonial days, the authority of government in the Massachusetts
town was vested in all the male adult inhabitants, who expressed theirwill
through the town meeting. This town meeting met frequently throughout
the year and, by popular vote, elected a moderator, levied taxes, made
appropriations and provided for the needs of public works, schools and
the poor. As the towns grew in size and the problems of local government
increased in complexity, it became impractical to govern directly by town
meeting, and the plan was adopted of selecting a small group of citizens
to supervise the functioning of the town government between town meetings.
This group was sometimes called the executive committee and sometimes the
board of selectmen. Under the latter name, this office has persisted to the
present time and has enjoyed an unbroken tradition of authority. It has
been written that its popularity can be attributed to the fact that this form
of government is adaptable to changing conditions and that itshistoric
prestige may be maintained while its operational structure is streamlined
to meet the demands of the twentieth century. In short, it is a workable
form of government.
Your Board has the responsibility and duty of supervising the work of the Highway, Police
and Fire Departments, and works in close conjunction with each, especially the Highway Depart-
ment. The Whalom Drainage Project was launched in 1961 by the Highway Department and, while
there is more work to be done, great strides have been made toward completion. Over the years a
total of $25,000 was voted for this project. The major difficulty was securing proper easements to
enable US to dispose of the water outside the limits of our Town. These were finally obtained but
not until we spent endless hours of consultation and bargaining. Had we been unable to acquire
these easements, the result could easily have been another $25,000 in costs and at least a two-year
delay in starting the work. Previous reports of this Board have emphasized serious drainage problems
and the need for a solution. The Whalom Drainage Project is, we think, a prime example of what
can be done in the way of helping to solve these problems. Other highway improvement projects,
still in the works, include White Street and Shirley Road. The relocation and improvement of West
Townsend Road and other smaller highway improvement projects, including the elimination of the

===PAGE 10===
dangerous blind curve and knoll which existed on Mulpus Road, were completed during the year.
During 1961, we were able to obtain the services of Mr. Robert W. Sealey, as our new Highway
Superintendent. Mr. Sealey brings to US years of highway departmental "know how". We are sure
that in the immediate years ahead the Town will greatly benefit from his knowledge.
Another responsibility and duty of the Board is the administration of the Protective By-Law
insofar as the initial processing of applications for building permits is concerned. We have lived
under the By-Law for about a year and a half now. Your Board has been criticized at times for
carrying out the spirit of the law. Perhaps, in some cases, the application of the law has seemed
to work a hardship. However, we are confident that our citizens will agree that it has, over all,
greatly benefited the Town by protecting property values and contributing to its orderly growth.
Still another duty and responsibility of your Selectmen is that of constituting the town's
licensing authority. There are so many endeavors and activities which, under the law, must be
licensed, the whole of this space would be required to list them. Nevertheless the Selectmen
have an obligation to investigate all applications, weigh the facts and make a judgment to grant
or deny. Oftentimes it is necessary to give hearings on proposed action against a licensee for some
breach of conduct or regulation. Sometimes this involves a further hearing before a court or some
higher State tribunal.
Any list of those activities coming under the jurisdiction of the Board of Selectmen should
include, in addition to the above, Dog Officer, Sealer of Weights and Measures, Town Hall,
Veterans' Services, Town insurance, acceptance of streets, assessment of betterments, preparation
of Town meeting warrants and the appointment of non-elective officials.
In Lunenburg the Selectmen also serve as the Board of Health and Board of Public Welfare.
While, strictly speaking, Health and Welfare matters do not belong in the Selectmen's report, the
duties and responsibilities of the Board of Health are so enmeshed with some of the Selectmen's
duties, there is hardly a break in the continuity of ouractivities and operation from either office.
We have given practical effect to this situation by correlating the handling, processing and
issuing of building, sanitation and plumbing permits. We are thus better able to at once exercise
control over a given situation whether it involves the attention of the Board of Selectmen or the
Board of Health. Needless to say, without the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health as our agent,
we should be completely unable to function as we do. Another Board of Health matter, the Town
Dump, was given considerable attention during 1961, the refuse having been pushed back and a
new front established. This method will make it possible to continue using the present dump site
for a number of years. We have also initiated a program of rodent control which we hope to con-
tinue in the interest of the public health.
We were most fortunate in having Mrs. Thelma LeBlanc as our Welfare Director, and most
unhappy to lose her during the year to the State Department of Public Welfare. However, we wish
her success in her new endeavor. We were again fortunate though, in being able to promote Mrs.
Glady's Goodale to the position of Director.
As is our practice, we invite you to read carefully and thoughtfully all of the other reports
and statistics contained in this Annual Report. Our thanks go out to the many helping hands who,
by their willing cooperation, have greatly assisted US in the discharge of our duties and have thereby
made our tasks easier and more pleasant.

LEGAL
Robert J. Larkin, Town Counsel
The activity which precedes every annual town meeting is usually in
high gear at or about the time each new year hovers into view. The
pressure builds up steadily and carries right through the annual meeting.
This was true of the period between January 1 and March 4, 1961. There
was a constant interchange of questions and answers concerning matters
affecting this or that department or committee. All articles and related
subjects were studied in order that any factual or legal questions might be
satisfactorily answered at the meeting.
It is not meant to be implied that all activities ceased following the annual meeting. The
Town Counsel attended most of the weekly meetings of the Selectmen and Board of Health through-
out the year. Oftentimes it was necessary at these meetings to offer legal advice in relation to the
various and sundry matters coming before the respective boards. From time to time, during the
year, consultations were held with many of the other Town departments desiring legal advice on
matters directly concerning them. Several public hearings, which required previous preparation
and follow-up attention, were also conducted. An example of the latter was an appeal from a
denial by the Selectmen of a petition for the transfer of a liquor license. This appeal was held
bèfore the Alcoholic Beverage Commission in Boston.
The usual quota of easements, agreements, contracts and other documents were handled
during the year. An especial amount of time and attention was called for in working up and
securing easements for the Whalom Drainage Project. Much time was likewise given to the pre-
paration of the legal work required in connection with the Town's accèptance of streets in the
Rolling Acres Subdivision.
In these changing times much of what used to be routine in administering the affairs of town
government is now threatening to become involved and complex. New legislation, new court de-
cisions and seemingly broader concepts affecting such things as planning, zoning, taxes and other
municipal functions account for these changes. This, of course, is inevitable so long as communi-
ties continue to expand and the needs of the inhabitants become greater and different than yester-
year's. Under our system of government all these changes must be made, controlled and regulated
by law.

TOWN CLERK, TAX COLLECTOR, TREASURER
Mary L. Padula, Town Clerk
The Town Clerk, Tax Collector and Treasurer also serves as Clerk of
the Board of Selectmen, Board of Health, Board of Public Welfare, and
Licensing Authority, and is a Registrar of Voters.
The Town Clerk acts as custodian of permanent Town records, Town
contracts and agreements, and of the Town Seal. She is the official re-
corder of all Town business. She administers oaths and certifies official
documents. The Town Clerk's office sets up, amends and maintains per-
manent files on Town By-laws; Board of Health Regulations; Public Highways; records of births,
marriages and deaths; minutes of elections and Town meetings; locations of poles, wires, and
conduits; grants of easement; and all other deeds, agreements and contracts. It is in this office
that business certificates are filed, financing statements are recorded, and licenses are issued.
The Town Clerk issues transcripts of Town records, and, as Justice of the Peace, performs civil
marriage ceremonies. She supervises the preparation of the official street listing, prepares voting
lists and official ballots used in Town elections, and is responsible for the conduct of elections
and Town Meetings.
During 1961, 142 births, 75 marriage intentions, 80 marriages, 56 deaths, and 259 financing
statements were recorded. Licenses issued included: 647 dog, 626 fish and game, 208 annual,
1 seasonal, 364 Sunday.
The Town Treasurer receives and takes charge of all money belonging to the Town and pays
over and accounts for the same. She is the only one who pays out monies, and does so only on
the authorization of the Board of Selectmen. The Treasurer negotiates all borrowing, keeps de-
tailed records of all debt, and is cutodian of trust funds. This office must verify all amounts to
be paid and is concerned with the legality of proposed payments. The Treasureracts as accountant
and is responsible for keeping accounts, maintaining books, and rendering statements of appropriation
balances. She maintains payroll and personnel records, receives budget estimates from department
heads, and prepares annual financial reports.
The Tax Collector is charged with collecting taxes and excise committed by the Board of
Assessors. In addition to keeping detailed books and individual tax records, this office types and
mails tax bills, processes payments, abatements granted by Assessors and refunds, and enforces
collections, when necessary, with the aid of a deputy tax collector and the Registry of Motor
Vehicles. Taxes, motor vehicle excise, and betterment assessments collected during the year
totalled $915,764.61. No previous year taxes remained unpaid as of the end of the year. All
poll taxes committed in 1961 were either paid or abated. As of December 31, 1961, ninety-
three percent of the current year real estate and personal property taxes had been collected.
Although, at the year's end, unpaid motor vehicle excise totalled $19,906.85, $13,881.72
of this amount was received from the State and committed by the Assessors in late December and
the bills were, therefore, not sent out until 1962.

The Clerk of the Board of Selectmen and Agent of the Board of Health serves as liaison with
the general public, State and County or private agencies, department heads, and the Nashoba
Health Board, disposing of matters, the solutions of which are based on existing policies, rulesand
regulations, and referring all other problems to the Board of Selectmen or appropriate board, com-
mittee or department. She provides the Board with factual information upon which they base de-
cisions and formulate policy; writes specifications; and receives and adjusts complaints, making
referrals to the proper agency. The Town Office sets up systems of permits which pertain to build-
ing, including building, alterations, sanitary, zoning, plumbing, and driveway permits, and
cooperates with the Planning Board, Board of Appeals, Plumbing Inspector, Nashoba and the Highway
Superintendent in correlating same. The Clerk handles correspondence, schedules appointments
and prepares the Selectmen's meeting agenda. Other duties of a more occasional nature include
preparing annual budgets for, and supervising the ledger accounts of the miscellaneous departments
under the jurisdiction of the Selectmen, and preparation of the Town Meeting Warrants. One of
the most time consuming, yet rewarding, projects undertaken by this office is the preparation of the
Annual Town Report.
In the ten year period from 1951 to 1961 the volume of work in the Town Office has more than
doubled. The Town Clerk is grateful to Mrs. Sylvia Oksanen and Miss Joyce White, who work in
the Town Office on a full time basis, and to Mrs. Ine Pihlaja, who is employed part time, for
their loyal, conscientious, and expeditious handling of the numerous and varied tasks which they
are called upon to perform. However, in spite of our best efforts, we find that we cannot keep
pace with increased work loads and have, therefore, requested the services of an additional clerk
commencing in March 1962. The comparison which follows has been included to substantiate the
need for additional clerical assistance.

POLICE
Sherwood G. Winchester, Chief
Frederick B. Hobbs, Sergeant
William R. Sullivan, Patrolman
Elwood C. Erickson, Patrolman
One of the principal activities of the Police Department is the control
and regulation of motor vehicle traffic. It goes without saying that a
general increase in automobile registrations has resulted in additional motor
vehicles operating on all of our traveled ways. Our records indicate that
one hundred eighty persons were prosecuted for motor vehicle violations,
and one hundred eighty-four operators were reported to the Registrar of
Motor Vehicles for action. Experience has shown that constant patrolling
of the streets and highways is the best way to effect a safe driving program.
In 1961 several cases involving violation of the new Massachusetts
boating laws were prosecuted in the District Court. These laws seem to be
very effective and definitely help control the safe operation of boats on
our various lakes and ponds. Of interest is the fact that Lunenburg was
the first town in the County to have upheld by the Superior Court of
Worcester decision relative to motor boat violations.
A continuing awareness of the upward trend in teenage drinking caused your Police Department
to embark on a program to combat the problem. The aid of the Parent-Teachers Association and the
teenagers themselves was solicited. We believe that this program, which attracted State-wide
attention, has been most effective and wish to thank everyone involved for their excellent cooperation.
In 1961, forty-three cases, tried in the District Court, involved adults who were found guilty
of supplying liquor to teenagers. Those convicted received punishment ranging from fines to jail
sentences. This problem needs constant surveillance and should be given top priority until some
sort of a solution can be reached.
Our Auxiliary Police force should once more be commended for the many hours donated by its
members throughout the year. Under the able leadership of Captain Robert Grenier, that Depart-
ment is a valuable asset to the Town of Lunenburg.

FIRE
Roger G. Bigelow, Chief
James P. Deming, Assistant Chief
Bernard J. Paton, Captain
Henry D. Kelley, Jr., First Lieutenant
Charles P. Deming, Second Lieutenant
During the year the Department responded to 84 alarms: 13 building,
26 grass or brush, 3 chimney, 14 car, 2 oil burner, 7 auto accidents, 3
electrical appliance, 9 town dump, 1 bomb scare, 2 rescusitator, and 4
false alarms.
Damage to buildings was $139,732.58. Insurance paid to cover this loss was $136,865.95,
leaving a net loss of $2,866.63. Damage to contents was $392,379.39. Insurance paid to cover
this loss was $236,666.39, leaving a net loss of $155,713.00.
The Town had the largest fire loss in its history when three commercial establishments suffered
heavy fire damage. The Acme Country Store burned in February; Niemi Manufacturing Company
in June; and Craft Manufacturing Company in September. Under our Mutual Aid Program, assis-

burg worked at the fire and Leominster covered our station. At Craft's fire, Fitchburg worked at
the fire and also sent a truck to cover our station.
Our Department was called to Ayer, Leominster and Fitchburg to render assistance under the
Mutual Aid Program.
Regular meetings were held every other Monday evening. Educational programs pertaining to
fire fighting procedures and techniques were conducted, along with outside drills, weather permitting.
Our new FWD 750 gpm pumper, built by the Farrar Company of Woodville, Massachusetts, was
delivered. It is an excellent piece of equipment and should serve the Town well for the next twenty-
five years.
It is strongly recommended that the street numbering program be completed and that which has
been done to the present time be brought up to date.

TREE WARDEN
Richard W. Brown, Tree Warden
Moth Superintendent
Shade tree planting, the cutting and removal of dead trees, and insect
control were the major activities of the Tree Department during 1961.
Approximately one hundred trees were planted along the roadsides, with
particular emphasis having been placed on the replacement of trees which
had been removed for one reason or another. A total of $1,074.95 was
expended from the "Tree Warden" account.
The two chain saws, for which $650 was appropriated at this year's Annual Meeting, were
purchased in March. This equipment, which includes a fifty and an eighteen inch bar, is used
extensively to cut dead and diseased trees and has greatly improved the efficiency of the department.
Mr. Brown also serves as Moth Superintendent and administers the "Control of Public Nuisances"
budget. During 1961, eighty-six diseased elms were cut, cleaned and burned. Thirty-five samples
were taken from elm trees and sent to the University of Massachusetts to determine whether or not
the trees were infected with Dutch Elm Disease. The testing disclosed that all trees tested were
diseased. Nineteen of these trees were cut by the Department of Natural Resources at a cost to
the State of $480. In addition, seventy-six trees which were obviously diseased, and therefore were
not tested, were cut and removed. Control of public nuisances during 1961 cost the Town a total
of $2,310, $163.47 of which was reimbursed by the State.
Although Lunenburg's financial liability for 1961 is $1,802.51, the Department of Natural
Resources has recommended that $2,694.50 be appropriated for carrying out next year's program.
The Moth Superintendent has submitted a request for this amount.
DOG OFFICER
Elwood C. Erickson, Dog Officer
The owner or keeper of a dog three months old or older must license
it annually, on or before March 31st, and shall cause it to wear a collaror
harness with dog tag attached. If the tag is lost, a substitute tag must be
secured from the Town Clerk. Any dogs acquired after April 1st must also
be licensed as well as those which become three months of age after that
date.
The fee for every dog license is $2.00 for a male and $5.00 for a female. If a female dog has
been spayed it can be licensed for $2.00 providing a proper certificate from a registered veterin-
arian is filed with the Clerk. Although a dog license is valid throughout the State, in case of
permanent removal to another Town, a transfer license must be secured from such Town within thirty
days. A fee of $.25 is charged for a transfer license. Special licenses are issued to owners or
keepers of kennels.

Sealer of Weights and Measures
Raymond F. Butterfield, Sealer
A little known, but important function of our Town Government is the
sealing of weights and measures. It is almost impossible to name anything
we wear, use or eat that has not been weighed or measured once or many
times in its production and distribution.
The Weights and Measures Department protects both buyers and sellers.
In 1961, every family in Lunenburg was afforded this protection at a NET
cost to the Town of $524.12. The average family of four has been estimated to have a financial
stake of $100.00 per year in weights and measures enforcement. Businessmen have even greater
financial stakes in making sure that their scales and measuring devices are correct.
Scales in Lunenburg are regularly tested for accuracy once a year, and unannounced spot checks
are frequently made. This is true too of gasoline pumpmeters and fuel oil truck meters and all other
commercially used weighing and measuring devices. One hundred sixty-three weighing and measur-
ing devices were adjusted and sealed during 1961 by our Sealer of Weights and Measures.
All inhabitants or persons having usual places of business within the Town using weighing or
measuring devices for the purpose of buying or selling goods, wares or merchandise, for public
weighing or for hire or reward, are hereby notified to bring them in to be tested, adjusted and sealed.

CONSERVATION
Harold D. Harley, Chairman
Richard C. Hayes
Arthur E. Colvin
Mrs. Reginald Peabody
Charles L. Porter
A major milestone in assuring the best utilization of the natural resources of our
Town was the establishment, in 1961, of a Conservation Commission for the promotion
and development of the Town's natural resources and for the protection of its ground
water supply. Thanks to the Town Forest Committee and to the Planning Board, much
preliminary work with regard to conservation has already been completed. Surveys have been made, large areas of
land suitable for conservation have been charted on aerial maps, and a conservation map for the Town of Lunenburg
has been completed as part of the master plan study.
A real conservation program will do much to enrich the Town. It will preserve the natural beauty and will pro-
tect and promote wild life. It will protect the watershed resources and will save needed forests. It will offer many
educational, recreational and aesthetic advantages. As population grows, the danger of indiscriminate dump truck-
ing of swamps, bulldozing of hills and chain-sawing of forests threatens our open areas.
The wild life of the State is a precious resource which is diminished with the draining of wet lands which are
the primary breeding and feeding grounds of birds. With due regard to mosquito control, wet lands can serve useful
purpose also, in preserving the water table where ground water is used by the community.
As the official resource agency of the community, the Commission maintains an index of all open lands and
makes recommendations to the State or Municipality as to their proper use and management. It may accept deeds
or easements of land to be used for conservation purposes, and is charged with the management of donated or pur-
chased properties. Conservation Commissions may help private owners take advantage of the many opportunities
offered by governmental agencies to help them plan and finance land and water improvements. A new act has ex-
tended the powers of Commissions to buy or receive easements of the development (improvement) right in land.
This device permits the land to remain in its original use and on the tax rolls at that basis, and costs only a fraction
of the outright purchase of land. Experience in other communities indicates that citizens will, when properly
approached, donate title to wet lands or ledges which should be preserved.
Lunenburg has about ten thousand acres of forest land, six thousand acres of cleared land, two thousand acres
of swamp land and one thousand acres of lakes and ponds. A number of thousands of acres of land in Lunenburg that
are presently almost worthless to the owners could be paying dividends if a planned conservation program had been
used. The cost per year of a conservation program is minute compared with the permanent benefit obtained.
The Conservation Commission strongly recommends that the Town acquire as rapidly as possible several parcels
of land which are most desirable for conservation purposes. A sum of money not to exceed one percent of the
assessed valuation (approximately $5,000) may be appropriated annually into a conservation fund. The State will
reimburse up to fifty percent of amounts appropriated from this fund toward the cost of acquisition of land obtained
for conservation purposes which permits public uses for recreation. These uses have been defined to include roads,
parking areas, picnic, camping, hiking, hunting, fishing, nature study, and scenic outlook.
In view of the above, the Conservation Commission has recommended that $5,000 be appropriated in 1962 and
$5,000 in 1963, towards the purchase of the so-called "Cowdrey Lot". This parcel which contains approximately
two hundred ten acres and is situated between Mulpus Road and Route 2A, has been described from a conservation
standpoint as being the most desirable parcel of land in the entire Town.

CIVIL DEFENSE
Karl Manelius, Director
One of the first tasks assigned to Robert S. McNamara, Secretary of
Defense, after he assumed responsibility for the Federal Civil Defense pro-
gram last August, was to give the American people the facts they need to
know about the dangers of a thermonuclear attack and what they can do to
protect themselves. A booklet entitled "Fallout Protection - What to Know
and Do about Nuclear Attack", published by the Department of Defense,
Office of Civil Defense, attempts to provide these facts. This bulletin has
been made available free of charge, and may be obtained from your local
Civil Defense Director, or at the local post office.
The foreign and defense policies of our Federal Government make a nuclear attack highly un-
likely. However, should a nuclear attack ever occur, certain preparations would mean the dif-
ference between life and death.
Because Massachusetts is situated in the northeastern portion of the Country and vulnerable to
manned aircraft attack from beyond the Arctic Circle, from submarines, and from intercontinental
missiles, it may be assumed that warning time will be even shorter than for other parts of the Country.
Also, in Massachusetts there exists a heavy concentration of critical industries, populations, and
many research centers making the State a desirable target.
Shelter, warning, radiological monitoring, training and education are all parts of a total
community civil defense program. The responsibility for integrating these parts, and relating the
whole to the needs and capabilities of the community, necessarily falls on the State and local
Civil Defense organizations.
Lunenburg has taken its first step in launching the local Civil Defense Program. Under a pro-
gram known as the National Shelter Survey, all public buildings in our Town have been surveyed.
All shelter spaces, which would accommodate 50 or more people, and which would be open to the
public in an emergency, will be marked and stocked with essential food, water, first aid kits and
radiation detection instruments.
Just as quickly as new planning assumptions covering warning time, presumed target areas, and
other emergency attack information, are received from the Department of Defense, this information
will be passed on to the Townspeople, thus fulfilling the second step in our local program.
Preliminary plans are being formulated for carrying out the third and last step, which is
extensive training and education programs. Every citizen needs to know how he will be warned of
imminent attack, where he will take shelter, how he should behave in the shelter, and whathe
should expect when he emerges from it. In addition, as many citizens as possible should be train-
ed in the techniques of shelter management, radiological monitoring, decontamination, rescue,
fire fighting, and restoring essential service.

WELFARE
Gladys M. Goodale, Director
Our local agency administers five programs of assistance: Old Age,
Disability, Aid to Dependent Children, and the new program, Medical
Aid for the Aged. All are Federal-State programs of financial aid for
needy persons. The Town receives reimbursements for these four programs
from the Federal and State Governments according to an established formula. We also administer
the General Relief program, which is temporary aid for persons who do not meet the eligibility re-
quirements of the other categories of assistance. This is also the program most affected by economic
and social conditions of the community.
Each program has eligibility requirements with regard to age, residence, and the financial
need of the recipient. The amount of assistance is determined in accordance with State standards
and Public Welfare laws. We would like to point out that it is not necessary to be destitute to be
eligible for public assistance. Although income and resources are a foremost consideration in de-
termining eligibility for Old Age and Disability Assistance, applicants may have cash on hand or
bank savings up to, but not exceeding $500 and are not expected to relinquish any life or health
insurance. Property provisions differ in the various assistance programs, as does the responsibility
of relatives for the support of their needy kin.
A misconception regarding the program of Old Age Assistance is the matter of a lien against
real estate. Contrary to popular belief, we do not "take over" the property, but merely protect
and insure the Town's interest in it. Under the law, a city or town granting assistance to a person
owning real estate, must place on record in the appropriate registry of deeds or land court an in-
strument creating a lien upon such real estate for the amount of assistance paid by it. Upon the
death of the recipient, it is the duty of the Welfare Department to enforce the lien. However,
action may be taken only with the written approval of the State Department of Public Welfare.
This is usually given unless the Department is of the opinion that enforcement will cause undo hard-
ship. Generally, the first $1,500 received is exempt from recovery by the Town. In no event will
the lien be enforced so long as the surviving spouse of the recipient continues to reside in the real
estate in question.
Medical Aid for the Aged, a program which came into being in October of 1960, pro-
vides medical care for persons sixty-five years of age or over, whose income and resources are
insufficient to meet the costs of necessary medical services. Such medical care may be provided
in the person's own home or in recognized medical facilities. During 1961, seventeen persons re-
ceived medical assistance under this new program. Most of these persons received monthly Old
Age and Survivors Insurance checks but the amount received was not enough to cover all of their
medical needs.
Public Assistance programs are set up to help people to help themselves. They are encouraged
- 55 -
===PAGE 58===
to make full use of their own capacities and resources, thereby strengthening their self-respect and
self-dependence. There is a popular misconception that the Welfare Agency assumes the responsi-
bility of managing the personal affairs of persons receiving assistance. Nothing could be further
from the truth. In most cases, the recipient receives assistance for necessities in the form of a cash
grant; however, he can, and is expected to, work out his own solutions within the limitation of the
program.
Most people who come to a public agency, do so in times of stress. Whether or not the family
is able to assist financially, they do have a moral responsibility toward their members. Children
have a responsibility to give moral support and sympathetic understanding to theiraging parents and
should make an active effort to assist in solving the many problems with which the parents are con-
fronted. Unfortunately, many families prefer to shift both the financial and the moral responsibili-
ty onto the public agency. Though the agency is ever ready and willing to lend a helping hand,
in times of stress it is the family who should be the main source of strength.

The wave of urbanization which has all but engulfed many previously
rural communities is steadily encroaching on Lunenburg. The resulting
transformation of the Town from an argricultural to a residential area has
brought with it not only the obvious need of serving more people with
existing town facilities, but also the need for providing wholly new services not required fora
rural population. Although the individual Town Departments are doing their best to keep abreast
of these needs, paying for new and expanded facilities imposes a real burden on the taxpayer, who
must bear the brunt of their cost.
A clearer vision of the Lunenburg of the future, composed of residences, businesses, industries
and areas reserved for recreation and aesthetic values and for the preservation of water supply and
public health, has resulted from the Master Plan studies of the Town. Detailed land capacity
studies indicate that the total number of dwelling units at ultimate development of the Town,
under our present zoning requirements, would approach 8000. This would mean a probable ultimate
population of about 28,500 persons. It has been estimated that without zoning the ultimate popu-
lation could have reached 60,000.
A major concern of the Planning Board is to minimize the impact of residential growth on the
rural character of the Town. Those persons who are well satisfied to live in the typical urban type
developments found throughout the Town still enjoy looking at, driving through and being surrounded
by, the open land owned by others. Since this land may not always be open, the Town must con-
sider ways and means for preserving some open spaces for future generations to enjoy. This will
mean acquisition of land where necessary and appropriate. Through close cooperation between the
Planning Board, the Town Forest Committee and the Conservation Committee, a conservation site
plan for Lunenburg has been prepared. Action will be initiated to acquire one of these sites at the
coming Annual Meeting.
Another area of concern to the Planning Board is the acquisition of land as a site for a future
sewage disposal plant. The study plan made about ten years ago has been reviewed and preliminary
steps have been taken to obtain approval of the site by the Massachusetts Department of Public
Health. In the near future, this problem will also be presented to the Town Meeting for action.

completed during the year. A public hearing was held and these regulations are now in effect.

were approved at town meetings, were held. Much time was spent on the Master Plan Studies

PARKS
Clarence E. May, Jr., Chairman
Raymond J. Okerman
George M. Walker
The erection of a new backstop at Marshall Park at a cost of $600 was
another step toward improving the area. In 1962 the Park Commissioners are
requesting an appropriation for a chain link fence to be placed along the front
of Marshall Park to further improve its appearance. Although thousands of
dollars could easily be spent on projects pertaining to the Parks, the work will
continue, as before, on a year to year basis, and the costs kept at a minimum.
Maintenance work at all our fields and parks is becoming more of a problem each year, due to
increased use of the facilities. Picnic areas will be developed at Marshall Park by doing extensive
brush cutting. A project that must be undertaken in another year is the dredging and cleaning of
Marshall Pond. Destruction of and damage to park equipment continues to be a serious problem.
The Park Department warns that offenders will be dealt with severely.
A well-rounded summer program, under the direction of Emory Merrill, was carried out this year.
The playgrounds were active, with planned programs, games, etc. scheduled and supervised by compe-
tent personnel. The 1961 program included well-attended arts and crafts classes; diving instruction at
Aqualand; baseball, softball and basketball for both boys and girls; tennis instruction; and a men's soft-
ball and horseshoe league. A trip was made to Benson's Animal Farm and an outing held at Willard Brook.
Although the running track was leased by the Fitchburg State College on a rental basis, this
arrangement was not considered satisfactory as it conflicted with the Babe Ruth and Lunenburg High
School baseball and field events.
Again this year, we extend our sincere thanks to Mr. Irving Kimball for his tireless efforts in
keeping the trees in the public parks properly trimmed. The Park Department lost a faithful and con-
scientious employee when Mr. Earle W. Lancey was forced to retire due to illness. We sincerely
hope that Mr. Lancey's health will continue to improve.
The contract negotiated during 1961 with the new owner of Aqualand has not proven satisfactory.
Therefore, an article has been inserted in the Warrant for the 1962 Annual Meeting proposing several
changes. It is hoped that sufficient facts and figures on which the voters can base their decisions
will be available at the meeting.

The increase in additional rooms required for elementary use reflects both the growth of the
elementary enrollment and displacement of elementary classes now in the Junior-Senior High School
by Junior High classes. The twelve room addition to the Junior-Senior High School was originally
intended to serve our needs for Elementary and Junior-Senior High Schools through the school year
1963-64. Our latest projections indicate that the space available now will be adequate until
1965-66.
The 1961 School Housing Study Committee was instructed at the 1961 Town Meeting to study
the problem of new administrative offices for the School Department and to make recommendations
at the 1962 Annual Town Meeting. Space for these administrative offices is available by rearrange-
ments in the Brooks House and the Study Committee recommends that these rearrangements be made.
The Study Committee recommended at the 1961 Town Meeting that the Center and West Schools
be abandoned for school purposes. It was voted that these buildings be transferred to the custody
of the Selectmen.
School Housing is a problem that requires careful study on a continuing basis. We therefore
recommend that a School Housing Study Committee for 1962 be appointed and charged with the re-
sponsibility of making recommendations no later than the 1963 Annual Town Meeting.

HOME DEPARTMENT
This year, leader training meetings were stressed with hope that more people could be reached. During the
month of January, Chair Caning was taught to a group of local women under the leadership of Mrs. George Gordon
and Mrs. Egide Mathieu. In early February, Miss Judith Braskie of the Extension Service lectured on the topic,
What the Labels Say, a subject which deals with various fibres and caring for them.
In March the latest techniques of Modern Vegetable Cookery were demonstrated to a group of local homemakers
at Gurley Hall in Whalom. Also, during the month of March a leader training program on the Basic Tailoring of
Children's Coats was held at the Parish House with Miss Braskie teaching Mrs. Howard Hamel and Mrs. George
Thomas to be leaders and this project will be available in the future for anyone desiring to take part. Officers
Day and Recognition Luncheon was held April 11 at Horticultural Hall in Worcester with the officers attending.

program entitled Family Life was conducted by the Extension Service.

Juveni Furniture in Winchendon; Bent Brothers Quality Furniture in Gardner; Heberts Candy Factory in Shrewsbury;
Worcester Electric Company; Wrights Factory in Warren; and Cutler Glass in Southbridge. In June a cake salewas

which the events for the coming year were discussed and outlined. A two-day course in Creative Stichery was also

paper materials and color schemes. October's subject, Planning Your Will and Settlement of Estates, was held at
Gurley Hall in Whalom with Deborah Caspole speaking to a large group of men and women as to why awill should be
planned. A program on Christmas Decorations was demonstrated by Mrs. Edward Stillman in November. Once more
the Annual Christmas Program for the entire county was held in Worcester at the Horticultural Hall and was well
attended by the Lunenburg Group. Mrs. Robert Berndt and Mrs. Russell Coburn prepared the Christmas centerpiece,
consisting of a bell with angels. Again, Lunenburg won first honorable mention. Much credit goes to both girls
who put much effort into this beautiful arrangement.
All Extension Meetings are held the second Wednesday of every month and are open to the public. You are
urged to take advantage of opportunities offered on all projects. Approximately twenty members belong to the group.
For information, please contact any member or watch the local newspaper. Projects remaining on program for 1962
are Making a Slipcover, Fish, Poultry and Eggs in Family Meals, Your Federal Income Tax, Role of Modern Woman,

Report of Superintendent of Schools
for the Year 1961
To the Lunenburg School Committee and Citizens:
I herewith submit my Twelfth Annual Report (Eighth since
withdrawal from School Union, Ashby-Lunenburg-Townsend) as
Superintendent of Schools,
"UNIVERSAL EDUCATION--or, more precisely, equality of edu-
cational opportunity--is a basic tenet of our democracy. The
concept appears neither in the Constitution nor in its amend-
ments, but it is implicit in the promises of America." Today, universal education
means the chance for everyone to get the kind and amount of education, including
college and graduate study, that his capacities warrant. Well-educated people are
a fundamental necessity for a successful democratic government.
The Lunenburg School Committee has spent many hours the past year searching in-
to the details of curriculum, merit salary schedules, policies, and custodial evalu-
ations as well as routine matters and budgetary allotments and expenditures.
The superintendent is grateful for all the assistance and considerate interest
in these times of need. One of our many problems was the selection of new adminis-
trative positions following the resignation of former Principal Harold R. Alley who
had come to us with the new Junior-Senior High School and who did much to start our
secondary educational program in the right direction. This fine start will be con-
tinued by Mr. Richard J. Carey, Principal, and Mr. Floyd F. Barney, Assistant Prin-
cipal. Already they have given evidence that their appointments were merited. Over
the past ten years we have been very pleased with the recognition that has come to
our school system through the efforts and worthiness of our personnel and of our
pupils whether as individuals or in group performances or participation.
HIGHER SALARIES -
Teachers' salaries continue to increase for several reasons, partly because of
monetary inflation, partly because of increased appreciation for better teaching.
While the Lunenburg salary schedule is not more than competitive it has enabled us
to compete for the better teachers. However, there are teacher shortages in some
areas which makes it difficult to always secure the teacher you would like. Fortu-
nately our teacher-trainee policy has enabled us to pick out some of the more out-
standing candidates.
Most of the BUDGET increases come through salaries--increases in salaries and
by additions to the staff. For the past several years, four or more teachers have
been necessarily added each year because of increased pupil population. May I again
point out that considerable reimbursement is annually received. Two of the larger
reimbursements this year under the General Laws are Chapter 70 (General School Fund)
$67,093.69; Chapter 71 (Transportation) $65,760.41 For other reimbursements or
miscellaneous receipts refer to the school financial report.
MERIT -
The Lunenburg School Committee, the school administrators, the Lunenburg
Teachers' Association and a laymans' committee, have spent a great deal of time in
study of the relative merits of a "better teacher" salary reward system. Over the
past several years some merit compensations have been built into the local salary
schedule. Others are under study.

"What parent, what school board, can measure the value of that teacher who has
inspired a student to delve into the cool, quiet beauty of a quadratic equation?
...
We cannot pay our teachers in terms of the number of scientists, mathematicians,
poets or authors they produce because it is not possible to tell precisely which
teacher inspired them." Good teaching must be slow enough SO that it is not con-
fusing, and fast enough so that it is not boring. It is a matter of timing as of
form or content; and masters of timing are rare in any art.
Several facts seem important. FIRST, teachers should be willing to try merit.
SECOND, they should have a large part in formulating merit policy. THIRD, the bulk
of the evaluation must rest in the hands of the Principals, supplemented by informa-
tion from Department Heads. FOURTH, merit will always cost more money. FIFTH, gen-
erally a school system must have a good salary policy before it tries merit. SIXTH,
merit can work if there is a willingness to make it work, but where there is sus-
picion and distrust, it can break down morale as nothing else under heaven. SEVENTH,
merit takes time--time to establish criteria and time in evaluating. EIGHTH, morale
is more important than merit. The teacher, more than any other factor, determines
the quality of education. There is no substitute for a person of high integrity,
sensitive to children and professionally trained, to perform the teaching function.
CURRICULUM LAYMANS' STUDY COMMITTEE -
A little over a year ago a laymans' study committee was appointed by the school
committee to assist in evaluating our school curriculum. Constant study and atten-
tion are being given to curriculum and program of studies. Content of courses is
constantly being analyzed, revised or implemented. The Junior-Senior High School
have recently completed two years of self-evaluation and have passed very satisfac-
torily an evaluation by a committee from the "New England Board of Colleges and
Secondary Schools". Several times a year members of our high school staff have
been encouraged to participate on such evaluating teams and Principal Thomas C.
Passios has participated on a similar project in evaluating an elementary school.
It is not surprising, therefore, that the laymans' committee have reported very
favorably to the school committee on our school curriculum, In comparison with
other systems, there has been little to criticize although a few well founded thoughts
for betterments are at intervals advised for consideration and action.
We have been concerned likewise with some of the new techniques for teaching
which are being studied throughout the country and have done some experimentation
with several, such as 21" classroom (Channel 2 Educational TV School Enrichment
Programs), some forms of teaching machines, conversational French classes in the
elementary grades, and formal team teaching.
TEAM TEACHING - -
"Team teaching" so-called, has been widely advertised the past year. It is
probably humanity's oldest way of organizing instruction. The world's first team
teachers were mankind's first families and the family unit made use of such "resource
visitors" as Uncle Harry and Cousin Sue. Team-teaching drastically affects the life
of a teacher. When he performs on a team, his performance is suddenly open to scru-
tiny and criticism. He is obliged to plan and evaluate lessons with others. Two or
more teachers are concerned with the fate of a significant part of the instruction
of a class over a given period of time. Very often, teaching teams have clusters of
rooms assigned to them using individual differences and talents of the groups to the
best advantage. Lunenburg has been doing much of this concept for years. Ten years
ago we were doing this with music, science, physical education, dramatics, etc.

Report of Lunenburg Elementary Schools
for the Year 1961
A community usually gets in education what it aspires
to. The attitude of the citizenry toward education and the
extent to which it enthusiastically, but prudently supports it,
determines the educational tone and action in any community.
Lunenburg's students are fortunate in that both of the above are
reflected in a positive sense in this community. School admin-
istration and teachers cannot help but reflect this same
positive attitude of "the best education possible" for our chil-
dren. As a result, we strive continually for excellence.
Excellence is not something a school suddenly reaches. In our school, we
are constantly aiming for that one step beyond in any program that will improve it
for our students. The pursuit of excellence is a continuing process -- not one of
static goals. We are interested in goals but moreso,we are interested in continuing
goals and definite actions toward the attainment of excellence and these goals.
The public press and general literature is replete with much criticism of
the ills of education. Criticism must be evaluated as to its source and authority.
Unfounded criticism, not based on fact, has no place. Educators generally value
constructive criticism. In fact, teachers and educational administrators are their
own best critics. In Lunenburg, the attitude we have towards continuing goals
creates a condition of continual self evaluation or self criticism and actions for
improvement in those areas of our school organization or programs which we find need
reinforcement. We do not fear a condition developing for we have an attitude of
doing something about it - taking definite actions to improve that condition.
Much is going on around us in education. With all the new approaches,
organization - this teaching - that teaching - this machine - that control- we could
just jump on the "proverbial bandwagon", become fashionable and be accepted. I do
not mean to underrate departures and experimental programs - far from this. We are
doing some ourselves. Experimentation and probing into new programs of learning are
most important. You will find in the body of this report some of these ventures
into new techniques of organization and learning that we in Lunenburg are implement-
ing - some of them most successfully. What I am trying to say here is that because
this "something new" is being done in this or that town is not necessarily a reason
why we have to "jump on the bandwagon". The "bandwagon" may not be "attuned" to
Lunenburg. Our continuing concern is that what we do is improving programs already
in force here, or how efficiently we act when we know that conditions are right to
begin new programs, or revise existing programs. Change occurs as a result of
necessity - not for the sake of change itself.
- 104 -
===PAGE 107===
To quote from last year's report -- "A school must always accept the best
of the past and keep it. But it must more vigorously improve - (Zip - Our school
must maintain constant surveillance over our curriculum and teaching practices as
well as our organizational and administrative theories and practices, to the extent
that we provide the best education within our power for the young people of our
community." Good education is earned - it is not suddenly reached. I belive our
schools follow the concept paraphrased in a statement of Oliver Wendell Holmes EN the
great concern is "not so much where we stand as in what direction we are going."
This positive attitude is Lunenburg's attitude to education.
ACTIONS FOR EXCELLENCE
During this past year we have taken some very definite actions for
excellence. When we have seen possibilities for improvement, our faculty admits it
and takes actions for improving a situation or upgrading a weaker area. A few of the
more important actions taken or completed during the past year will be outlined.
The curriculum study in social studies in the lower grades, and geography
and history in the upper grades, was completed by the faculty. As a result, a
revised curriculum guide was prepared which by the school year 1962-63 will be fully
implemented. It might be pointed out that this study was done over a period of two
years in after school study sessions and with additional outside research on the
part of the faculty members engaged in the study.
The reading program continues to be strengthened. The tone of the reading
personnel is set in a recent report of the reading supervisor, when she states
"There is no room for complacency in the field of the teaching of reading. We must
aim high if we expect to improve and move forward."
Some actions taken in the various programs of study have been: (1) We have
added a reading teacher which made it possible to improve the remedial reading
program and add a speed-reading program. (2) We are looking into and using
specially constructed phonics programs. Some rooms in Grade One are using certain
prepared phonics programs in addition to our regularly outlined program. Though our
regular program has been accomplishing the teaching of phonics satisfactorily, we
are presently carrying on these additional programs for three consecutive years, at
the end of which period an evaluation will be made to determine whether the addi-
tional program has sufficient value to retain it in the reading program. (3) SRA
Reading Laboratories, an individualized program designed to develop and strengthen
reading comprehension, phonetics, word analysis skills, and listening skills, has
been incorporated into our program in Grades 4-6 and is being tried out for possible
permanent use in Grade Three. (4) In addition, our own reading skills kits are being
prepared by our reading personnel for Grade Three and in future years for other
grades. These latter kits will contain readily available exercises to be used by
individual pupils in skills in which they need assistance, at, above, or below grade
level. (5) In Arithmetic we have begun a two-track program beginning at the first
and second grade level. This procedure allows those pupils who can to proceed at a
more rapid rate. At the same time, extension of skills already learned as well as
additional enrichment mathematics is being introduced in the upper grades inaugurat-
ing an accelerated program in arithmetic for those students who can proceed at a
more rapid rate. It is hoped that additional features and supplementary materials
will improve this program. (6) The English skills program has been completely out-
lined for Grades One, Two and Three, defining more clearly the teaching responsibili-
ties for each grade in this area. This was a step taken over a year ago when we
noted a weakening in some of the English skills areas in the primary grades in test
results.

through the materials and equipment secured under the National Defense Education
Act. Audio visual, illustrative, laboratory type equipment, up-to-date maps and
globes, manipulative arithmetic devices - have all helped to strengthen teaching
techniques in these important areas of study.

THE SCHOOL BOARD : Citizens in a class by themselves
These are exceptional men and women. The presence of their name on a ballot in-
dicates their interest in civic affairs, They seek an office that means a great deal
of work for no compensation. Their only reward: hopefully, a better education for
every American child.
Each has his own day to day activities to attend to. Yet somehow, every member
manages to find the EXTRA HOURS--for planning a new school, balancing the year's bud-
get or proposing a much-needed bond issue,
As members of the business community we can support these dedicated people in
their work. We can muster our influence and experience behind better school buildings.
We can study and support needed bond issues so that teachers and students will have
the facilities to reach their full teaching and learning potential. We can sponsor
scholarships, offer research grants, and, in general, keep abreast of the problems of
education.
As private citizens we can elect those best qualified to serve as board members.
And once elected, we can support their best efforts,
Your continuing interest in your local school board is appreciated by its mem-
bers. Honest criticism is welcome, help and assistance gratefully accepted. You'l be
as proud of your contribution as the board members are of theirs.

Report of the School Nurse
for the Year 1961
Early in the year a survey was made to determine the number of children who had
not yet had their fourth polio shots. It was found that a large number still remained
who had not received this protection. Arrangements were made with Dr. E. H. Smith,
Medical Director of the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health, to conduct a clinic.
Four hundred twenty-eight (428) children had their fourth shot at this clinic and
twenty-eight (28) children received their first, second, and third shots.
Booster shots for diphtheria and tetanus was offered to pupils in grades six
and eleven for the first time this year. One hundred ninety-three (193) children
received these boosters and five (5) children who had not previously been immunized
had the series of three shots at monthly intervals until completed.
The physical examinations are being conducted annually by Dr. John DeLeo, School
Physician. Students in Grades one, four, seven, and ten are the grades involved.
Students who participate in active sports are examined annually also. The entering
first graders usually have their physical examination during the spring and summer
before entering school, Fifty-two (52) children were examined by Dr. DeLeo at two
special clinics held in May. Dr. E. H. Smith assisted at the clinics by doing the
immunizations. Thirty-one (31) children had smallpox vaccinations, twenty-nine (29)
had diphtheria-tetanus boosters, and twenty-nine (29) had polio boosters. The rest
of the entering group of first graders were taken to their own family physicians for
this very important examination.
The Mantoux Test for Tuberculosis is done annually in Grade I and every third
year to grades 7 and 11. Two hundred ninety-four (294) students were tested; of
this number, five (5) had a positive reaction and were x-rayed, together with members
of their families. Fortunately, the x-ray films were negative in all cases,
Vision and hearing tests are given all children annually. Very good cooperation
is received from parents regarding correction of defects noted during these tests.
In several cases the nurses have helped to secure financial aid in order to obtain
glasses.
A Future Nurses Club was organized this year in the Senior High School. Mrs.
Waino Lahti, Assistant School Nurse, is the sponsor of this club which has thirty
(30) members, All girls interested in nursing or any other branch of medicine are
eligible to belong. The group visited the local hospitals and outside speakers and
films relating to nursing have been enjoyed at the meetings.
In closing, I wish to express my appreciation for the cooperation and support
given us by the Superintendent of Schools, the Principals, the Teaching Staff, the
Volunteers who have assisted us in our various clinics, and all the School Personnel.
Respectfully submitted,
SIGNE E. SCHULTZ, R. N.
School Nurse

Report of the School Lunch Program
for the Year 1961
Dear Mr. Billings:
This is my Sixth Annual Report of the Lunenburg School Lunch Program, and is for
the school year, September 1960 to September 1961.
During this period, we fed 139,954 Type "A" lunches to students and 10,304
lunches to adults for a period of 171 lunch days, which is an average of 878 lunches
a day in both schools combined. Beyond these figures, we sold to students, 145,418
half-pints of milk under the special milk program, as well as 3,958 half-pints to
adults. That is a total of 299,634 half-pints of milk purchased in all schools
during the year.
Financially this past year we ended with a deficit of $271.71. The program
absorbed an increase in salaries to personnel of approximately $2500,00. It is very
difficult to absorb more labor costs from the program yet the cafeteria workers are
as deserving of raises as other departments. They are a part of the schools' program.
Commodities bought and dispersed by the United States Department of Agriculture
were fairly abundant last year--receiving flour, rice, corn meal, butter, cheeses (a
few months), dried eggs, dried milk powder, pea beans, hamburg, peanut butter, apri-
cots, cherries, turkeys, dried date pieces, green beans, tomatoes, peaches, canned
corn, applesauce, canned beef and gravy, ripe olives, grapefruit sections, lard and
chickens. All these commodities were not available every month--some only one month
--and were limited in quantity, according to the number fed. The total valuation in
our program was $9,698.61.
Participation percentage-wise for the year in the Junior-Senior High School was
77% according to attendance and 73% according to membership. In the Elementary
School, participation according to attendance was 60% and 57% according to membership.
Anything over 50% is considered good in the State and we are over the State average
as a whole.
The personnel in the Junior-Senior High School consisted of Mrs. Dorothy Maki
as the Head-Cook and Mrs. Myrtle Eastman as the Baker. The general-workers were
Mrs. Myrtle Dickey, Mrs. Edna Huff, Mrs. Ethel Malone, Mrs. Fannie Ovaska and Mrs.
Emelian Perry. In the fall (1961) Mrs. Eastman and Mrs. Malone resigned for personal
reasons and were replaced by Mrs. Perry as the Baker and Mrs. Ovaska, New workers
were Mrs. Edith Burger and Mrs. Mary Thibault. I found it very necessary to add
another worker at the Junior-Senior High School this fall, with the extra load, larger
kitchen area and more equipment to care for. This worker is Mrs. Ivy Carabba.
The personnel in the Elementary School consists of Mrs. Phyllis Prouty as the
Manager; Mrs. Hazel Whitney, the Head-Cook; Mrs. Helen Hebert as Assistant-Cook; and
Mrs. Lucy Chartrand, the Baker. The general workers are Mrs. Bertha Geigner, Mrs.
Elizabeth Gilchrest, and Mrs. Rita Seguin.
The success of a program depends SO much on these people--and we can not carry
on without them.

lunches. The Elementary Annex pupils were transported to the Junior-Senior High
School to enjoy meals with us. At Christmas, we served 1,408 lunches, The tables

The Spring annual workshops were held in Boston and South Hadley during our
April vacation. I attended for the three-day period at Boston and received a cer-
tificate. Most of the personnel from Lunenburg attended one day at one or the other
of the Workshops.
The Annual Summer Workshop usually held at Fitchburg State College and a few
times at our Lunenburg Schools, was changed to the University of Massachusetts. This
was for Supervisors and Managers or anyone who kept records this year. Mrs. Prouty
and I attended for the week and received a certificate from the University. Our lec-
tures and courses were given by the Staff of the University or by representatives of
large feeding operations.
On Friday, November 3, 1961, the convention of Worcester County School Lunch
personnel was held at Westboro High School in conjunction with the Teachers' Conven-
tion. Sixteen workers and substitutes attended from Lunenburg School Lunch,
The Annual Convention of the Massachusetts School Food Service Association was
held September 22 and 23, 1961 at Chatham Bars Inn at Chatham on the Cape. I attended
this Convention and was installed as Secretary of the organization for two years.
The Massachusetts State Officers were invited and attended a New England Regional
meeting of all New England State Officers at Portsmouth, New Hampshire, October 6
and 7, 1961 in conjuction with the New Hampshire State Convention.
With the transfer of the sixth grade to the new wing of the Junior-Senior High
building we have an extra lunch period this year--making four in all. These young-
sters are very delightful and a few operate the cash registers for their grade and
handle the change box.
I am presently attending a course on "Emergency Feeding" in case of a disaster
--under Civil Defense personnel out of Natick.
The enlargement of the Junior-Senior High School kitchen by moving the dish
washer across into the new addition and the extra and larger equipment was very much
appreciated and helps US in our daily tasks.
In closing, I wish to express my thanks to you, Mr. Billings, and to the School
Committee; also the Principals of the schools, Mr. Carey and Mr. Barney, Mr. Passios
and Mr. Downs; to the town personnel and boards; the faculties; the custodians and
to my own personnel, who are assuming so much responsibility.
Respectfully submitted,
RUTH A. CORRELL, Supervisor
