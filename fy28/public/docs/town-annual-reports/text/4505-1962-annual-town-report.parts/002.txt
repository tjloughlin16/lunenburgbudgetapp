QUESTION NO. I
PROPOSED AMENDMENT TO THE CONSTITUTION
Do you approve of the adoption of an amendment to the constitution summarized below which was approved by the General
Court in a joint session of the two branches held May 13, 1959, received 143 votes in the affirmative and 118 in the negative,
and in a joint session of the two branches held March 29, 1961, received 144 votes in the affirmative and 121 in the negative?
SUMMARY: The proposed amendment imposes no new taxes but gives the General Court the power to pass an income tax at
graduated or proportioned rates and is a change in the constitutional limitations now in effect on the power of the General Court
to pass tax measures. The proposed amendment would add a new Article to the Constitution of the Commonwealth by which full
power and authority is granted to the General Court, in the alternative to the power and authority to tax incomes in the manner
provided in Article XLIV of the Amendments to the Constitution, to impose a tax on incomes at rates which are proportioned or
graduated according to the amount of income received, irrespective of the source from which it may be derived, and to grant
reasonable exemptions, deductions and abatements. It further provides that any property the income of which is taxed under the
provisions of the proposed Article may be exempted from the imposition and levying of proportional and reasonable assessments,
rates and taxes as at present authorized by the Constitution, and that the Article shall not be construed to limit the power of the
General Court to impose and levy reasonable duties and excises.

Superintendent's Annual Report
A few years ago I came across a statement in a bank window which read something
like this, "Good luck is the lazy man's definition of a worker's success", I have
often reflected on the genuine truth there is in such a statement. Its truth applies
to our school system, for the success it now enjoys has come about through the com-
bined, dedicated, and often frustrating, efforts of many sincere and conscientious
people; Teachers, Secretaries, Custodians, Lunch personnel, Administrators, School
Committee, Finance Committee and the Citizens of the Town.
We live in a time when probably the greatest asset remaining to America is the
potential which lies in its youth. Without question, our future greatness, yes, even
our survival as a great nation, depends on them. On US lies the burden and respon-
sibility to think and see big enough that Lunenburg children receive the advantages
of education which are second to none. To continue to fulfill this obligation is a
real challenge to the various interested peoplesin our community.
The operation of the Lunenburg Public Schools has become big business when we
consider the fact that about 4/5 of a million dollars will be spent this year in
support of schools, With this fact in mind, the School Committee moved in the
direction of setting up a new Budget format which makes use of a new system of
Accounting. This was a rather important decision to make since it involved indoc-
trinating all personnel associated with the School System, not only with a new Super-
intendent, but also with a new Budget format. This step was taken in anticipation
of action by the Massachusetts Department of Education in requiring this system of
Accounting for all school systems in Massachusetts. This Accounting system is
currently used and recommended throughout most of the nation. Massachusetts is
nearly alone in not using a similar standard system. The financial accounting
manual used in the changeover was developed through the efforts of five nationwide
educational organizations and the United States Office of Education over a two-year
period. With the installation of IBM machines at the State level, all school
systems within the Commonwealth will be required to change to this commonly used
system of Accounting within two years. Lunenburg has chosen to make this step
before being required to do SO. The major advantages for such a move are threefold.
(1) It establishes a sound basis of Cost Accounting; (2) It should improve the
accuracy of local, State and National summaries of expenditures; and (3) It should
facilitate comparisons of financial information among communities and among states.
So that we may have some basic understanding of this system, we are providing a
simple breakdown with thumbnail descriptions of each category. In future budgets,
we will try to relate each year's request to these accounts in our report so that
citizens may be able to see where increases or decreases in the Appropriation
Request are reflected. It has been especially gratifying to me to see the compe-
tent way that Teachers, Department Heads, Clerical personnel and Principals have
adjusted to this new system. The cooperation of the Finance Committee in under-
standing and approving this format has been especially gratifying. Such understand-
ing cooperation has made this transition effective, successful and relatively smooth.

- SECONDARY PROGRAM OF STUDIES -
American educational practices, since the advent of Sputnik in 1957, have been
the target for much criticism. It has become almost popular to berate what we are
doing in our schools. This alertness on the part of interested citizens has been
good for education since it has kept educators continually alert to the need for
constant self analysis SO that our educational program might be truly meaningful and
effective. With this in mind, self analysis, evaluation of the Program of Studies
at the Junior-Senior High School was initiated about two years ago which culminated
in the adoption of a new Program of Studies at the secondary level which became
effective in time for the 1962-63 academic year. More than a year of study was re-
quired in its development involving the combined efforts of teachers working with
their respective Department Heads and supplemented by weekly meetings of the Admin-
istrative Staff, Department Heads, and Supervisors to coordinate the program being
made. This new program was an effort to upgrade and strengthen the school's offer-
ings by incorporating new courses in some instances while deleting others in the
light of recently developed needs, demands and expectations of high school graduates.
After presentation to the school committee for their study and after several joint
meetings, the new Secondary Program of Studies was officially approved in April of
1962.
This Program required that all High School students must take five(5) required
major subjects rather than the traditional four (4). With this increased require-
ment, the number of earned credits to graduate was stepped up to eighteen (18)
rather than the traditional sixteen (16). It has been interesting to note that a
considerable number of students have through their own initiative and through the
advice of the Guidance personnel taken six (6) major subjects this year with
apparent success, With the increase in the number of required subjects, additional
courses had to be provided. Earth Science, a second year of Biology and Botany
combined, Conversational French for the non-college bound student, more advanced
languages, and specialized English and History courses were added. To make such
additional courses available brings with it the ever increasing cost of education.
New offerings required additional textbooks, increased laboratory materials and all
the equipment that make the advances meaningful. To accomodate the increased re-
quired courses the Activity period during the day had to be eliminated. This was a
time when special interests in Music, Art, Dramatics, Publications and other co-
curricular subjects functioned. With the addition of this time to the subject
areas, the academic day has in effect been lengthened to about six (6) hours allow-
ing twenty-five minutes for lunch. Indeed, the Academic time has increased by
moving the co-curricular interests to the end of the day which has created problems
for these areas of interest. In a community such as Lunenburg where a large major-
ity of students are "bussed" many transportation problems are encountered as the
result of a wide scope of after-school activities. Possibly, we are saying that
some sort of transportation should be provided for those participating in after
school activities although this opens up a whole new area of expense. This is a
matter which needs honest reflection and a hoped-for solution.

strengths, weaknesses and interests of the individual students. Using such know-
ledge gained through objective testing and the skill of the teacher's and guidance
counselor's observations we can now do much to help students fulfill their purpose

in life. This we endeavor sincerely to accomplish in the Lunenburg Schools. For

- BEYOND THE HIGH SCHOOL -
The 1962 graduating class of the Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School finds a-
bout 57% of the students seeking higher education in four year colleges, Junior
colleges, Business and Technical schools. The remaining students are gainfully em-
ployed or are members of the armed services.
The great day!
Good placement opportunities for students not pursuing a college or commercial
program are understandably difficult. Although teachers, guidance personnel and
principals are constantly encouraging every student to make the most of his own
abilities, there are still those who pursue a course of study simply because it is
less strenuous. We urge parents to consult with the principal and guidance personnel
about the curricula their sons or daughters are pursuing. Remember, the one thing
we cannot buy back is "time". For those whose interests are vocational or agricul-
tural let's be certain that every opportunity is pursued.
Careful planning and thinking on the part of parents is vitally necessary to
aid the student's acceptance for college training. It is not too soon to begin
making college plans by the eighth grade. This should include conferences with
teachers and guidance counselors to determine the student's scholastic aptitudes
and achievement.
Visits to college campuses should be initiated by at least the tenth grade,
as college costs are rising, with a prediction from the Federal Office of Education
that they double by 1970. If the economy continues to spiral upward, parents are
urged to plan several years before college enrollment for means of meeting college
expenses. Most parents will find it helpful to consider the various financial aids
available. These are: (1) Working your way; (2) Scholarships; (3) Grants-in-aid;
(4) Loans (Federal, State and Private); (5) Deferred tuition payments; and (6) In-
surance plans. Detailed information about these assistance plans is available from
your guidance services. Parents should encourage their sons and daughters to make
inquiry about these plans and schedule an interview with a counselor for the
parents' information.
- ADULT EDUCATION PROGRAM -
On September 30, 1957, the community voted an enabling act to establish and
- 98 -
===PAGE 101===
maintain State-aided Vocational Education. Quoting from the Town Report of that
year: "This vote indicates the result of a growing community and, the desires of
its citizens for programs to expand their knowledges in useful, beneficial and
cultural directions." This program started February 27, 1958 with three classes,
and opened again on October 9, 1958 with five classes: two in Basic Clothing, one
each in Upholstery, Rug Bralding, and Decorated Ware.
In the interim period the program has had its difficulties with money not
being appropriated to continue the program. Currently, however, the program is
being carried on with five courses being offered as follows: Basic Sewing, Advanced
Sewing, Rug Braiding, Upholstery and Furniture Refinishing. These courses cover
two ten-week periods during the year with half of the cost for the program being
reimbursed by the State. Currently, eighty-four citizens are participating in the
in-town program, while sixteen citizens are enrolled in Adult courses in other
communities taking courses not offered in Lunenburg since we do not have the
specific facilities for these courses.
The Advisory Committee for this program and the School Committee are anxious
that any evening Adult Program might truly reflect the interests and desires of the
community; they have sent out a questionnaire to parents asking them to indicate
their interests. With this information at hand, it is the hope that the program
might be enhanced in 1963 by using the State-aided Vocational courses as a nucleus
and adding possibly limited academic or cultural courses reflecting Community needs
as determined by the survey. These additional courses would have a reasonable
tuition fee that should make the course practically self-supporting. It is my sin-
cere hope that this program as a result of current plans will come to be one which
will truly fill Adult Education interests of the community.

School Nurse
SIGNE E. SCHULTZ, School Nurse
Following is a report of school nursing for the year:
Booster shots for diphtheria-tetanus and Salk polio vaccine were made available
to students in Grades 1, 6, and 11. One-hundred fifty-two diphtheria-tetanus and one
hundred ninety-nine polio boosters were given.
Mantoux testing for tuberculosis were done on children in Grade 1. Five had a
positive test and were required to have a chest x-ray along with members of their
families. X-ray findings were negative in all cases, A large number of teachers,
school personnel and bus drivers had Mantoux tests and chest x-rays as required by law.
Sabin Oral Polio Vaccine became available in the Spring to children under seven
years. Types I and III of the oral vaccine were offered to children in Grade 1. They
were very well received as evidenced by the fact that all but a very few were given
permission to participate. This vaccine has now become available to all age groups
and we are now scheduling dates for giving it throughout the schools.
A dental fluorine program is held annually for children in grades 2, 5, and 8.
Nearly all children participate in this program which includes cleaning and exam ination
for dental defects and application of stannous fluoride. Miss June Duteau is the
Dental Hygienist-in-charge Notices urging early dental care for thosewho are in
need of it are sent home.
Through the sponsorship of the P. T. A., a dental education program was carried
out during Dental Health Week in February. The talks and demonstrations were given
in Grades 1, 3, and 4 by Mrs. Clarence May, Dental Hygienist.
Vision and hearing tests are given annually to all students. Notices of defects
are sent home and most of the forms have been returned signed by an eye, ear, nose
and throat specialist.
Physical examinations are made routinely involving children in Grades 1, 4, 7,
and 10 by Dr. John DeLeo, School Physician. All students who participate in competa-
tive sports have annual physical examinations.
The Future Nurses Club which was organized last year continues to be popular; the
monthly meetings are well attended. Members of the group have assisted us in our work
on several occasions and their assistance is much appreciated.
In closing, I wish to express my appreciation to the school administrators, the
teaching staff, the school personnel and volunteer helpers, who have cooperated in
making the school nursing program successful.

School Lunch Program
RUTH A. CORRELL, Supervisor
This is my Seventh Annual Report of the Lunenburg School Lunch Program and is
for the school year, September 1961 to September 1962.
During this period we fed 161,810 "Type A" lunches to students and 10,518 lunches
to adults for a period of 171 lunch days, which is an average of 1,007.7 lunches per
day in all schools combined, Beyond these figures, we sold to students 133,052 half-
pints of milk under the United States Department of Agriculture Special Milk Act Pro-
gram, as well as 4,511 half-pints to adults. That is a total of 309,891 half-pints
of milk purchased for all schools during the school year.
Financially at the end of 1961 the program ended in a deficit of $271.71 which
was paid by a transfer of $2,000.00 by the Finance Committee. After the deficit was
paid, this left an opening balance of $1,728.29 for the beginning of September, 1961.
After all bills were paid, September ,1962, there was a balance of $4,051.16 for the
beginning of the school year 1962.
Commodities donated by the United States Department of Agriculture were a big
factor in helping the program during the year. We received butter, lard, frozen lamb,
beef, whole wheat flour, rolled wheat, dried milk powder, pea beans, peanut butter,
cheeses, potatoes, dried raisins, rice, and fresh cranberries, canned cranberries,
corn, tomatoes, applesauce, sliced apples, peas, sauerkraut, grapefruit sections,
pumpkin, red sour cherries, peaches, green beans, apricots, and canned pork and gravy.
These are available different months and in quantities in keeping with the number of
students fed. The total valuation of these donated commodities was $15,962.40.
Participation percentage-wise for the year in the Junior-Senior High School was
77% according to attendance and 74% according to membership. In the Elementary School,
participation according to attendance was 67% and 63% according to membership.
Personnel in the Junior-Senior High School consists of Mrs. Dorothy Maki as the
Head Cook, and Mrs. Emelian Perry as the Baker. The other workers are Mrs. Edith
Burger, Mrs. Myrtle Dickey, Mrs. Edna Huff, Mrs. Fannie Ovaska, Mrs. Florence Scionti
and Mrs. Mary Thibault. In June, Mrs. Dickey and Mrs. Scionti resigned for personal
reasons and were replaced by Mrs. Ethel Malone, who was with us before, and Mrs. Louise
Nicoll. Mrs. Maki had to resign in August because of ill health and Mrs. Lucy Char-
trand, from the Elementary School, took her place.
The personnel in the Elementary School consists of Mrs. Phyllis Prouty as the
Manager, Mrs. Hazel Whitney, the Head Cook, Mrs. Helen Hebert as Assistant Cook, and
Mrs. Lucy Chartrand, the Baker. The general workers are Mrs. Elizabeth Gilchrest,
replaced by Mrs. Genevieve Nass; Mrs. Bertha Geigner, and Mrs. Rita Seguin. Mrs.
Seguin replaced Mrs. Chartrand as the Baker and a new worker, Mrs. Irene St. Jean,
was hired.
I feel we have had a very fine and dedicated staff in the lunch program for the
past two years and it shows in our financial report.

At the Thanksgiving Dinner this past November 20, 1962, we served 1,607 "Type A"
lunches. At our Christmas Dinner, we served 1,569 lunches on December 20, 1962. The
Elementary Annex pupils joined us at the Junior-Senior High School to enjoy the holi-
day meals. The Elementary grade students and teachers made very appropriate table
decorations for the holidays and Mrs. Drumm's art students decorated the High School
cafeteria very beautifully.
The Annual Spring School Lunch Workshops were held in Boston and Westfield for a
three-day period during our April vacation. The cafeteria personnel split into thirds
so a group attended each day of the workshop in Boston. I attended the three days in
Westfield and received a certificate.
Mrs. Prouty, the manager in the Elementary Cafeteria, and I, attended the Super-
visors and Managers Conference at the University of Massachusetts for the second year
after school ended in June. Lectures and courses were given by the staff of the Uni-
versity and other State universities. We were assigned to different classes in
accordance with the particular position held in order to make the meeting most mean-
ingful. Both of us received certificates from the University of Massachusetts,
The Annual Convention of the Massachusetts Food Services Association was held
this year in Worcester at the Sheraton Hotel, September 28 and 29, 1962. As I am
presently secretary of the Association, I attended the two day period while Mrs. Prouty,
Mrs. Whitney, Mrs. Chartrand, and Mrs. Perry were in attendance on the second day
only.
On Friday, November 2, 1962, in conjunction with the Teachers' Convention, a con-
vention for all Worcester County School Lunch personnel was held at Oakmont Regional
in Ashburnham. Lunenburg was represented 100%.
I completed a course on "Emergency Feeding", sponsored by Civil Defense, in
Bourne, Massachusetts, doing an actual "cook-out" and demonstration for a couple hun-
dred people.
Due to the change in activities in the High School this Fall, I have had to
train twenty-six students to use the cash registers and make change in the hallways.
Besides these students, twenty-seven boys are available as regulars and substitutes
to clean tables between lunch periods. They are on a rotating basis and seven work
each day for a lunch. This includes the sixth grade in the Junior-Senior High School
as well.
We felt we had to change our cash and money operation this Fall in both schools,
SO a new system of collection was begun in the Elementary School, December 3, 1962.
Eventually I would like to have one register, the same as that used in the High School,
and have everything on a day-to-day basis thereby relieving the faculty of money
collection.
In closing, I wish to express my thanks to you, Mr. Brown, and to the School
Committee, also to the Principals of the schools, the faculties, the town personnel
and boards, to Mr. Archambault and his custodians, and to my own personnel, who are
assuming so much responsibility in making our lunch program truly effective and
successful.
