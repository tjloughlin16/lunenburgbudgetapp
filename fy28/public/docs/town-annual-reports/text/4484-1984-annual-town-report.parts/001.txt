BOARD OF SELECTMEN: Seated left to right - Ann P. Hall, Chairman August N. Misner and Walter B. Keeler. Standing
left to right - Executive Secretary Barry J. Stell and Town Counsel Timothy S. Hillman.
In recent years financial problems have dominated the tone and tenor of Town Government. Proposition 2½ -- while a
boon to the property owner -- has been the bane of local government officials. We have struggled with one another over a
smaller number of dollars and battled with the State for more assistance in order to provide the services of local government--
from education to snow-plowing. We are pleased to report that in Lunenburg the lines of communication are more open than
ever, and in most instances, the spirit of cooperation and respect for one another's problems and responsibilities is greater than
ever.
This spirit was manifested by the widespread support for education at the 1984 Annual Town Meeting and again by support
for the special needs of other departments at the November Special Town Meeting, notably by support for those faced with the
task of managing the problems of development in our community.
Growth is undoubtedly the major concern of Lunenburg for 1985. The robust economy under President Ronald Reagan has
opened the way for building and development; and our Town appears to be the darling of the developers. Responsible local gov-
ernment cannot deny building and so must MANAGE growth in a manner dedicated to retaining the desired quality of life in a
changing community.
As the Chief Executive of town government, the Board of Selectmen the duty to co-ordinate the planning and regulatory
activities of ALL town officials. Growth Management will require the financial support of those who must deliver supervision
and services in an eraof growth and change. The structure of town government must be equipped to meet the needs of the future.
Part-time officials and home grown common sense may have sufficed in the past, but the future must be met with the addition of
adequate facilities, professional management, and expertise if Lunenburg is to continue it's tradition of responsive, well-man-
aged local government.
REPORT OF EXECUTIVE SECRETARY TO THE BOARD OF SELECTMEN
The Board of Selectmen spent a great deal of 1984 confronting issues related to zoning. The increased complexity of zoning
was in proportion to the increase in zoning applications. In conjunction with zoning issues, the Board of Selectmen, serving as
Board of Health, began to take a close look at methods which would help protect the community's natural resources, specifically
its water supply. The Board of Selectmen committed themselves to drafting new Board of Health regulations which will
place tighter restrictions on the construction of additions on lots with limited square footage in order to regulate the effect of
construction on the use of the existing septic system and to determine the need for any improvements to the septic system.
The Board charged several new committees to look at specific issues that affect the residents of the community and town
government itself. A Cable Advisory Committee was appointed to advise the Board of Selectmen regarding theoperation of cable
television in the community. An Ad Hoc Communications Committee was formed resulting in a recommendation for a new and
modern phone system for Town Hall, the Highway Department and the Police and Fire Department complex. This phone system
became operable in August of 1984.

Within the town government structure, a Safety Committee, consisting of representatives of various departments, was
formed for the specific purpose of improving safety conditions and thus reducing or eliminating accidents which result in loss
time in wages for employees and increased insurance premiums for the Town. A committee consistingof representatives of various
town departments and members of the community has also been developed to compile an assessment of the accessability to Town
programs and facilities for handicapped individuals. In addition, the Building Committee became active with new members who
spent long hours assessing the current status of the Town Hall facilities in order to ascertain what is needed to meet the current
and future needs of various Town Departments, Committees and Boards.
At the Annual Town Meeting in 1984, the Board sponsored an article to adopt Chapter 41, Section 23A of the Massach-
usetts General Laws which permits the Board of Selectmen to grant more authority and responsibility to the Executive Secretary.
This article was passed at the 1984 Annual Town Meeting.
The Office of Selectmen applied for two State grants in 1984. A feasibility study on the joint use of lock-upfacilities
by the City of Fitchburg and the towns of Ashburnham and Lunenburg was funded, and will determine adequate liability costs to
be shared by the communities and will hopefully result in a long term agreement regarding the use of the City of Fitchburg's lock-
up facilities by the Town of Lunenburg. In addition, a grant application was submitted to provide computer consultant services
for a feasibility study of the computerneeds of the town government and determine what coordination can be accomplished with
the current computer system within the School Department. The Board of Selectmen also signed an application for a grant from
the Clean Lakes Program. This application, sponsored by the residents of Lake Shirley, would provide a portion of the funds
needed to conduct an in depth feasibility study of the water quality of Lake Shirley and its watershed area, and develop recom-
mendations for methods to improve the current conditions.

Auditor
I have examined the financial statements for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1984. My examination included the books and
accounts of the Treasurer, Tax Collector, and all Committees entrusted with the receipt or expenditures of money, and all original
bills and vouchers on which money has been paid from the treasury. I have verified cash and reconciled the bank balances as
required, and in my opinion they present fairly the balances in these accounts (see Schedules 1, 2, and 3 attached). My exa-
minations were made in accordance with generally accepted auditing standards.
In my examination of all original bills and vouchers I also made tests for duplicate payments to certain vendors. No dis-
crepancies were found. All treasury warrants have been approved by the accounting officer. Audit testing disclosed number
of vouchers processed for payment without the proper approval of the appropriate department head. A standard approval form
has been initiated which has corrected the voucher approval process by the appropriate department head.
During my review of payroll, I found time sheets being processed without appropriate department head approval and instances
of sequential voided checks not being filed. A standard time sheet has been initiated by me and accepted by all department
heads and all checks are now being maintained and filed. Also, note was made of inconsistencies on pay for part-time employees
on holidays, and have been corrected by the Personnel Board.
Consistent with the practices of many municipalities in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, the Town has not maintained
historical cost records of its' property, plant and equipment. I have, in a letter to the Selectmen, recommended a complete
inventory and cost up-date be initiated to provide current insurable values and eventually, fixed asset accounting in accordance
and conformity with generally accepted accounting principles. Neither property, plant and equipment, or materials and supplies
are capitalized at date of purchase, but are recorded as expenditures.
I submitted to the Selectmen a standard procedure for the settlement of insurance claims, and suggested that it be adopted
by the Town, for use by all departments. Some confusion occurred in the settlement of replacement for stolen property. It is
noted that all claims should be handled by one central control board, namely, the Board of Selectmen.
The Town of Lunenburg accepted the Uniform System of Accounting in 1930, and still uses its accounts systems. The Town
has adopted the revised 1981 Uniform Massachusetts Accounting System (U.M.A.S.) for its' financial statement presentation for
1982. The Department of Revenue has recommended the segregation of billing, collection and accounting functions for more
effective internal control. It is my opinion that safeguards exist, and that control is adequate.
This report completes my first fiscal year (F.Y. 1984) audit. It has been one of education, cooperation, initiating certain
corrective measures (as mentioned above). My examination foundother minor discrepancies which have been corrected. I certify
this report states my opinion of the accounting procedures in the Town of Lunenburg, and is correct to the best of my knowledge.

Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors: left to right - Secretary Jacqueline A. Keeler, William F. Reed, Bernard J. Paton and
Chairman Kathryn M. Herrick.
The year 1984 was a hectic one for the Assessors. The job of Assessors has become more complex and
time consuming over recent years. We feel that the work load, like that of the Building Inspector, has increased
to the point that our weekend field trips from September to February will no longer be sufficient to complete
the Job. The Assessors feel that now would be the best time to hire an Assistant Assessor and will include the
acquisition in the FY '86 budget request.
In FY '86 Lunenburg will be required to go through triennial recertification by the Department of Revenue
to show that we are assessing at "100% Full and Fair Market Value". This means that we will need a mini
revaluation. The firm of R. J. Finnegan Inc. was low bidder and chosen to do the real property portion. A. R. Gan-
non, Inc. was hired to do the personal property portion and both will begin in early January 1985.
In conjunction with the revaluation, a micro computer will be set up in the Assessor's Office. This will
enable the firm to complete their work more timely and also allow on-the-job computer training for assessors
and clerks. The Assessors will include an article in the Annual Town Meeting Warrant for the purchase of a
micro computer and associated software. We feel that this acquisition would greatly reduce the clerical work
load and eliminate outside contractural services which will result in a three year payback of funds required to
complete the process.

Planning Board: left to right - George B. Larkin, Marion M. Benson, Paul J. Shattuck and Chairman James A.
Mann. Not present when photo was taken was Frederick L. Cuddy.
Since the acceptance by the Town of a new Master Plan in 1983, your Planning Board has set about in-
itiating its implementation. As we all recognize, Lunenburg is a desirable place in which to live, largely
because, in an area which is rapidly becoming suburbanized, Lunenburg has managed to maintain much of its
small town, semi-rural atmosphere. Recognizing that increased growth is inevitable, the Master Plan reflects
your concerns and wishes for orderly growth.
The variety of Planning Board articles in 1984, two special town meetings, the annual town meeting were
attempts to deal with some primary issues -
- keeping a zoned residential area, residential.
- incorporating accessory housing in a manner intended to assist current residents.
— initiated a survey of the town's water resources so as to enable sensible current land use development.
- initiated a town center study which will hopefully resolve most of the problems associated with population
growth which always carries with it an increased need for services - viz: fire, police, municipal administration,
recreation, education, etc.

subdivision off Arbor Street, now Canterbury Drive, while initially approved, remains in limbo. There is at pre-
sent a preliminary plan for a sub-division off Island Road.
Numerous questions for zoning for cluster and condominium type housing developments have been
presented to the Board and are under consideration. It is the Board's consensus that such ordinances will
come about, but only after it has a better grasp on the best practical utilization of land in Lunenburg, l.e., water
availability, and the ability to safely dispose of waste waters.
Needless to say, much of the expertise needed for resolution of a number of the Board's questions does
not exist in-house. We are, therefore, seeking to obtain funds to retain the services of consultants as they are
needed. It is expected that this same consulting service will be useful to the needs of the Conservation Com-
mission as well. Not only do the two groups share common quarters, but have associated problems including,
until very recently, the availability of clerical assistance.
In closing, the Chairman would like to express his appreciation for the cooperation and effort put forth by
his Board - three of whom were new to the Board in the year 1984 and the fourth for approximately one year.

Library Trustees: seated left to right - Carlton A. Wollenberg, Director Elizabeth H. Burnap and Chairman Ed-

The year 1984 marked the arrival of Ritter Memorial Library's 75th anniversary year. As part of the "Old
Home Days" celebration in June, the laying of the library cornerstone was recalled with an open house and
ceremonies with trustees and town officials. The architect's original plans for the building were displayed, as
well as pictures and programs from earlier days. Among the guests were John and Alfred Gilchrest, who had at-
tended the actual cornerstone laying 75 years ago. Another event in the year long observance was a program
with Edith Thomas of Leominster demonstrating wall stenciling techniques and speaking on the history of
-10-
===PAGE 13===
stenciling. Other speakers are scheduled for the coming spring, and a grand finale in August, 1985, all funded
by a grant from the Massachusetts Arts Lottery. The trustees are sponsoring an art contest on the theme of
local history, with entries to be displayed through the spring and a prize awarded at the library rededication in
August.
Steadily increasing use of the library continues to stretch resources to the limit. Each year, the amounts
budgeted for basic on-going expenses must be held constant or reduced. Fewer books and other materials are
purchased. The building cannot be properly maintained and does not meet safety standards. Staff size is held
down even while absorbing growth in services and offering new ones, such as the circulation of videocasset-
tes supplied by the Central Massachusetts Regional Library System. A heavy, unexpected expense, now in the
offing, will be repairing the foundation, site of a serious leak. There is no money for this in the regular budget,
due to its already skeletal nature. Neither will there be funds for the usual professional spring cleaning or any
painting, projects hitherto accomplished with remaining year end funds.
Total circulation of materials for the year was 56,596 - 37.7% more than just five years ago. These items
were divided into the following categories: 22,157 adult, 1,070 young adult, and 33,369 children. Services for
children continue to be the fastest growing part of library operation. Accommodating four pre-school story
hour groups, pre-school movies, a summer reading program with 190 sign-ups and numerous special events,
the regular visits of all Primary School classes, and after school pupils from all grades requires skillful
organization, and continuous planning and supervision. A successful "Author Day" with Christopher Collier,
writer of children's historical fiction, was held in the Middle School, funded by the Massachusetts Arts Lottery.
The annual February Children's Art Festival was attended by an enthusiastic crowd.
The Friends of the Library have again provided invaluable support with their volunteeer work and funds for
various important items including: museum passes, story hour materials, summer programs, book awards for
the summer reading program, and rental books. They sponsored International Nights, speakers on investments
and calligraphy, and the Children's Art Festival.
Funds from the Lunenburg Memorial Fund, given in memory of Dr. James L. Congdon, were used to pur-
chase frames and poster size prints of photographs taken by Suzanne Blake and June Dettenrieder for an at-
tractive wall display. Chapter I Staff of Fitchburg donated a number of new books in memory of Mary Emery.
Books were also given in memory of Ronald Arnold, C. Cameron Burnap, and Mr. and Mrs. Ernest Tanguay.
Serious cuts in book buying were avoided through use of a substantial amount of library trust fund interest. A
bequest of $2,000 to the trust funds was received from the estate of Hazelle C. Kimball.
An intensive effort to recover long overdue books, including an amnesty from fines, brought a number of
volumes back to the shelves where they are once again available to the public. It was discovered that less than
1% of all materials checked out remain out indefinitely, indicating that there are relatively few truly irresponsi-
ble patrons.
Saturday hours have been changed from 1 p.m. - 5 p.m. to 10 a.m. - 2 p.m. which seems to please most
users.
A vacancy on the Board of Trustees, due to the resignation of June Dettenrieder, was filled in December by
the appointment of Jane Oliver-Purton to serve until the next town election.
Automation has now become a fact of life in large libraries and due to the growing interconnectedness of
all informational services, in smaller libraries as well. While the cost of computerizing library functions seems
prohibitive at present, it clearly will be a matter of "automate or stagnate" in the future. The trustees and staff
are investigating when and how this awesome step might be taken. At the same time, it must be borne in mind
that many individuals still want to use library resouces for recreational reading only, and their needs must be
considered before money is diverted from book buying to provision of automated information. All signs at this
time indicate serious problems ahead for the library in attempting to provide full services to users. The out-
come will depend simply on whether or not townspeople value their library enough to provide adequate sup-
port.

The Lunenburg Housing Authority is in its 15th year in the operation of the facilities at 131 White Street,
known as the Pearl Brook Housing for the Elderly. Presently, we have forty-five apartments occupied by single
persons, and three occupied by couples.
The tenants have organized the Pearl Brook Tenants' Association which meets on a regular basis and ar-
ranges Interesting projects for the benefit and enjoyment of its members. Mr. Eugene DesLauriers holds the of-
fice of President. He is the principal liaison between the tenants and the Authority members.
The tenants are provided with weekly transportation by the Council on Aging, and the shut-ins are provid-
ed with "Meals on Wheels". The Nashoba Associated Boards of Health provide monthly Well-Oldster Clinics
for the tenants. Home Health Care provides household duties and shopping. Holy Mass is celebrated every
Monday at 11:00 A.M.
The requirements for occupancy are that the applicants be at least sixty-five years of age, and in need of
good housing. Rents are variable in that no more than 25% of income can be charged for rent which includes
heat, water, and electricity. The rising cost of energy makes it very difficult for the Authority to keep within
their budget.

Personnel Board
Personnel Board: Seated Lynn F. Markham. Standing left to right - Michael V. Sack, Beatrice E. Stepton and
Chairman, John L. Plank, Jr. Not present when photo was taken was Richard E. Baker.
The Personnel Board continues to find new challenges in dealing with Town personnel. The ulitmate
responsibility of the Board is to ensure that both the Town and its employees are treated equitably and fairly.
Town employees are encouraged to ask questions and air complaints with the Board.
Because of the Town's budgetary procedure, the Board must decide very early in the budget process just
what increase there will be in the Salary Administration Plan. This puts the Board and the Town employees at a
disadvantage because other groups settle just before or after Town Meeting. If there is a difference in the
percentage increase between the various groups, then the Board must play "catch up" at later Town Meetings.
The Personnel Board wishes the situation were different, but as our Town budget procedure now stands, we
see this "catch up" process as being a fact of life.
The growth of Lunenburg and new government regulations In recent years has added a new dimension to
the scope of the Board's responsiblity. As other boards see their workload increase, we are adding higher level
employees to our plan to aid the Town in handling the increased responsibilities.
Lunenburg is fortunate to have fine, dedicated employees and the Personnel Board intends to keep them
enthusiastic and dedicated through fair treatment and prompt attention to their problems.

The Conservation Commission wishes to extend its gratitude to the Board of Selectmen, the Board of
Health, and the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health for their cooperation during the past year. This coopera-
tion was especially appreciated considering the fact that the Commission has added four new members.
These include Russell Harvey, Matthew Pearson, Thomas Lawson, and Lee DeNike.
The Commission regretfully acknowledges the resignations of former Chairman, Raymond Chapdelaine
and Members, John Lahti and Robert Garneau. Their years of sage guidance have resulted in a legacy of protec-
tion for the Town's wetland areas.
Historically, the task of the Commission has been one of managing and protecting the Town's con-
servation lands. While attempting to continue to do this, the emphasis of the work of the Commission has
dramatically changed during the past year because of legislative mandates and the continued building growth
within the Town. Through the Commonwealth's Wetlands Act, the Commission is charged with the respon-
sibility of protecting the Town's wetlands. This responsibility has resulted this year in ten hearings, twenty-
seven inspections, and two cease and desist orders. This unanticipated but important role has led members of
the Commission to temporarily defer their goals of obtaining more conservation land and acquainting the
Townspeople with the existing holdings.
One of the potential critical problems facing the Town is that of contaminates leaking from underground
storage facilities. Such a condition would pose a threat to the environment and to the public health. To prevent
such damage, the Commission solicits the support of the citizens to enact safeguards.

This has been a year of orientation and
reorganization for the Historical Commission. With a
full slate of new members, the Commission is once
again taking up the task of an inventory of our
historical assets. The purpose of the Historical Com-
mission is to preserve the past for future genera-
tions. The inventory is the first step in documenting
the historical assets of the Town. Included in the in-
ventory are areas, buildings, structures and sites
which are historically, architecturally, archeological-
ly or culturally significant. The inventory contains
the exact location and a statement of its
significance to the community with appropriate
documentation. Once completed, the inventory is
sent to the Massachusetts Historical Commission.
Through the Massachusetts Historical Commission,
application could then be made for such things as in-
clusion on the National Register of Historic Places,
consideration for federal and state assisted pro-
grams, federal tax benefits, and other programs to
assist in the maintaining of our historic structures
and sites.
Lunenburg is a town with a rich and colorful
history. We are fortunate to be a town with residents
interested in preserving that history. The Historical
Commission wishes to continue this tradition. The
Commission stands ready to assist in any way with
other committees in Town who are involved in plann-
ing for the Town's future. It is imperative that as we
plan for the future, we are cognizant of the ir-
replaceable historic value and significance of our
Town and its structures, and incorporate into our
plannning that which preserves the past as well as
meets current and future needs.

Town Water Study Committee
Water Study Committee: Seated left to right - George B. Larkin, Scott W. Foster and Russell N. Harvey, Jr.
Standing left to right - Donald Gillis, James L. Deming, James A. Mann and Arlen D. White.
The Water Study Committee began meeting in the fall of 1984. Its objective is to form a strategy for identi-
fying and protecting new sources of drinking water. The Committee unanimously believes the need exists,
especially when viewed against future population growth.
Four potential sources have been discussed: (1) Buying water from a neighboring community; (2) Use an
existing surface water supply such as a lake; (3) Develop additional water bearing sand and gravel deposits
within the Town; and (4) Develop bedrock aquifer within the Town. The first and second alternatives were given
low priority because of political, cost and/or lack of control problems. The third alternative has the highest
priority because it is the least expensive and is a proven approach. The fourth alternative has great potential,
but is not allowed by the State unless the third approach has been fully researched.
The following are the Committee's recommendations
1.
View the situation regarding potable water not just as of today, but also for the longer
time span of 50 or 100 years into the future. We should develop an action plan as a
community focusing on long term health and independence. Water is critical to both.
2.
The Town of Lunenburg should consider this its problem. There is a present problem.
It does not stop with the Water District, but includes areas beyond the District's con-
fines and financial capacity.
3.
The future should be secured with new additional water sources, beyond the water-
shed of the present Town wellfield. Testing should be continued to Identify such
sources. Once identification is completed, the Town should acquire In fee Title the
primary source area if not a larger land area. Two, three or more sources should be set
aside and protected for the long term well being of the community.

future, and were already placed in a job, signed up for military service, or had opted for further education.
(These figures are based on a survey of 180 returns out of 200 graduates.)
A new course offered to staff for the first time In 1984 was an after-hours computer class, using Apple IIE
and Atari 800 computers. When the second session is completed in 1985, 48 staff members will have expanded
their computer knowledge. Thomas J. Shaughnessy, Director of Guidance, received the Outstanding Public
Employee Award from the state, and was recognized in a special ceremony in June, 1984. Monty Tech was
again the site of the Fitchburg conference, an annual professional improvement week of seminars and
workshops.
Students recorded academic and vocational achievements. "Eighty-four Odyssey", the yearbook, received
a Meritorious Achievement Award from the New England Scholastic Press Association. Students had ex-
cellent results in VICA (Vocational Industrial Clubs of America) competitions, earning one gold, two silver, and
one bronze medal. Students were active in the National Honor Society, S.A.D.D., Student Council, and the Stu-
dent Advisory Council. Parental interest resulted in the establishment of the Parents Advisory Committee,
which publishes an informative newsletter about the school, and provides volunteers in several school offices.
Academic programs continued to change, expand, and improve. The Monty Tech academic program matches
any in the area, and students who meet the expectations of the program will more than meet requirements of
any further education they may pursue.

TOWN CLERK, TAX COLLECTOR AND TREASURER
I am pleased to submit my second annual report. The year 1984 has
proved to be a very busy year, which, through both education and
experience, has resulted in improved operations.
As can be seen from the first page of this report, a hallmark of my
administration has been to fully communicate with the townspeople.
It is the responsibility of all public officials to report on a
regular basis what they have done to better the town. This allows
for feedback SO that voters have input between Town Meetings.
Much has been studied and discussed about ways to improve town
government. We are not faced with a crisis of management, rather
we are faced with a crisis of communication. Changing the form of
government may not enhance the exchange of information if there
are still problems. We must solve the obvious problems, rather
than trying to solve manufactured ones. There must be a free
flowing communication without distrust on the part of any depart-
ment. One solution is a monthly networking session of all Town
department heads, with each, on a rotating basis, presenting a
program on how it works.
In order to serve the townspeople more conveniently, the three
offices are now open on Thursday nights until 7 p.m. This coincides
with the hours of Lunenburg banks, and will help those who work out
of town to conduct town business.
TREASURER
Robert J. Ebersole
MONTY TECH, ROUND 1
The Town is faced with many assessments over which it has little direct control, such as the Worcester County
Tax or Retirement System. The Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School is another such assessment.
If 2/3 or more of the member communities vote the submitted budget, it passes over the objections of those
opposed.
Monty Tech is outside of the restraints of Proposition 2½, and can increase its assessments at a greater rate
that we can increase our taxes. Due to changes in state funding in 1984, "pass through" aid to cites and
towns stayed with regional schools. This money, plus underestimated state aid, were not included when the
FY 1985 budget was presented. Over $850,000 was involved.
As Treasurer, I sent out letters of Finance Committees, Mayors, Selectmen and Monty Tech representatives of
member communities to alert them of this situation. Through much media exposure, attendance at public hearings
and many phone calls to pressure for a favorable vote, we were successful.
The Monty Tech School Board voted to return $500,000 in April, and further reduced the assessments even more
than originally requested. This is in part due to a state audit which ordered a reduction in the amount kept
in the school's various accounts. What was the final effect? Lunenburg's original assessment estimate of
$85,253 is now $42,366, which is over a 50% cut.
LUNENBURG RATES AN "A"
Moodys Investors Service provides bond ratings for states, cities and towns throughout the United States.
This bond rating comes into play for major borrowing needs. There are many factors which affect ratings. Many
are beyond the control of a municipality, such as the general economic climate of a state, national growth
(or decline), or state-wide restrictions on the ability to tax (such as Proposition 2½).
However, the financial operations of the Town do play an important part in the process. Does the Town pay its
bills on time? Is there reliance on short-term borrowing to finance current operations? Does the Town have
a reasonable debt level? In other words, does Lunenburg pay its way without borrowing against the future?
Prop. 2½ was not looked upon favorably by Moodys because it impaired a town's ability to pay debt as well as
current expenses. Since Lunenburg has voted to exempt the fire truck debt service, it will help maintain our
rating because it shows that we are willing to tax ourselves to finance needed capital projects.
Another important aspect to the rating is control over growth of budgets. According to Moodys Bond Survey
dated 2/11/85, "The ability to successfully exert expenditure controls appears for many municipalities a
difficult objective to achieve.' Although the current financial situation is better than most had forecasted,
spending on non-capital items should not be increased at the expense of badly needed capital items such as
-36-
===PAGE 39===
roads, public buildings and equipment. For example, once new employees are added into budgets, those positions
will probably never be eliminated thereby impairing the capital budgeting process.
As has been stated year after year, the amount of "Free Cash" available at the end of a fiscal year is
considered to be an important indicator of financial health. Between 3 and 5% of the town's budget is what
Moodys considers reasonable. For Lunenburg, this would be between $230,000 and $390,000. A major source of
free cash is that amount which is not used during one year, and carried over to the next. As the economy cools,
we will need to keep more free cash because state aid and local receipts will decrease. We should therefore
resist the temptation to once more draw it down to dangerous levels.
COMPUTERS
Under Prop. 2½, state mandated procedures which are deemed "incidental" by the state do not need to be funded
by the legislature. Many agencies are requiring more and more reports, which entail more record keeping than
currently in place. These procedures would be greatly simplified if the office was computerized.
TAX COLLECTOR
The Tax Collector is perhaps the least liked officer in the Town. No one really likes paying taxes, but if
the collection process is administered fairly and consistently, all can be assured of equal treatment. The
State has mandated many procedures to insure that taxes will be collected. All bills are counted, amounts
totaled and an affidavit of mailing is filed with the Department of Revenue. The responsibility for payment
is upon the taxpayer, whether a bill is received or not.
Town cash flow considerations require that the collector be ever vigilant. We were able to do the difficult
work to attain our goal of 100% collection rate for fiscal year 1984 real estate taxes. This meant that
either the taxes were paid, or the property was placed into tax title, thereby protecting the town's interest
in the taxes due.
The process to collect fiscal year 1985 real estate taxes began with the May Annual Town Meeting, where the
amount to be raised was determined. We were 5th in the state to have the tax rate set, due to cooperation
between the Treasurer-Collectors' Assessors' and Selectmen's offices. This allowed my office to process the
bills, mail them on time, and receive payments SO that the Town did not have to borrow for short term uses.
As an interesting side note, most people do not realize that we send over 10,000 motor vehicle excise bills a
year. Eliminating transfer registrations, there are approximately 8000 vehicles in Town. It is easy to see
how traffic problems occur.
TOWN CLERK
ELECTIONS, ELECTIONS, ELECTIONS
It is clear that 1984 was a year of contrasts. Over 5000 people were registered to vote for the Presidential
Election, yet we had the lowest turnout in over 30 years of the Annual Town Election. Turnout depends on the
contests and issues involved - the November election was SO important to people that a record 4211 voted,
while there were no contests in the Town Election except for the debt exclusion for the fire truck. The
Presidential Preference Primary was held on a snowy day in March, reducing the vote to only 738.
Throughout the year, major efforts were made to assure that all could register to vote if they were eligible.
Registrations sessions were held at the High School, Fitchburg State College, Mt. Wachusett Community College,
as well as weekend and evening sessions.
To speed returns of elections, votingmachines are part of the Town's capital budget plan. Over the years, we
have been able to find hardworking and talented people to hand count ballots, and the Town can be grateful to
them for their quality work and the spense saved.
RECORDING OUR WORDS FOR POSTERITY
For the first time in Lunenburg's history, the special and annual town meetings held in 1984 were tape
recorded. Authorized by state law, the recordings will provide a back-up to the manual minutes process, and
will be an important addition to the Town's historical records. The tapes may be consulted to look behind a
vote in the event that there is a contest or lawsuit as to the purpose of a particular bylaw or appropriation.
These tapes are a public record, and are accessible during regular office hours.
HISTORICAL PRESERVATION
The Town Clerk is charged with the safekeeping of the Town's records. Based on this duty, a grant was
applied for, and received from, the Massachusetts Council on the Arts and Humanities to survey our records.
The Northeast Document Conservation Center in Andover performed the work by reviewing the records, their
storage and access, as well as building and environmental conditions.
The 22 page report detailed simple and complex solutions to the current situation. Some of the changes will
depend on what the Town decides as to the fate of the Town Hall - renovation or building a new structure. The
records are threatened by light, dust, handling, mold, moisture, heat, fire, vandalism and rodents.
