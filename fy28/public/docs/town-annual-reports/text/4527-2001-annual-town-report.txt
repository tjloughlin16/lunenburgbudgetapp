MEMORIAM
PHILIP M. CROUSE
1934-2001
Phil will be remembered by his community as a dedicated educator and public servant. He came to
Lunenburg in 1960 and for 35 years worked in town schools, teaching mathematics and also working
as an administrator. He retired in 1996 as the acting assistant principal of Lunenburg High School.
He was treasurer of the Retired Educators Association of Massachusetts, past board member of the
Lunenburg Credit Union and an active member of Faith United Parish in Fitchburg. In 1994 he was
named "Teacher of the Year" by the high school's National Honor Society Chapter. He spent many
years as the chairman of the town's Finance Committee. His knowledge of the workings of the
school made him a valued voice on the Finance Committee.
PATRICK C. PALMACCIO
1924-2001
Pat came to Lunenburg in 1973 serving on the town's Finance Committee, School Building
Committee, Republican Committee and the Memorial Fund Committee. He was also a past chairman
of the town's Personnel Committee. He was a member of St. Boniface Church where he served on
the parish's Finance Committee. He was a former member of the Board of Directors and a past
treasurer of the Northern Worcester County Board of Realtors, and worked for Century 32 Denault
Realty in Leominster before his death.
JOSEPH L. McBRIDE
1929-2001
Joe lived in Lunenburg for many years and was the Town's Plumbing & Gas Inspector for the past
five years. He was a master plumber and gas fitter, who along with his brother James, owned and
operated McBride Brothers Plumbing and Heating Company for several years. He also worked for 17
years at the former Fort Devens as Chief of Facilities at the U.S. Army Intelligence School until his
retirement in 1993. He was plumbing and gas inspector for the Town of Townsend for 30 years. He
was an avid skier and a member of the area's Flopenaufin Ski Club for more than fifty years. He was
a certified National Alpine Ski official and officiated at many World Cup ski races throughout New
England. He also enjoyed cycling and served for many years on the original committee of the Arthur
M. Longsjo Bicycle Race in Fitchburg. In addition, he participated in and finished three Boston
Marathons in the 1980's and for the past several years worked as a volunteer for the event on race
day. He was a member of St. Boniface Church, the Lunenburg Historical Society and the American
Legion. For many years he was the Town's Santa Claus for the Lunenburg Elementary School
students.
HERBERT N. HOFFMAN
1914-2001
Mr. Hoffman was a longtime member of the Lunenburg School Committee, the Lunenburg Planning
Board and the Zoning Board of Appeals. He was past president of the Lunenburg Historical Society
and a board member of the German Congregational Church of Clinton. He was also a member of the
Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School Committee, and served on several of its
committees. He worked as a design engineer for the General Electric Company for 42 years; 29 years
were spent at the Fitchburg Plant. During his career he was awarded 22 patents.
JOHN O. PIEKSEN SR.
1925-2001
John had a career in banking and served as Vice President of the former First Safety Fund National
Bank in 1063 and up until his retirement in 1990 was President of the former Merchant's National
Bank. For many years he served on Lunenburg's Finance Committee. He was past president and
past director of Leominster Hospital, served on the Board of Director for the United Way, Our
Father's House, Leominster Chamber of Commerce, Fitchburg Red Cross, Children's Aid and Family
Service in Fitchburg. He was an advisory member of the First Federal Reserve Bank and American
Bankers' Association. He was also a member of Oak Hill Country Club, Fay Club and Northfield
Associates.

BOARD OF SELECTMEN/SEWER COMMISSIONERS
The Board of Selectmen/Sewer Commissioners have been involved with (Contract I), Phase I which is the sewer
construction in the Whalom Area. As of this writing that phase is nearing completion and they are awaiting the final
approval from the State to proceed with Contract II, Phase I which will include the center of Town and public buildings.
The Board has also included Electric Avenue for sewering during the next construction season as the Town was the
recipient of a 1.2 million dollar Public Works Economic Development (PWED) Grant for the reconstruction/resurfacing of
Route 13.
The Police/Fire Study Committee has been a priority with the Selectmen as they have been involved in their meetings to
downsize the original structure and still meet the needs of the community, and purchasing the property for the siting of the
new facility. They have also conferred on numerous occasions with the Teen Center Committee and have agreed that a
second building located on the same lot as the Jones House would be more appropriate for the Towns needs. Designs are
still continuing and will include improved parking facilities for the municipal buildings located in the center of Town.
With the expertise of the Chief Administrative Financial Officer, William Gustus, the Town was able to implement the Pay-
As-You Throw trash/recycling program which began in November 2001. Bags are available at the following local retail
establishments; Shop 'n Save, Aubuchon Hardware, Honey Farms, Centre Pizza, Maki Building Center, and Willie's Woolen
Works and are purchased in lots of five (5) for $7.50. The curbside recycling program consists of paper and commingled
recyclables and is done on alternating weeks. For more information on this program please contact this office.
Band concerts again this year were a big hit with young and old enjoying the music and food. Different organizations
sponsor the concerts with hot dogs, hamburgers and desserts. A fun and relaxing way to spend a Monday evening with
friends and neighbors, thanks to Steve Archambault, Conductor, Roger Gilchrest, Organizer and the band members.
The Board once again want to extend a special "thank you" to the following: Lunenburg/Townsend Rotary Club for
decorating the Gazebo at Christmas time, the Turkey Hill Garden Club for the Christmas decorations outside Town Hall as
well as the summer flowers in the Town common. The Lunenburg Grange #169, every year fill the flower boxes at Town
Hall which are donated in memory of Warren Wood.
On a personal note, the Board would like to recognize the recent passing of two of the Town's prominent volunteers:
Chairman of the Finance Committee Philip Crouse who passed away unexpectedly In September and Lunenburg's own
philanthropist, Raymond Powell who passed away in November after a long and courageous battle with cancer. They will
be deeply missed by this board and community.
Your Board has enjoyed working in service to the Town, working with each other, and with you the citizens. They
especially want to thank Bill Gustus (Chief Administrative Financial Officer) for working so well with all town departments
and committees, his expertise is greatly appreciated.
Please feel free to direct any questions to our office staff Laura Dunbar, Executive Secretary or Linda Douglas, Secretary @
978-582-4144, if they cannot assist you, they will forward your call to the appropriate authorities. Our office hours are
Monday, Wednesday and Thursday 8:00 AM to 4:00 PM, Tuesday 8:00 AM to 6:30 PM and Friday 8:00 AM to 12:30 PM.
CHIEF ADMINISTRATIVE FINANCIAL OFFICER
The office of Chief Administrative Financial Officer (CAFO) was created in the year 2000. Under the new Town Charter, the
CAFO is responsible for the coordination and direction of all administrative and financial affairs of the town. The CAFO
takes policy direction from all elected town boards and implements board policies on an ongoing basis.
My goal as CAFO is to improve the quality of life for Lunenburg residents in a cost-effective manner. During 2001, we
implemented a number of new programs, streamlined departmental operations and improved worker efficiency by
providing our people with the tools necessary to do their jobs. The most noteworthy of our initiatives were the construction
of new sewers in the Whalom area and the implementation of the town-wide curbside trash and recycling program. Both of
these efforts were undertaken as the direct result of public participation in our decision making process and serve as a
positive reminder that local government works best when It is responsive to the needs as expressed by it's citizens.
Administratively, we have created a new Department of Public Works by consolidating the former Highway, Parks,
Cemetery, Sewer and Public Facilities Departments. This new organization can now more efficiently coordinate their
efforts, share their personnel and equipment resources, and, in effort, do more for less. Additionally, our Technology
Department has implemented a number of new programs in our Library (a new regional library network), in our Police
Department (a new criminal justice computer system), in our Financial Departments (a comprehensive software program
7
===PAGE 11===
that will integrate all financial management and reporting functions) and in our schools (interconnectivity between each of
the schools and the town's servers).
During the course of the year we continued to improve the financial position of the town. The FY 2002 budget again was
representative of the philosophy of fiscal restraint. All ongoing programs were fully funded, new programs as outlined
above were implemented, and we managed to increase the value of our rainy day fund by almost 50% while continuing the
policy of not taxing our residents to the limit allowed by law. These financial achievements were recognized this year by
Moody's Investor Services, who improved our credit rating by two grades from Baa1 to A2. We were also successful in
finding lucrative alternative financing sources and grants for financing some of our capital projects. The Town applied for
and was awarded over $2 million dollars to upgrade Rt. 13 from the Leominster line to Rt. 2A, completely rehabilitate the
exterior of Town Hall, assist homeowners to tie in to the new sewers, and start up our trash and recycling programs.
In 2001 we continued our commitment to giving our departments the tools necessary to do their jobs and improving our
capital facilities. Over $400,000 was spent on capital equipment and building repairs including everything from the
purchase of new police cruisers and playground equipment to repairing parking lots and improving the air quality in our
schools. With respect to our capital facilities, 2001 saw the first attempt at implementing a comprehensive plan to upgrade
our public buildings. Although the overall Capital Facilities Plan was not adopted this year, its presentation went a long
way to raise the consciousness of the community with respect to the Town's capital needs. In fact, that effort has already
begun to bear fruit. This year, the Town voted to build a new $10.5 million primary school and acquire a site for a new
public safety building.
In retrospect, it has been a very productive year, and I want to thank all of our elected Boards, the Board of Selectmen in
particular, for their guidance and support of my activities; and the department heads and staff, for their unstinting
dedication and cooperation in making Lunenburg a wonderful place to call home.
Respectfully Submitted,
William J. Gustus, CAFO
BUILDING INSPECTOR/ZONING OFFICIAL
The Building Inspectors office is located on the second floor of the Town Hall. Office hours for the Building Department are
Monday, Wednesday and Thursday 8:00 AM until 4:00 PM, Tuesdays 8:00 AM until 6:30 PM and Fridays 9:00 AM to 12:30
PM. The Inspector is available in the office dally and inspections are by appointment. Roger Williams is the Building
Inspector and Zoning Officer for Lunenburg. Harry Cullinan serves as the Assistant Building Inspector and Lisa Normandin
as Administrative Assistant.
Building activity in Lunenburg has been steady in the residential districts. The building department issued 373 permits in
the year 2001. There were 37 new homes constructed, also several garages, barns, additions and renovations, and 20.
wood stove permits. Permit fees in 2001 totaled $45,868.00.
Residents should be aware that Massachusetts Law requires that all persons, partnerships and corporations who bid or
perform residential contracting (reconstruction, alterations, additions etc.) for work exceeding $1000.00 be registered with
the Commonwealth. This office will enforce this provision, however, you should ask your contractor about his/her
registration. Please contact this office with any questions about this requirement, or call for any other information you may
need on Zoning requirements or Building Code issues.
Recent News in building effective March 1, 1998 is the Energy Conservation Requirements for Low-Rise Residential New
Construction. Builders must satisfy two different parts of the energy code. 1. General Requirements, Applies to all low-
rise residential buildings and includes where and how vapor barriers and insulation must be installed: what must be done
to minimize air leakage in buildings and ducts: sizing and controls for heating and cooling systems and requirements for
domestic hot water systems. For more information regarding the energy code contact the Building Inspectors office for a
brochure explaining all the options.
CAPITAL PLANNING COMMITTEE
Committee Members: Marion Benson, Chair, Steven deBettencourt, Elaine Murphy & Ernest Hunter
The Capital Planning Committee continues to strive toward developing a workable Capital Program. Simply stated, a
definition of a Capital program is an expenditure plan of large-scale needs, such as facilities and FY expenditure plans for
current needs of over $5,000.00.
8
===PAGE 12===
The Town has approved an important part of the Town's Capital Program by approving a new Primary School in the fall of
2001. This is a beginning for the large-scale portion of a Capital Program, but the Town must realistically be aware of the
other pending facility needs. The Town must move forward with a scale of economy to achieve these needs as outlined in
the Town's Master Plan.
Since 1992, the Town has approved at Town Meeting, an average of $400,000.00 per year, through its FY plans.
Expenditures have been service vehicles and equipment, facility improvement and capital maintenance. The premise for
these expenditures has been a Policy Criterion created at the onset of the capital planning process. These criteria, set by
the Capital Planning Committee, are mandates, need, crisis, safety, cost effectiveness and ability to fund. The Committee
is always in defeat in the "ability to fund" review.
The Committee has long pleaded the need to balance the revenues to place the proper emphasis on capital spending. The
bottom line for the FY plans of $400,000.00 is not sufficient to address the pending crisis in our present public buildings.
School renovations should, and could be addressed, on a yearly basis so the Community is not faced with large output
necessary to bring these buildings to a workable and safe level. One major concern is the combined heating system for the
elementary and high school. Another concern is the marginal heating system at the Ritter Memorial Library.
The FY 2002 Plan passed at the 2001 Town Meeting included technology updates for all departments, including Phase 1 for
GIS Land Use Program, guard rail replacement on Lancaster Avenue, wood chipper, pickup with plow, 6 wheel dump truck
with plow and sander for the Highway Department, final funding for a three year plan for the Town Beach building, asbestos
removal, one boiler replacement for the schools, boller replacement for the Brooks House, a police cruiser, and Town Hall
roof repairs. These items reflect the type of items that have been the FY expenditures since the beginning of the process.
They have, in many ways, helped update service departments and small facility needs. They have also been effective in
installing technology for all departments. The problem is that everything is cyclical and ongoing capital spending is
needed just to keep pace.
The Committee has been working since August 2001 in preparing the FY 2003 Plan. The Charter notes that the Capital
Program shall be based on material prepared by the Capital Planning Committee established by by-law that includes the
following: a concise general summary of its contents, list of all capital improvements to be undertaken during the next five
years, cost estimates, methods of financing, recommended time schedules and annual cost of operating and maintaining
facilities and major equipment.
The Town's Master Plan outlines the priorities for major facility expenditures. The Capital Planning Committee is presently
concentrating its efforts on the FY Plan looking at the five-year time line. The Committee considers the five year plan a
revolving plan and that plan presently does not take into consideration planned renovation and facility improvement needs
that will maintain our present public buildings. To address this problem would not be difficult under the Committee Criteria
Policy except for the problem of "ability to fund" issue.
The Committee had been planning toward the $400,000.00 average bottom line for FY 2003; however, the Committee was
instructed to prioritize the items that met the criteria within a reduced bottom line. This is due to the analysis of the
financial predictions of revenue availability through the State and local level for the next few years. The defeating criterion
of "ability to fund" again cuts into the program. As the bottom line is cut, there will be a ripple effect on the revolving five-
year plan including the Vehicle Rotation Plan. This is defeating the concept of addressing capital needs in an appropriate
time line, which in turn puts the Town further behind in maintaining facilities and equipment.

CENTRAL MASSACHUSETTS MOSQUITO CONTROL
The Central Massachusetts Mosquito Control Project (the Project) currently provides its services to 34 cities and towns
throughout Middlesex and Worcester Counties. The Project's headquarters is located at 111 Otis Street, Northboro MA.
Tours of the headquarters or visits to field work sites may be arranged by calling the office in advance. Please call 508-393-
3055 during business hours for more information. The Project practices Integrated Mosquito Management (IMM), blending
state of the art methods and techniques with expertise, experience, and scientific research to provide our member
communities with environmentally sound and cost effective mosquito control.
10
===PAGE 14===
During 2001 the Project received 5,271 requests for service from town residents and officials. We scheduled five hundred
and forty-four (544) adult mosquito operations in residential areas, and two hundred and eighty-five (285) larviciding
operations. A total of over 9,327 pounds of Bti (Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis) was applied by helicopter in 2 towns,
Chelmsford & Billerica, and by hand throughout our service area to wetlands to reduce the emergence of adult mosquitoes.
This represents over 1800 acres of wetland that was treated with this mosquito-specific bacteria, significantly reducing
adult mosquito populations in these areas. Seventeen thousand and forty three (17, 043) catch basins were treated with
larvicidal briquettes to control the mosquitoes that seek out these cool dark wet areas to breed, including the Culex
mosquito, a major target for West Nile Virus transmission. Three thousand four hundred forty-seven (3447) culverts were
cleaned in an attempt to eliminate unnecessary standing water and reduce mosquito breeding. This work was done in
conjunction with cleaning, clearing and digging of 108,499 feet of streams, brooks and ditches. This represents over
twenty and one half miles of waterways which were cleaned and improved by Project personnel in 2001.
The Mosquito Awareness Program which we offer to elementary schools and other civic organizations in our district has
become very popular. Project staff meet with students, teachers or concerned residents to discuss mosquito biology,
mosquito habitat, and control procedures. Much of the presentation is directed towards what children and their families
can do to prevent mosquitoes from breeding around their homes. Slides, videos, coloring books and other handouts make
this an interesting program. This program is tailored to meet the needs of the specific audience.
As part of our effort to reduce the need for pesticides we continue to expand our wetlands restoration program. By
cleaning clogged and overgrown waterways, mosquito breeding can be reduced and drainage areas are restored to historic
conditions.
Bti mosquito larvicide is used to treat areas where mosquito larvae are found. We routinely check known breeding sites
kept in our database, but also encourage the public to notify us of any areas they suspect could breed mosquitoes. Our
field crews will investigate all such requests and treat the area only if surveillance gathered at the time shows an imminent
threat of mosquito emergence.
Our goal is to handle all mosquito problems with education, wetlands, restoration or larviciding, but we recognize that
there are times when adult mosquito spraying is the only viable solution. In such cases areas are treated with either
hand-held or pickup truck mounted sprayers if surveillance gathered shows sufficient number of adult mosquitoes present
to warrant an application. This program is available on a request-only basis, and the exclusion process allows residents
and town officials to exclude areas under their control from this, or any part of the program.
The Project's surveillance program monitors adult mosquito and larval population density, and is the backbone for
prescribing various control techniques. Rain gauges are set out and data is collected by our surveillance crews in an effort
to predict when mosquito breeding will occur. Specialized mosquito traps are deployed throughout the Project's service
area to sample mosquitoes that may be transmitting mosquito-borne diseases. In conjunction with the Mass Department of
Public Health we sample in areas suspected of harboring WNV and other viruses.
Educational pamphlets are available to anyone interested in learning about mosquito control and the services provided by
the Project, and these items are routinely stocked in member Town/City Halls and Libraries. We also have a website,
www.cmmcp.org that has extensive information our procedures. This website has become a model for other
Mosquito Projects, and has been widely used throughout our service area and beyond.
We would like to thank you for your support during 2001 and we look forward to helping you and your community with its
mosquito problems in 2002 and beyond.

HISTORICAL COMMISSION
Rebecca Lantry, Chairperson
The Historical Commission of Lunenburg is responsible for community-wide historic preservation planning. This mission
has taken us in many directions this past year. Town records cataloguing have continued in the vault at Town Hall, with a
light beginning to glow at the end of the tunnel. All the books have been indexed and only loose papers and Deeds are still
being organized. We have purchased a software package called Past Perfect, which when it is up and running, will allow
researchers easy access to what we have for historic documents and artifacts. We are collaborating with the Historical
Society to begin using this cataloging tool while we await a more modern computer to handle the software. One member in
our Commission has focused on the Town Cemeteries, collecting more data and information so they will be accepted by
the State Historical Commission as part of our Town wide Inventory.
The Commission has been involved in two preservation campaigns to threatened local historic properties. The Stillman
Stone House at 395 Massachusetts Avenue and the Jones House at 42 Main Street in Lunenburg. The Stillman Stone
house was lost to demolition for parking lot expansion despite newspaper articles and nomination to the 10 most
Endangered Historic Resource List for 2001. The Jones House was reconsidered against demolition by the Town for a new,
to be built, Teen Center. A plan of mutual co-existence with the Teen Center is now being planned for the home site. Both
of these houses made the Historic Commission acutely aware of how vulnerable Lunenburg's Historic homes are to
destruction and not preservation. The Historic Commission has worked with the Planning Board to put a Demolition Delay
By-law on Town Warrant this spring to protect our historic properties from hasty decision of demolition without all
preservation options being exercised.
The Historic Commission applauds the efforts a few citizens have made to make the Town aware of the Community
Preservation Act. This would be a valuable preservation tool for maintaining Lunenburg's Historic properties especially in
these tight economic times. The Historic Commission looks forward to a time of more success stories and less ineffectual
hand wringing.

June 30, 2002 after serving as Chief for nineteen and a half years. I have served on the Fire Department for a total of forty-
two years. I will very much miss doing the job but am also looking forward to pursuing other interests and spending more
time with my family. I sincerely thank the people of Lunenburg both those associated with the Department and those that
aren't for all the support and encouragement given to me in my tenure as Chief. You've made this job a dream for me and I
hope in some small way I've been able to have helped some of you when you needed help.
I want you to know that the men and women of the Lunenburg Fire Department and Rescue Services are second to none in
their devotion to the services they render to you. They are superbly trained and deeply committed to providing you with
the very best fire protection and emergency medical care possible. I am very proud to have had the opportunity to lead
them over the last nineteen and a half years and leave with Immense satisfaction.
We are still operating from our cramped and unsafe quarters on School Street. This is the single most important project
that needs to be addressed as soon as possible. We are in desperate need of a new public safety facility now. It can't wait
much longer. For us to be able to continue the services we supply we need to have adequate quarters to work from and
store our apparatus. This is a critical need. We ask your support in this venture.

POLICE DEPARTMENT
Chief Daniel F. Bourgeols
The year 2001 has brought many changes to the Law enforcement Community both locally as well as nationally. In the
aftermath of the tragic events of September 11, 2001, it became evident that "homeland security" was to take on an entirely
new meaning.
Locally the Lunenburg Police Department prepares to strike a balance between local community policing initiatives and
homeland security. The events of 09-11-01 have only strengthened our resolve to continue to provide the best public
safety initiatives possible for the residents of Lunenburg. Our officers, like many of you, watched in disbellef, sadness and
compassion as the events of 09-11-01 unfolded. Since then we have struggled with the desire to do more and commit
ourselves to new areas of learning and training to prepare for the future.
On September 18, 2001 Attorney General Ashcroft directed all U. S. Attorneys to establish an Anti Terrorism Task Force in
their respective districts. The ATTF will work in concert with the Boston FBI, which established a Joint Terrorism Task
Force. These agencies have already opened lines of communication with all police chiefs and the Mass. State Police. The
resulting network of contacts enhances our communications on a local level thus enabling our department to quickly
access information, protocols and training in the area of domestic preparedness.
I am proud to report that Lunenburg continues to be a safe community. Violent crime is at a minimum and crimes against
property have leveled. Due to the dedicated work of our officers and detectives we have been able to increase follow up
investigations on calls for service resulting in the arrest and prosecution of numerous criminal offenders.
I continue to dedicate my efforts in working with town officials in an effort to solve our operating space needs. Our police
station, built as an addition to the fire department in 1978, is inadequate and does not meet our present operational needs.
We have assessed the current deficiencies and long-range needs of the department and continue to work with fire,
dispatch and town officials to address the future of a Municipal Public Safety Building to meet these important needs.
Therefore we ask for your continued support on our building plans for the future. It is incumbent on our town officials to
properly address our public safety operational and staffing needs in order to be best prepared for the future.
During this time of homeland security crisis combined with local and state wide fiscal uncertainties, it becomes a difficult
task to balance the cost of providing adequate police protection for the town of Lunenburg as well as meeting the
equipment, training and technological needs of our officers. As our town and neighboring populations and commercial
properties grow, so does the need for additional staff to meet the increased demand for police services. I will continue to
work with town officials to address this issue and ask for your support during these uncertain times. I commend our
officers for stepping up to the plate to meet the increasing challenges and demands placed on them while dedicating
themselves to providing the most professional public safety and law enforcement possible.
Our community policing initiatives proved to be very rewarding during 2001. Bike and walking patrols in our
neighborhoods and shopping areas continued to assist officers with direct contact with our residents on a pro-active level.
Boat and Jet Ski patrols on Lakes Whalom and Shirley ensured safe recreational times for residents as well as visitors to
the area. Our first Senior Citizen Public Safety barbeque was a huge success bringing our staff and seniors together to
learn more about the police and fire departments and our equipment and operations. Teen dating violence, internet crimes,
Halloween and bicycle safety as well as driver's safety classes were held in our public schools to better educate our youth
in these areas, Firearms safety, child fingerprinting and Rape Aggression Defense classes were also held for the
community. Unfortunately the upcoming Community Policing State Grant budget appropriations may leave a reduction of
up to 60% of funds available for our community policing initiatives in 2002.
Traffic issues and accidents continue to increase and are a top priority for our department. As our town and area
populations increase so does traffic on our local roadways. I am presently working with our CAFO In hopes of Increasing
our present budget appropriations to enhance our present traffic enforcement and education programs within our
community. The Police Department also respectfully requests your patience and consideration with traffic details and
delays during the upcoming sewerage projects planned throughout the town for 2002.
In the year 2001, the Lunenburg Police Department documented 8491 incidents. Included in these incidents were 123
Domestic Abuse investigations, 149 Disturbance calls, 401 Fire and Medical assists, 388 motor vehicle accidents, 640
alarms answered, 592 Community policing initiatives, 114 Mutual Aid responses, 113 Well being checks and 112 responses
to Hang-up 9-1-1 calls.

RITTER MEMORIAL LIBRARY
Susan Tallman, Director
A proclamation to Ritter's forty-five volunteers reads "volunteer assistance is critical to this library that is truly a
community center where the poor and the affluent, the young and not-so-young, and people of all religions, races, and
beliefs feel welcome in an atmosphere of learning and tolerance for diversity and opinion"
Library service is improving steadily. A comparison of our library with those in towns of comparable population and EQV
(property valuation) reveals that open hours, number of items lent, and programs are far above average while costs are far
below average. Lunenburg is getting a big bang for its buck. Volunteers help make our superior service possible. Services
are sorely strained, however, by a severe parking shortage, lack of accessibility, antiquated wiring, and no quiet study or
private meeting area. We are on the waiting list for a $1,782,563.00 grant (about 45% of the cost) from the Massachusetts
Board of Library Commissioners and State legislature to build a facility to meet the needs of a community and world much
changed since the current Ritter was built at the turn of the last century.
Ritter is now a member of C/W MARS, the Central and Western Massachusetts Automated Resource Sharing network. Our
catalog is online at cwmars.org. Our library card is a universal card for virtually all libraries in central and western
Massachusetts. Online searching and fast interlibrary delivery makes the resources in all the libraries easily available. Full
text magazine articles and other types of high cost, high quality information in the fields of academic research, medicine,
business, and more are now available at Ritter in a collection of databases which are purchased through the network and
State library agencies. Our tiny library is a technology-rich research facility far surpassing the home Internet.

FINANCIAL ANALYSIS COMMITTEE
The Financial Analysis Committee consists of representatives from the Board of Selectmen, School Committee, Finance
Committee, Board of Assessors, Planning Board, Treasurer, Accountant and Members at Large. The Committee is an ad
hoc committee appointed by each participating committee for its representation. Members at large are appointed by the
Board of Selectmen.
The Charge to the Committee reads as follows:
Define Town's overall financial position as related to construction and/or reconstruction projects.
Financial analysis of all major construction and/or reconstruction projects and any cost related thereto, including
increased operating cost.
Overlay future projects with financial analysis to assess affordability.
Analyze or recommend timing from a financial perspective.
Provide tax payers/voters with the Financial Analyses as listed above.
The committee met through the year to discuss, analyze and provide information concerning the Impact on the financial
structure and the tax rate for a Capital Building plan, long-range school and other Town building renovations. The
renovation needs were based on the State of Town Facilities Five-Year Assessment report. The Committee also reviewed
other capital expenditures, particularly the proposed Primary School. A complete presentation was given by the school
representatives, including the Superintendent of Schools and architect.
The Committee, recognizing the need for capital expenditures, supported the funding for the school, noting the
reimbursement schedule. The Committee's continued discussions also recognized the need for other facilities, including
renovations, but cautioned, in their deliberations, that the other large facility expenditures require a time line in order to be
absorbed in the tax rate. It was proposed that some renovations could be, in limited amounts, accomplished in the yearly
FY Capital plans.
BOARD OF HEALTH
The year that passed was a relatively quiet one for the Board of Health with no major incidents.
With the signing of a contract for the installation of the first phase of the public sewer system in the Whalom area, we were
able to provide waivers from some of the provisions of the Title V septic system regulations for a number of the properties
along the route saving our citizens the significant expense of replacement costs.
Reports of the northerly progress of the West Nile Virus demanded our attention and the Board responded to several
reports of dead birds in Lunenburg. These were forwarded to the Massachusetts Department of Public Health laboratory
for analysis. Fortunately, the birds tested proved to be negative and as of this writing the virus has not been discovered
here in Lunenburg.
Several changes in State regulations have resulted in yet more responsibilities for local Boards of Health. The frequency of
required water tests for swimming areas open to the public have doubled. Additionally, the explosion of the Beaver
population that resulted from an initiative petition law passed several years ago, caused a modification to the law which
allows for the extermination of these rodents under certain circumstances. Responsibility for both of these new
regulations was placed upon the local boards. These have required a significant increase in field time in gathering water
samples and investigating problem beavers as well as the increased paperwork load.
Each of these is unfounded mandates (which are supposedly forbidden by law) from the State to the local boards and
further strains our already meager resources. However, each of them is important, and we have managed to absorb them
into our operation.
Through our affiliation with the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health, we continue to provide various health and
immunization clinics for our citizens throughout the year. Schedules for these clinics are regularly advertised in the local
newspapers or can be obtained from our office. Additionally, dental checkups and fluoride treatment for our children
continues at all of our schools.

The Lunenburg Planning Board continues to work with development through Master Planning, Statutory requirements of
MGL, Chapter 40A and 41, local Zoning Bylaw and Rules and Regulations Governing the Subdivision of Land. The Board
continues to develop policies, which assists the development process.

Our Master Planning is a revolving document. The Facility Element of the Plan has been a major concern; however, other
aspects of the plan have been on the planning table. Our Town is still wrestling with housing. Development continues to
take place with larger homes being built in greater numbers than the smaller units. Growth management is still in effect
through Zoning Bylaw, Section 4.11. This vehicle ensures a maximum number of permits allowed in one year; however,
there are other aspects that augment the numbers such as the grandfathering factor. Any growth will affect our
infrastructure, increase operating budgets and increase demand for adequate housing. The Community has to deal with
reality by addressing these needs.
The Board again impresses upon the Community the need for smaller units, particularly for the young, empty nesters and
the elderly. There is a portion of our Community that needs, or wants, to downscale without being forced to leave
Lunenburg. The State emphasis on affordable housing is moving in the direction of the smaller communities. This
mandate and need could leave Lunenburg with the type of development that does not meet our goals. The goals and
policies in our Master Plan direct that we preserve the essence of Lunenburg. Therefore, as a Community, we need to plan
creatively. This means that the Community, through its Planning Board and other pertinent boards and committees, must
plan to determine our own destiny. In that respect, the Planning Board is looking at methodologies for preservation of our
historic structures and implementing zoning for the types of enterprise that balance development growth. In this area, the
Board is working on a demolition delay procedure, overlay districts in the Whalom Park area and support policies for
Zoning Board of Appeals in any 40B project.
The commercial development direction is a concern for the Planning Board. Our Route 2A commercial corridor has come
under criticism. The two major shopping malls present well planned projects; however, the perception is that the corridor
is not balanced in the type of commercial enterprises. This can detract from the area as a full potential commercial
corridor. The Town needs to maintain the existing permits through enforcement aids to assist in attracting other
enterprises and help the existing businesses.
The industrial aspect of development has the ability to increase. With a sewer line construction in Pioneer Park, new
development, or additions to the present development, will be forthcoming. A new industrial plant, Oldcastle Precast, at
the Keating property, is under review at the time of this writing. This review involves the Planning Board, other land use
committees and professional input. The review is being conducted thoroughly to insure that all compliances are met. The
review includes open communication as it relates to sensitivity for the residential area.
Telecommunication tower construction is a difficult development problem for the Town. While the new technology industry
has needs and is under Federal auspices, the Board works toward siting to protect the residential area. A study is being
conducted to identify Town-owned land and its relation to telecommunication reception. The end result of the study is to
determine an overlay district for tower placement.
A public hearing will be held before Town Meeting on the revised Rules and Regulation Governing the Subdivision of Land.
This revision has been in the works for two years. The basis for the update was to streamline the document and meet all
new statutory requirements. The process included a complete review by code experts and engineering firms. Some of the
work was done as gratis review. The document includes a well-defined Fee System and Simplified Permitting and policies
such as Criteria for Common Driveways. All Federal and State mandates are listed by reference to the appropriate
documents, which will be available in the Planning Office.
Current projects are in various stages of review. Robbs Hill Road development is completed-awaiting spring final review.
Robbs Terrace is in construction. Maplewood Estates is awaiting spring final review. The Village at Flat Hill is in
construction as is White's Woods Subdivision. Phases 2 and 3 of White's Woods have not yet been submitted.

The Post Office is complete; all signing is in place. New Outlooks is open, but will continue site work in the spring. An

The Planning Board still works diligently under the Development Plan Review process, and the Board considers the

represent the Planning Board on other committees, such as Montachusett Regional Planning Commission, Montachusett

The Board foresees continued need for diligent attention to Town planning and active Board participation in all aspects of

TECHNOLOGY DEPARTMENT
The Technology Department for the Town of Lunenburg has been very busy on many fronts. In the following, I will
encapsulate our year to date.
Our efforts in the school lie in maintenance. Technology is relied upon heavily in the schools for a number of reasons. The
higher the grade, the more intense the need is. Administrational use is constant. This level of need and use creates many
challenges for a two-person department. The crisis is in funding and levels of technology. This push-pull relationship is a
struggle. As a Technology Department, we want to serve our departments to the best of our ability. We do not decide what
the schools should teach or how to teach it. We are the suppliers of equipment and some services. To this end, we can't
even come close to the demand. In a recent article in Curriculum Administrator, eight out of ten teachers are moderate to
serious computer users. They cited three problems with technology in public schools: working conditions
(chair/desk/lighting), external groups making constant demands, and unreliable technology. The last reason is
bothersome. Our current replacement plan does not meet any minimums. We are strictly on a band-aid mission. We are
understanding of other's needs, but technology is like your car, when it is working fine, you don't think about it and when
you break down, the world stops. If you don't replace parts, or get a new car, your chances for failure greatly increase.
The industry standard for computer replacement is three years. One Computer Advisory member said that his company
replaced computers every year. To the school systems credit, we are tapping every available asset for use. But as the
years go by, we are falling more behind the times.
Other work in the schools lies in networking. The backbone of technology is the network. A solid network is the
foundation of great things. We have been working on this area. We have installed a great system connecting the main
campus. Since the interconnection phase, we have been working on the Internet and Email for all faculty. We are making
strides in these areas.
The Town this year has embarked on several major projects. The first is the GIS project in conjunction with the Assessor's
Department. This could touch every department to varying degrees. A grant has been sought for GIS use in the schools as
well. This is Phase I of a three-phase project. Second is the Police-Fire Migration. They were on an old antiquated system.
This is Phase III of a three-phase project. Third is the Accounting System. Our present accounting system was shelved.
Receiving substantial savings to migrate to a new product, we have begun an exhaustive conversion. This conversion is a
yearlong process that affects the very core of the town's operations. Fourth was the Ritter Memorial Library's technology
update. The Technology Department had a minor role in this conversion as the volunteers and employees rallied together.
Many other projects saw small gains, such as the town's official website www.lunenburgonline.com as we hope this will be
an epicenter of information and contacts for the citizens of Lunenburg. Email for school personnel being largely underway,
the town employees, departments, and committees will soon have email to complete the connectivity of the town. Email
defaults are first initial and last name @lunenburgonline.com, except for some special circumstances. I thank all for their
patience and perseverance.
Next I would like to talk about technology's advisory committees. The Computer Advisory Committee has taken the duties
of long range strategic planning. They have continued prior work with the Technology Plan. In conjunction, I have
challenged each school to form a technology committee that will conduct its own long range planning. Although this
process does not have a finish line, the evolving documents will be merged into Lunenburg's Technology Plan. This is
very important for two reasons. First, we should have a clear path for all to travel. Results are better when the goals match
the clearly stated vision. Secondly, the base criteria for grants and funding opportunities Is a valid technology plan.
Lastly, I want all to know that Lunenburg has a broad range of technology. Your technology staff works very hard for you.
We have established priorities in each department as well as overall. For two people to conduct these services is a lot to
ask. The question is what can be funded, what can be serviced, and what can two people do for you? We have responded
with projects moving, computers being serviced, and critical needs being met. I'm proud of the accomplishments made
considering the manpower, budget, and training.
At this time, I would like to give two thumbs up to: Beth Caron (Technology), Officer George Aho and Firefighter Scott
Dillon, Lisa Wanamaker (Town Clerk's office), Norma Frost (Assessor's), Tammy York (Teacher), Jim Besarkarski (Teacher),
Technology Independent Study Students, and the Computer Advisory Committee.

SUPERINTENDENT'S MESSAGE
Richard Mr. Carlson, Ed. D., Superintendent of Schools
It is an honor, as Superintendent, to submit my ninth annual report regarding the Lunenburg Public Schools as well as to
restate the mission statement that is the heart of our school community. "To develop responsible, self-motivated students
through a learning environment that is exciting, challenging, and innovative." We, in the Lunenburg Public Schools, do not
take this mission lightly nor do we attribute the success of our schools to any one individual. Each member of our staff,
along with the community at large, contributes significantly in helping our students and schools realize their goals. We are
more than a team; we are a family who recognizes and embraces change, celebrates success, and most importantly, helps
each other meet challenges and weather bad times. I am proud of the Lunenburg Public Schools, and even more proud, to
be a member of its family.
Our schools opened on August 28, 2001 smoothly but without some long-time, well-known and respected staff members.
While retirees Anthony Falcone, Richard Kullman, Robert Saudelli, and Miriam Light will be missed, our administrative team
began their quest to find new staff early and once again this year our good fortunate prevailed with the hiring of some first-
rate people. In addition to the appointment of some top-notch teachers, tutors, and support staff, Ms. Suzanne Mahoney
joined our school community in November as T.C. Passios Elementary School's Assistant Principal. She is a seasoned
educator who brings to Lunenburg a depth of knowledge and experience. With her vast pedagogical background and
maturity Ms. Mahoney was able to jump right into her role with ease and has already made her mark in the community as a
contributor to the Lunenburg Ledger.
The School Committee welcomed some new members too. Dr. Gregory Berthiaume and Ms. Milree Keeling came on board
in May replacing veteran members Marcella Collier and Diane DelliCarpini. They, along with experienced members
Seymour Schnuer, Ernest Hunter, and Peter Duynstee, were immediately met with the challenge of addressing some
complex agenda topics Including a moratorium on travel, weighted grades, school nutrition, and school start times, as well
assuming a vital role in developing a FY03 budget that meets the needs of our students within very limited financial
parameters.
New staff and school committee members quickly realized the caliber of the compassionate team they had joined when we
were faced with the September 11, 2001 tragedy. We watched the strikes on and collapse of the World Trade Towers, the
attack on the Pentagon, and a crash site in Pennsylvania over and over again in disbelief. This devastating event not only
deeply saddened us as Americans, but also pierced the hearts of our small school community as we witnessed first hand
the pain of one of our staff members, Shella Szocik, who lost her son Kevin. Kevin will be remembered as one of
Lunenburg High School's talented alumni. True to form, our staff gathered to show their support. Further support was
displayed when our school community along with the School Committee, Town Selectmen, and other town departments
united for an unprecedented vigil of hope.
As school communities are microcosms of society, each year we mourn the loss of students, alumni, and present and past
employees. It is an extremely difficult reality, especially when the loss involves one of our young people. I have avoided
using this medium to remember or eulogize those who have passed, as it has been my objective to keep this message
upbeat. This year I have decided to make an exception in remembering an extraordinary educator. Philip Crouse died on
September 15, 2001-a few short days following the September 11 tragedy. He touched the lives of many students as a
teacher and an administrator at Lunenburg High School for more than thirty years. Mr. Crouse also served as Chairman of
the Lunenburg Finance Committee. Following his retirement in 1996 Mr. Crouse continued to answer my calls for help
whenever and wherever I needed him to fill in as an interim administrator. He exemplified the work ethic he demanded from
his students. I am grateful for Philip Crouse's commitment to the Lunenburg Public Schools-he will be missed.
The September 11, 2001 tragedy brought calls from concerned parents about safety in our schools. While no school
system can guarantee safety, I do want to assure parents that we take security seriously. Over the past several years we
have done much work reviewing safety procedures and the security of our buildings. I, along with the administrative team,
have made many changes in both these areas including establishing contingency plans in the event we must evacuate
students. Review of safety/security is ongoing. We ask that parents assist us in our security efforts by reporting to the
office when entering a building and sharing with us any security concerns. In fact, safety concerns raised by parents have
precipitated several procedural changes.
Ironically, while watching the rubble of the collapsed towers being removed in New York City, Lunenburg was building on
the future by moving forward with their school project. In December the town voted in favor of constructing a new Primary
School that will house students in pre-kindergarten through grade two. Architect Margo Jones, of Margo Jones Architects,
has designed a two-story building, which will accommodate students born in the new century. This is an exciting project
for the school department as well as for the town. Looking to the future is always important, but more so in these difficult
times.

We were pleased to learn that we are ranked sixty-ninth in the state which is a considerable improvement over last year's

HIGH SCHOOL
Michael G. Barney, Principal
Lunenburg High School is particularly proud of two major accomplishments this year. After submitting our one-year report
to the New England Association of Schools and Colleges, we continue to be fully accredited with the Association. This
process, which takes place every ten years, includes a two-year self-study, public report and an evaluation from a visiting
team of professional educators. Our faculty worked extremely hard to implement recommended changes and will continue
to improve in all areas to better serve our community and its most precious resource, the children of Lunenburg.
The Class of 2003 also had a tremendous showing in the first round of MCAS testing which is a state requirement for
achieving a diploma. Almost 99% of our students passed the mark in English Language Arts and 95% passed the
Mathematics portion of the test on the first try, This is a tribute to the hard work of the students as well as the faculty and
curriculum teams who continually review, improve, and align our curriculum to the state frameworks.
The High School continued several traditions including our annual Homecoming Parade and 37th annual Competitive Class
Plays, as well as beginning a new one, the Alumni Tallgate before the Homecoming and Thanksgiving games. We invite all
LHS Alumni back home to all our events.
Academically, we piloted a new Interdisciplinary course this year called "Inventions", in which students research an
invention of the past, present its historical significance and place in time, then design and build a prototype. It was an
exciting first year and a big hit with the students who took the course. We also sponsored our first "Career Day", when
forty-five area professionals generously shared their time and expertise with our entire student body. In small groups, they
talked about career preparation, education and practical experience of the real world. This day was a tremendous success
and we would like to again publicly thank all the participants. We also placed several of our students into internship
opportunities this year, expanding their education beyond the walls of the high school.
Our athletic programs have done well this year. Our Field Hockey team was Mid-Wach C league champions and division 2
district finalists. Our girls' winter track and girls' basketball teams were both Mid-Wach C league champions, and all our
winter sports teams have qualified for district play. My particular congratulations to Coach Stachowicz, completing his 39th
year of coaching at LHS, and Coach Lamothe for his induction Into the Massachusetts Baseball Hall of Fame.
Through the generosity of the Chester W. Mossman trust, our greenhouse has been rebuilt, along with a new potting shed,
which will enhance and support our excellent horticulture program. Lunenburg High School would also like to recognize
the Herculean efforts of the Lunenburg Track and Field Association, their board, and scores of volunteers whose dream will
soon become reality. Their efforts are truly appreciated and will touch several generations of young people in Lunenburg.
We certainly appreciate all they do.
Several of our students realized dreams individually, including winning awards in the prestigious Boston Globe Scholastic
Art Show, placement in the district and state band and chorus, breaking several records in track and field (school and meet
records), conversing with Sir Arthur Clarke via satellite in Sri Lanka from WPI and the achievement of a full athletic
scholarship to Boston University.
It has been a year of tragedy and triumph, and we look forward to the future with continued optimism, hope and
determination.

The 2001-2002 school year at Turkey Hill Middle School had a number of important highlights and significant accomplishments.
MCAS scores in all grade levels improved dramatically. Organizations and activities which challenged students of varied
abilities were initiated or expanded. The School Improvement Plan, designed by parents, teachers and administrators, continued
to serve as a vehicle to enhance the academic program and to meet the personal needs of students. The focus to enhance the
physical plant continued to make the school a more attractive environment and thus increase the pride our students have in their
school. Lastly our students engaged in a variety of altruistic activities which were designed to serve others and to teach
valuable lessons. We are very proud of the students of Turkey Hill Middle School.

Last year, for the first time, all students took one or more MCAS tests. Fifth graders took both science and history, however,
because it was a practice test, scores were not reported. Our sixth graders took the test in mathematics. The math test remains
the hardest of all the MCAS tests. That being said, 50% of our students scored in the advanced or proficient category and only
20% received a warning. The data with which we compare these results is the state performance because this was the first year
the sixth graders took the test. At the state level a total of 36% scored in the advanced or proficient category and 33% received a
warning. This puts us 14% higher in the top two categories and 13% lower in the warning category.
Our seventh graders took the English/Language Arts test, and this also was a first time test for them. 74% of our students
ranked in the advanced or proficient category compared to 55% at the state level. Only 2% of our students received a warning
compared to 12% of students across the state.
Our eighth graders also did quite well. In ELA, 78% of our students ranked in the advanced or proficient categories; this
compares to 67% at the state level. Only 3% of our students were in the warning category compared to 8% for the state. Our
total ELA average was up 4 points, and this represents our best score everl
In 8th grade math 46% of our students received an advanced or proficient score compared to 34% at the state level. 14% of our
students received a warning compared to 31% at the state level. Our average score was up 6 points, and this again represents
our highest score ever attained!
Our 8th grade history and social studies scores were comparable to the state scores. This test remains a problem for all schools
because the Department of Education has yet to agree on the Frameworks. Our average score was higher than previous years
but no one is happy with the results. Almost all public schools feel the same way, however the problem lies with the DOE's
inability to finalize their work.
Overall our students did very well. We improved in almost all categories, and we continue to Improve our average scores in
math, ELA and history/social studies. While we expect to do better than state scores, this year we were significantly ahead of
the state in most if not all areas. We view these tests as important, but they are only one barometer of our schools success. We
continue to teach reasoning and thinking skills, the ability to make good decisions, the ability to use knowledge in an ethical and

\The Thomas C. Passios Elementary School continues to strive toward educating the total child. We utilize a variety of
instructional methods and modalities in our approach to meet the individual needs of every child. We believe that the success of
our programs is dependent upon a healthy and productive partnership between home and school. Parents play an active role in
many of the daily programs and routines of the school.
This year continues to be a challenging and exciting year for the students, faculty, and staff of the Elementary and Primary
Schools. With a combined enrollment of seven hundred students, class sizes continue in the twenty to twenty-four range, with
the younger children benefiting from the smaller class sizes.
The summer of 2001 proved to be very beneficial to many children in Lunenburg. A number of academic programs were offered
to the children: a five week program for children with special needs in grades preschool through grade 4, and a five week
program for children in Grades 1-4 who were experiencing difficulty in the areas of reading and math. Funding for the Grades 1
and 2 programs was provided through School Linked Services; State MCAS Grant monies supported the Grade 3 and 4 program.
As the Elementary School continues to analyze our students' results on the Massachusetts Comprehensive Assessment System
(MCAS) and revise curricula and strategies, we continue to grow as an excellent educational facility. We are extremely proud of
the work the faculty continues to undertake each year. As teachers continue to reflect on their teaching practice, students
continue to benefit.
Our results on the State Grade 4 MCAS Tests Indicate that in the areas of both English/Language Arts and Math, the number of
Grade 4 students performing in the "failing" category continues to decline. Children in Grade 3 were administered their first
MCAS Test last spring in Reading. We are proud to say that no child failed the Grade 3 Reading Test. Individual Success Plans
were developed on those children who scored in the lower end of the 'needs improvement' category with the intent that
additional time/teaching would make a difference.
This year the Elementary School Improvement Plan focused on the areas of reading comprehension and writing across all
curricula areas. At this point in the year, the School Council is proud that we have accomplished many of our goals. This fall we
implemented a writing assessment schedule/practice in which all students in grades kindergarten through four were asked to
write on a given topic. Students' writing was evaluated using a rubric (scoring guide). All students were assessed again in
March and again in May/June. By implementing this program, teachers and parents will be able to document individual student
writing progress and re-teach as needed.
We are pleased to report that our Reading Assessment practice has also been re-evaluated and revised. Each child in grades 1-
4 will be evaluated twice each year in reading; in October and March. New individual reading folders have been implemented to
document student progress as well as strategies for improvement.

OFFICE OF INSTRUCTION
Loxi Jo Calmes, Director
During this past year the Office of Instruction continued to focus on the three areas critical to the District's mission: (a)
curriculum development (standards), (b) assessment and evaluation (measures), and (c) continuous improvement for ALL
students. Following is a summary of activities and results in each of these areas.
Standards
Revisions to Mathematics, Science & Technology, and Integrated Health curricula are at various points of completions as we
initiate the second generation of aligning local curricula to state standards. Teams of teachers with representatives from each
grade level reviewed the newly adopted state frameworks and were involved in the process of rewriting local curriculum. Before
this school year ends we will initiate a review of the English/Language Arts curriculum.
Annually, faculties in all the schools are involved in reviewing MCAS results for each curricula area. As a result of these
reviews at various levels of the organization (grade/department, school, and district) we continue to identify strengths and
weaknesses within our curriculum and instructional practices. We have implemented a local assessment system to annually
monitor student progress in mathematics.
We continue our commitment to a unified educational system for ALL students that values diversity and avoids pitting one group
against another. A review of MCAS results Indicates that like their non-disabled peers, students with disabilities in Lunenburg
perform above the state average in English/Language Arts. Performance in mathematics, however, is weak and we have identified a
series of professional development activities aimed at improving these results.
Measures - MCAS Results
In November the District received the results from the fourth administration of the Massachusetts Comprehensive
Assessment System (MCAS). Student performance on MCAS is reported according to the following four levels with the
exception of the 3ʳᵈ grade reading tests that only reports 3 categories of performance as indicated with an asterisk (*): Advanced
- Students demonstrate a comprehensive and in-depth understanding of rigorous subject matter and provide sophisticated
solutions to complex problems. *Proficient - Students demonstrate a solid understanding of challenging subject matter and
solve a wide variety of problems. *Needs Improvement - Students demonstrate partial understanding of subject matter and
solve some simple problems. *Warning - Students demonstrate minimal understanding of subject matter and do not solve
even simple problems.

proficient range in reading. In grade four 59% of the students perform in the advanced or proficient range in English/Language

BOARD OF ASSESSORS
The Board of Assessors and its staff are responsible for the valuation of all real estate and personal property subject to taxation.
Assessed valuations are based on "full and fair cash value" as of the January 1st preceding each fiscal year.
Our office requires a substantial amount of information in order to carry out its valuation mandate. Much of the office's effort
centers around maintaining ownership records, tax maps, and property characteristic data. This data is constantly changing.
Fiscal Year 2001 saw a significant increase in new building site approvals, home construction, and improvements to the existing
housing stock. The resulting increase in inspection activity, time spent in setting up new records, maintaining existing data, and
dealing with all of the political and economic implications brought on by growth kept the office unusually busy.
This year saw the office migrate to the new CSC-CAMA system. This windows based assessment program provides substantial
improvements over our old system. For example, property photos can now be captured digitally and electronically pasted onto
our assessment records. The new database upon which the program is based is much more flexible and allows for easier
querying and reporting. Another advantage of the new system is that it can be integrated with our Geographic Information
System (GIS).
In an effort to enhance the Town's Geographic Information System (GIS), the office went out to bid to have new aerial
photography and digital orthophotography prepared. The contract was awarded to Kucera International, Inc. of Willoughby,
Ohio. The digital orthography will serve as the foundation for a complete re-map of Lunenburg.
Our department welcomes Fred Liatsis to the Board of Assessors. He replaces outgoing board member, Ernie Sund who served
the office well over the past twelve years. We are pleased to report no staff changes the same crew of Norma Frost as
Administrative Assessor, Dorothy Musgrove as Principal Clerk, Janet Leavitt as inspector, and Harald Scheld as Assessor carry
out the Board's directives. Collectively they bring approximately 55 years of experience to the office.
Finally, it should be noted that in December of 2001, the Townsend Board of Assessors voted to pull out of the Tri-town
agreement with Ashby and Lunenburg. Having been a successful program initiated by outgoing Lunenburg Assessor Ernie
Sund in 1994, the Townsend Board of Assessors now believes that its Town's needs warrant hiring its own professional
assessor.
We thank the Town for its support of our office and the efforts we have made to insure that tax revenues flow to the Town in a
consistent and timely manner.
