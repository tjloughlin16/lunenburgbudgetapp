IN MEMORIAM  
 
 
Sarah M. Grant passed away on February 4, 2022. She had lived in Lunenburg since 
2012 and been a member of the Council on Aging from 2013 to 2022.  
Sarah founded one of the Council on Aging’s annual events, the Tea Party, which was a very 
popular event. She was very focused on all the little details that go into a formal tea party. 
Sarah also led craft classes at the Senior Center as she was very talented. She will be missed.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
William J. Gustus passed away in his home on September 19, 2022, surrounded by 
his loving family. Bill owned and operated Settlers Crossing Golf Course in Lunenburg 
and the Westminster Golf & Country Club in Westminster with his wife Laura Caron-
Gustus and business partner Donnie Lyons. Prior to his retirement he was the Town 
Administrator for the Town of Lynnfield, and the Chief Administrative and Financial 
Officer for the Town of Lunenburg.  
 
 
 
 
Longtime dance and physical education teacher, Genevieve C. Nass, 67, died 
Nov. 29, 2022.  Genevieve was born on Sept. 30, 1955, attended Lunenburg 
schools and graduated from Lunenburg High School in 1973.  
Known for the twinkle in her eye and her big smile, she ran her own dance studio 
for more than 40 years and taught physical education for 20 years in the 
Lunenburg Public Schools.  
She looked forward to summers at the Town Beach and also directed the 
Lunenburg Town Beach for many years, acting as a lifeguard, swimming 
instructor and lifeguard trainer.

TOTAL AREA– 26.63 MILES 
Lunenburg was settled in 1718 and incorporated in 1728. It occupies a land area of approximately 26.4 
square miles. Lunenburg is located in north central Massachusetts  approximately  43 miles from  
Boston,  It is bordered on the west by the Town of Ashby and the City of Fitchburg, on the south by the 
Town of Lancaster and the City of Leominster, on the east by the Town of Shirley and on the north by 
the Town of Townsend. 
 
Lunenburg is a semi-rural community located in northern Worcester County.  Lake Whalom and the 
former Whalom Amusement Park delineated the Whalom District from the remainder of Lunenburg. This 
district was developed as a housing base for many factory workers working in the mills and factories of 
Leominster and Fitchburg. In many ways the characteristics of the Whalom District closely reflect 
characteristics of these cities more than of the rest of Lunenburg. The Hickory Hills Lake area was 
developed as a private community comprised largely of vacation cottages. Shirley Reservoir is a public 
water body also developed with mainly vacation cottages that have now been modified to year-round 
residences.

REPORT OF THE TOWN MANAGER 
FY22 
It is my pleasure to submit the annual report on the activities of the Office of the Town Manager during 
Fiscal Year 2022. Fiscal Year 2022 started off like most other years however, there were lingering after 
effects of the pandemic. The pandemic brought about a shift in the employment marketplace, causing 
vacancies to last longer and a smaller applicant pool to fill positions. The State extended the allowance of 
virtual public meetings, which most boards and committees continued to use as a matter of convenience and, 
at times, for personal health reasons. The federal stimulus funds that were part of the American Rescue Plan 
Act made their way down to cities and towns and this would result in many discussions in how best to use 
these windfall monies. Driver shortages, supply chain issues, and increased demand for items disrupted our 
Pay As You Throw program, severely impacted the delivery schedule of vehicles/equipment the Department 
of Public Works, Fire Department, and Police Department ordered and spiked costs for construction projects.  
Despite the ripple effects of the pandemic, the Town continued to provide a high level of services and was 
another year of progress. Below I would like to highlight the topics that were addressed and the 
accomplishments within the Office of the Town Manager: 
 In July of 2021, as part of the annual goal setting process, the Selectmen reviewed and voted to accept the 
Town Manager’s annual goals for FY22. The Town Manager’s goals for FY22 included continuing to 
take the necessary steps towards the redevelopment/reuse of 925 Massachusetts Avenue, working with 
the Select Board on creating a means tested senior citizen tax relief program, and action items related to 
enhancing facilities maintenance and management.  A majority of the action items related to these three 
goals were completed.  In regards to 925 Massachusetts Avenue, the Town Manager continued to work 
Joe McLaughlin of BETA Group, MassDEP, and MassDevelopment about possible cleanup funding. In 
the end we worked with the EPA and this project was put on the list of projects in line for federal funding 
through Targeted Brownfields Assessment funding. The funding would pay for removal of the 
underground tanks, limited soil removal and make the Town eligible for future cleanup funds. This work 
is slated to be completed in FY 2023. In addition, a survey was completed for the property and after 
much due diligence to eliminate any possibility that the Town would be legally responsible for cleanup, 
the Town foreclosed on the property, which was a necessary step to receive the federal funding from the 
EPA.As it related to one of the action items under the Town Manager’s annual goals for Facilities, the 
Town Manager followed the Town Charter process for an Administrative Organization Plan and after a 
public hearing process, received approval at the May 2022 Annual Town Meeting to separate Facilities 
from the Department of Public Works. The third goal, to assist the Select Board with a means tested 
senior citizen tax relief program was initiated and the Principal Assessor presented information from 
other communities with similar programs to the working group. Ultimately additional time was needed to 
develop the framework of the program and its effects on taxpayers, so this goal was carried forward to 
FY 2023. 
 In July 2021, the Select Board set their goals for the upcoming year: they amended their FY21 goal/work 
on the old Primary School at 30 School Street with additional action items such as engaging with a 
landscape architecture firm to discuss a vision of multi-use, passive recreation/ green space project at the 
site and all necessary steps to bring the associated costs to the 2022 Annual Town Meeting. Another goal 
was to work with the Town Manager and Finance Director to establish a means-tested senior citizen 
property tax exemption or abatement. The last goal was to develop a policy designed to encourage 
volunteerism in Town, specifically for town boards, committees, and commissions. A Request for 
Proposals was put out twice for selection of a landscape architect for 30 School Street and ultimately 
architect Ray Dunetz was chosen for the project. Various staff and community meetings led to a 
conceptual design that requested additional funding at the May 2022 Annual Town Meeting, but funding 
was only approved for abatement and demolition of the structure and the associated engineering costs. 
The goal of developing a policy designed to encourage volunteerism was discussed at a workshop but the   
Board was split on the language of the policy so no action was taken during fiscal year 2022 on this goal. 
 At the end of Fiscal Year 2021 the Town received a letter from Mass Housing  that Pond View 
Commons, LLC had filed an application for a 40B housing project. The proposed development would 
consist of 200 units of rental housing on 18.5 acres of land located at 318, 390 & 400 Howard Street.

 August 2022 began with a workshop on Chapter 40B led by Town Counsel Adam Costa on the role of the 
Zoning Board of Appeals would provide in the application process. The Town was required to submit a 
response to the letter from Mass Housing by September 2, 2021, which an 88-page response was 
submitted that included numerous letters from residents, town boards, town counsel, and the Town 
Manager. Mass Housing reviewed the town’s response, requested information from the applicant and 
ultimately granted a positive 40B project eligibility in March of 2022. From that point on, the applicant 
filed a comprehensive permit application with the Zoning Board of Appeals. The application is still 
ongoing as of writing this report.  
 The Town Manager submitted recommendations to the Select Board on adoption or amendment of 
various policies throughout the year, including the Capital Budget Policy changing the threshold for 
individual projects from $10,000 to $20,000, a new Senior Citizen Property Work Off Program Policy 
which would change to an internal function of reviewing the applications by the Assistant Town 
Manager versus a committee, amendments to the Liquor License Regulations, the Boot Drive Policy, 
MA Emergency Paid Sick Leave, and Sign Policy. In April the Board finalized a new Commemorative 
Policy and began working on their third goal of a Volunteerism Policy. In May a new Tailings Policy, 
which addresses uncashed checks issued by the Town, was adopted and in June a new Ambulance 
Billing Collection Policy was adopted. 
 As previously mentioned, Fiscal Year 2022 was a difficult year for retaining staff and filling vacancies. 
The pandemic created opportunities for employees to move to new careers and made it a more competitive 
marketplace for employers. The Assistant Town Manager/HR Director who is responsible for advertising 
vacancies and working with the relevant departments to bring on new employees was constantly reviewing 
applications, interviewing and trying to fill vacancies throughout the year. In August 2021 the Veterans 
Agent, IT Director (shared between Town/School), and a Board Clerk resigned. The Veterans Agent 
position was filled in September, the Town Manager hired an outside consultant in September to fill the 
role of the IT Director, and additional Board Clerks were hired throughout the year. In September 
Principal Assessor Sue Byrne announced her retirement effective end of September and Christine Kumar 
was hired in September to fill this position, but resigned at the end of November. The position was filled 
with a temporary Assessor, Rena Swezey, from December 2021 until March of 2022 when Christopher 
Carroll was selected to fill the permanent position. In October the Principal Clerk for the Town 
Accountant retired and this position changed to an Assistant Town Accountant position after the 
November Special Town Meeting and was filled in January 2022. Also in October the Recreation Director 
Kayla Wright resigned and Rob Thompson was hired in December to fill this position. In March DPW 
Director Rob Oliva resigned and this position was unfilled until May of 2022 when Bill Bernard was 
appointed. Also in May of 2022 Conservation Administrator Matt Marro retired and Nadia Madden was 
hired in May to fill this position. The Police Department also saw staffing changes with three Reserve and 
Full-Time Officers leaving for other positions, and these were filled at different times during the year. The 
DPW had up to three vacant Heavy Equipment Operator positions at all times during the year. The 
Council on Aging and Public Access also had vacancies for Van Drivers and Videographers during the

BOARD OF ASSESSORS 
The Board of Assessors is responsible for establishing the value of property, both real and personal, for 
taxation purposes for the town. Assessed values are subject to a five-year revaluation and interim year 
adjustments for certification by the Department of Revenue.  The Assessors grant all statutory exemptions for 
Senior and Surviving Spouse, Blind and Veterans. They act on all abatement applications to property values.  
The Assessors are responsible for defending all appeals to the Appellate Tax Board. They also administer all 
special assessments qualifying farm and forest management programs. Assessments in Massachusetts are 
based on full and fair cash value as of January 1st. 
The Assessors analyze all sales from 2021 for FY 23 valuations.  Market value has seen increases in the sales 
of homes in town.  Properties have been selling over the assessed valuations reflecting an overall increase to 
market value of the town. The next town wide recertification will occur this year for FY 2024.  
Each property in town has a property record card on file with all the components that go into the valuation of 
the parcel - such as land size, building description, sales information, etc.  We recommend that the taxpayers 
review their property field card annually to ensure all the information is up to date and correct. The property 
record cards are available in our office or online through the GIS maps. 
The Department of Revenue certified the results of assessment data to be within their statistical requirements 
and the tax bills went out on time.  The tax rate went from $17.19 to $14.62 for FY 2023. 
The office continues the process of mandated cyclical inspections of all properties throughout the town.  This 
is achieved by inspections, visiting the properties, and by means of sales verification, building permits, 
occupancy permits and abatement inspections.  The Board of Assessors strives to maintain accuracy in the 
assessment to sales ratio as well as assessment uniformity. 
The office staff strives to continue to provide good service that the Lunenburg taxpayers have come to expect.

CONSERV ATION COMMISSION 
The Town of Lunenburg Conservation Commission is a seven member commission, appointed to  three year terms by 
the Select Board, whose duties are codified under the Conservation Commission Act MGL c. 40, § 8C. The 
Commission enforces the Massachusetts Wetland Protection Act MGL c. 131, § 40 and Chapter 239 of the local bylaws 
for the protection of wetlands.  The Commission also manages and controls town-owned lands retained for conservation 
and passive recreation use. 
The year 2022 was as last year, one of a very busy and active agenda containing both public hearings and meetings for a 
wide range of projects from commercial development, subdivisions and small single family upkeep projects and larger 
scale industrial/commercial proposals. 
The Commission held numerous inspections with the commission administrator on weekends prior to meetings to 
familiarize themselves with the upcoming proposals that would appear on their agenda. The Commission continues to 
work with the Town on familiarizing itself with the continually changing storm water mandates from the EPA and 
working towards compliance both by procedure and with any necessary codification as well as aiding with the 
community implementations the “MS4” regulations. 
The Conservation Commission continues to implement forest stewardship plans developed using grants as outlined in 
the last two annual reports. The upcoming management efforts will be concentrated on the Rayno property on 
Northfield Road and the Hawes property on Hollis Road. The stewardship plan for the Hollis Road property is oriented 
toward enhancing bird wildlife habitat in conjunction with the Mass Audubon Society as part of the DCR "Forestry for 
the Birds" program.  The forest stewardship plans which provide environmental management including timber-stand 
management were developed by Gary Gouldrup of New England Forestry Consultants. The Commission continues to 
hold public forums on the implementation of its open space policies and timber-stand management. The funding from 
the sale of harvested timber provides the seed money to make it possible for the Commission to apply for grants to 
maintain and improve Lunenburg’s conservation lands.  
The Commission continues to implement a Recreational Trails Program grant providing color-coded trail markers and 
coordinated “You Are Here” trail map signs to expand the safe use of Lunenburg’s existing trail network.  Regional 
trail system map signs show how trails are interconnected among multiple properties. The project also provides 
informational kiosks and emergency access gates.  During 2022 trail maps were developed for and continue to be 
refined in detail for eleven conservation properties.  These trail maps are posted on the new Conservation Commission 
Trails website, on informational kiosks on the properties, and on “You Are Here” trail map signs at trail heads and trail 
intersections. The Commission would like to express our gratitude for all the hard-working volunteers that work so 
enthusiastically in maintaining and improving Lunenburg’s conservation lands. The Lunenburg Snow Riders continue 
to support the maintenance and improvement of the trail system on Lunenburg’s conservation lands.  The club replaced 
a bridge on the Cowdrey Conservation Area.  The club was awarded a Recreational Trails Program grant that will erect 
five new trail head kiosks, improve the cart paths on the Lane Conservation Area, level and de-stump Small Town 
Forest trails and gravel the parking lots at the Cowdrey Conservation Area.  The Conservation Commission will fund 
the material for this work using Timber Rights Funds.  
The Commission has continued to work closely with Lake Shirley and Hickory Hills on the implementations of their 
lake management plans.  These plans set goals and action items designed to give a better picture of the overall health of 
the lakes and define various strategies to ensure continued protection of these valuable resources. 
During the year of 2022, change of administration occurred along with an additional staff member. In the month of May 
Conservation Administrator Matt Marro retired after many years of service to the Conservation Commission and Town 
of Lunenburg. After his departure, Nadia Madden had taken his place as Conservation Administrator. The town 
additionally hired a Conservation Administrative Assistant, Rebecca Manley, who has been a great help for the 
Conservation Administrator. In November 2022, Anna Petrie was hired as Conservation Administrator after Nadia’s 
departure. 
The Commission thanks all the town agencies for a great year and looks forward to many more!

DEPARTMENT OF PUBLIC WORKS 
 
The Department of Public Works is the resources of the Highway, Cemetery, Sewer, Vehicle Maintenance, Facilities, 
and Parks divisions combined to maintain roads, bridges, sewer infrastructure, Town buildings, and parks and to provide 
services essential to the welfare and acceptable quality of life for the citizens of Lunenburg. 
Fiscal Year 2022 was transitional year for the DPW with the Director, Robert Oliva, two Heavy Equipment Operators, 
and the Recreation Director leaving for other employment opportunities and the hiring of a new DPW Director, William 
Bernard.  Major changes continued in early Fiscal Year 2023 with the establishment of a new Facilities Department and 
the removal of the Facilities and Parks Divisions from under the umbrella of the DPW.  Despite the upheaval, the DPW 
accomplished much work while providing excellent services.  One mainstay throughout all was the DPW Executive 
Assistant, Samantha Lenox, who provided business and professional support to all staff. 
Departmental Mission: 
The mission of the Department of Public Works is to provide high quality, cost effective, efficient, and safe services to 
protect, preserve and improve the Town’s roadway infrastructure and related assets in a manner which meets and 
enhances the current and future social and economic needs of the community; to contribute to a healthy, safe and quality 
environment for the Town’s citizens and visitors; and to provide environmentally sound means of solid waste and 
wastewater disposal.    
Departmental Goals and Objectives 
 Be prepared to respond to any weather, roadway infrastructure, or accident-related emergency that may arise on a 
twenty-four hour per day, three hundred and sixty-five day per year basis. 
 Maintain and enhance a highly qualified and dedicated staff of public works professionals. 
 Provide preventative maintenance in a proactive manner to reduce reactive maintenance as follows: 
 Maintain and update the sewer system and associated pumping stations. 
 Maintain and repair roadways in accordance with the new (2022) pavement management plan. 
 Maintain and repair sidewalks in accordance with the 2020 PROW ADA Transition plan. 
 Increase the number of roadsides mowed by developing a roadside maintenance plan. 
 Increase the number of catch basin and stormwater structures cleaned and the number of miles of roadways swept 
per the June 2020 Operations and Maintenance Plan prepared by Comprehensive Environmental, Inc. 
 Establish a comprehensive evaluation of bridges and culverts, and prioritize by condition, use, and plans for 
rehabilitation. 
 Increase training offered by our insurance provider to obtain credits to lessen premiums.   
 Identify new sources (Federal, State, and other grants) of funding to supplement the operational budget. 
 Continue to maintain a high level of courteous communication and productive interaction between members of 
boards and commissions as well as Town residents. 
 Strive to modernize the operations of the Department by obtaining and using the most up to date equipment and 
methods, including asset management.

Pavement Management  
The Town first developed a Pavement Management Plan in 2009.  This plan was updated in 2015 and defined a yearly 
paving plan through Fiscal Year 2023 and included a $4,000,000 voter authorized debt exclusion.  The goal of the plan 
was to achieve an overall town-wide Road Surface Rating of 72-75 out of 100.  In July of 2022 the DPW contracted with 
BETA Group, Inc. to have them provide a new pavement management plan as the current plan was ending and the debt 
exclusion funding had been exhausted.  Although the work was completed in Fiscal Year 2023 and not Fiscal Year 2022, 
it is worth revealing that BETA Group surveyed our roads using LIDAR and reported an overall Road Surface Rating of 
84.75.  The Project Manager for BETA Group commented “it is one of the highest scores I’ve seen in New England over 
the past 12 years that I’ve been doing this”.  The DPW is proud to have successfully administered the plan and 
anticipates carrying out the newly developed plan in a similar manner.

Storm Water 
Staying in compliance with our EPA NPDES MS4 Permit continues to remain an important function of the DPW.  Along 
with the Storm Water Task Force, Land Use Director, and our Storm Water Consultant, the DPW works to meet the 
obligations of the permit and required reporting each year.  The DPW provides maintenance and care of the Town’s 
storm water management systems.  This includes, but is not limited to catch basin cleaning, street sweeping, pipe jetting, 
replacing pipes, and swale cleaning.  The Department responds to calls for clogged catch basins and culverts to help 
control ponding and flooding. 
Tree Removal   
A Tree Risk Management Inventory of almost 1,200 trees on the roads in Lunenburg was completed in 2015.  The 
inventory prioritizes the removal and/or pruning of the trees.  However, storm damage and natural attrition creates a 
backlog of tree work that exceeds the Department’s allotted budget for tree removal.  The Department strives to fulfill 
each resident’s request for tree work but must prioritize work based upon the determined degree of danger to person and 
property and location.  The DPW will continue to act in a reactionary manner as needs arise.  All tree work other than 
brush removal, chipping, and the removal of small diameter trees will continue to be outsourced due to the lack of a 
bucket truck or crane and a licensed tree professional.  Each spring and after storms, the DPW staff remove and dispose 
of woody debris within the Town’s right-of-way.  An aggressive program by Unitil in recent years has lessened the 
burden of the Town by removing those trees that may cause an interruption of electrical service to the community.  The 
DPW appreciates and thanks Unitil for their responses to remove hazardous trees when asked to do so. 
Roadside Vegetation Maintenance 
The DPW staff responded to many requests to cut and clear overgrown vegetation from roadsides.  This work is done 
using lawn mowers, saws, and other hand tools as the existing roadside mower was disabled.   A new utility tractor with 
a boom rotary mower attachment (22-foot horizontal reach and 19.5-foot vertical reach) was ordered in September of 
2022.   The DPW is developing a town-wide program of selective curbside mowing and vegetation clearing using the 
new tractor and boom mower that will significantly enhance our roadside vegetation maintenance program.   
Landfill 
The closed and capped landfill off Youngs Road is inspected by a consultant semiannually and remains in compliance 
with the requirements of the Massachusetts Division of Environmental Protection (DEP).  Gas monitoring and 
groundwater contamination sampling and testing is conducted by a consultant.  The landfill site including the perimeter 
fencing is inspected on a regular schedule by DPW personnel. 
Adjacent to the closed landfill is a storage area for catch basin materials and street sweepings both determined to be 
hazardous waste by the EPA.  The DEP has issued a Beneficial Use Determination (BUD) permit which allows the 
storage of these materials.  A consultant performs the yearly testing of these materials.   
On seven Saturdays in the fall and the spring, DPW personnel oversee and manage Yard Waste Days.  Residents may 
drop off brush, leaves, and other organic materials for composting.

Solid Waste 
Solid waste (trash) is collected curbside weekly under the Pay as You Throw (PAYT) program by our vendor E.L.

Lake Shirley Dam 
In the early 1990’s, with the structure in need of repair, The Lake Shirley Management Corporation Association turned 
over ownership of the dam to the Town.  After the completion of the total reconstruction of the dam, the responsibility 
for the maintenance, monitoring, and record keeping were completed by a small number of volunteers from the Lake 
Association.  Compliance with State dam regulations, the establishment of an operations manual as well as an 
emergency action plan was placed under the umbrella of the Department of Public Works in 2004-2005.   The dam is 
inspected by a professional engineer licensed in Massachusetts.  The most recent inspection resulted in a positive report, 
and we remain in compliance with regulations. 
Winter Operations 
The DPW was prepared for and responded to all snow and ice weather events with the goal of providing a high level of 
service in a timely manner while at the same time trying to minimize costs.  Lunenburg joins with 17 other towns in a 
cooperative bid to obtain the best possible pricing for rock salt.  However, the price of rock salt continues to steadily 
increase every year.  The DPW maintains a small fleet of snow removal equipment (trucks, plows, spreaders, loader, 
backhoe, and sidewalk plow).  The fleet is supplemented with vendors as needed.  Due to breakdowns of the aged 
Town’s fleet of large dump trucks, more vendors were needed; thus, adding to costs. 
The clearing of snow and ice from roadways is a critical operation of the DPW as it is a matter of public safety.   
The Town and its residents are fortunate to have a dedicated group of DPW personnel and private vendors who tirelessly 
and successfully challenge the worst Nature has to offer regardless of the severity or duration of a storm.  “DPW 
workers are the original Storm Troopers.”

Holman, and two seasonal cemetery laborers.  The Superintendent coordinates burials with families and funeral homes 
and meets regularly with the three-member Cemetery Commission.  The Highway Division provides equipment and staff 
for maintenance and burials, which on average is forty per year.   
Wastewater

vendor is contracted to maintain, repair, and respond to emergencies at the 10 sewer pump stations.  The outstanding 
Sewer Business Manager, Barbara Lefebvre, manages the administrative functions of the Sewer Division including 
billing, customer service, permitting and budgets.  An Assistant Sewer Business Manager, Paula Bertram, was hired to 
help facilitate the many tasks of the division.  The Sewer Business Manager and DPW Director worked closely with the 
Sewer Commission to ensure sewer rules, regulations, and policies are followed and to assure the future long-term 
operation of the wastewater collection system. 
Fleet Maintenance 
The skilled and dedicated DPW Mechanic, James Sticklor, is responsible for the preventative maintenance and repair of 
all Town vehicles.  The vehicles include over 30 pieces of highway construction equipment, high performance police 
vehicles, Fire and Rescue first responder apparatus, mowers and more.  Highly technical repairs and repairs that cannot 
be completed in-house are outsourced to specialty vendors as needed.  A fleet maintenance software program continues 
to be a valuable tool as each repair is logged by vehicle identification number and stored in a comprehensive database 
and required services, repair costs over the life of the equipment, and part numbers are easily obtained.

FINANCE COMMITTEE REPORT
 
Fiscal Year 2023
 
Annual Town Meeting is an important event for community members to come together and vote on warrant articles that 
directly impact how we manage our town. Amongst these articles is the FY23 budget, capital plan, authorizations for 
various enterprise and revolving funds, and appropriations relating to stabilization funds and reserves. Throughout the 
year the Town Manager and department heads carefully review expenses and have thoughtful conversations about how 
to best serve and manage the town. While developing the budget they considered funding, contracts, and mandates, as 
well as what the community wants and needs to thrive. The Town Manager formulated a recommended budget, which 
she presented to the Finance Committee on February 17th. Over six subsequent Finance Committee meetings, 
department heads presented overviews and plans relating to their budgets and capital requests. A Public Hearing was 
held on March 31st, as required by the Town Charter, and the Finance Committee voted on recommendations pertaining 
to financially relevant articles. 
 
Armed with all the information gathered during this process, the Finance Committee recommends approval of the Town 
Manager’s omnibus budget, as outlined in this warrant. 
Overall, our financial condition is good. Revenues are stable, aided by strong collections, increases in state aid, new 
growth, and a variety of federal and state grants that the town has secured. On the expense side, while not meeting every 
need outlined by the department heads, this budget does begin to address several emerging and previously unmet needs. 
The total in requested expenditures is $49,953,531.76. While we are pleased with our current fiscal standing, we do face 
challenges ahead; especially with regard to a number of our municipal buildings, which we outline in more detail below. 
Notably, our certified free cash this year is $2,666,962, which is substantially higher than in previous years, a temporary 
benefit resulting from appropriations that were not fully expended in FY21 as a result of COVID-related restrictions and 
impacts. Town fiscal policy directs portions of free cash to accounts such as stabilization, the OPEB allocation (a fund to 
pay for future retiree benefits), and the capital plan. 
Accordingly, this year’s recommended capital plan (funded by free cash, tax revenues, and unspent capital 
appropriations from previous years) is $2,162,849. It is an unprecedented investment of available funds (without 
incurring any additional debt) into the capital infrastructure that supports our town. As of the printing of this warrant, the 
Finance Committee is seeking to answer a few specific questions pertaining to the capital plan. We will have a 
recommendation on the capital plan, Article 6, at Town Meeting on May 7. 
Other notable items: 
 For FY23, the proposed appropriation for education is $23,397,371, 56.2%% of the Town’s total budget, and a 
3.78% increase over last year. This includes our Monty Tech assessment at $1,054,376, which is a 20.4% increase 
for FY23, mainly due to an increase in enrollment from 83 to 94 students. Additionally, the town budget includes 
$1,207,421 in School Retiree Health Insurance. Fortunately, this year the town benefited from an $898,610 increase 
in Chapter 70 aid, which more than offsets the $673,617 increase in the Lunenburg School District budget. 
While our Chapter 70 increase was welcome news, the increase was partly a result of formula changes that recognized 
more low-income students at a higher tier of funding. Our vulnerable populations are of top concern for all of our 
department leaders. 
An area of concern for the Finance Committee are several personnel positions that the School District is funding through 
ESSER 2 and ESSER 3 federal grants. These grants, totaling $1,939,868 will expire in FY24. Many of these positions 
are addressing acute health, academic, and social/emotional needs that have arisen from COVID-19. Over the next two 
years the School Committee will need to determine what positions we need to maintain, and how to fund them., 
 This year’s budget supports an increase of $53,443 for the library budget to support the addition of two part-time 
personnel and additional expenses. This increase will enable the library to extend its operating hours to include

SENIOR PROPERTY TAX WORK-OFF PROGRAM 
JANUARY 1, 2022 – NOVEMBER 1, 2022 
The Senior Property Tax Work-Off Committee is administered by the Assistant Town Manager/HR Director. 
What is the Senior Property Tax Work-Off? 
 
The Senior Property Tax Work-Off Program offers income eligible senior citizens age 60+ an opportunity to have an 
abatement applied to their property tax bill. Participants work in various town departments doing jobs such as general 
office work, custodial, library shelving, meals on wheels, school assistance, etc., to earn the tax abatement. 
The 2022 tax abatement maximum is $1,500 and applied to the participant’s 3rd quarter real estate tax bill. The current 
hourly rate is $15.00 per hour. 
 
Eligibility Requirements 
 Applicants must be 60 years of age or older 
 Must occupy and be the owner of the property as of July 1st 
 Receive a property tax bill in their name or their spouse’s name (60 years+)

SEWER COMMISSION – FY22 
The Sewer Commission bears the responsibility of overseeing the Sewer Enterprise Fund, planning for the future, and 
developing and implementing rules, regulations and policies. Under the Town’s Charter, responsibility for the day-to-
day operation, care and maintenance of the sanitary sewers are under the supervision of the Town Manager who 
delegates the responsibility to manage and maintain the operational side of the system to the Department of Public 
Works Director. The Town of Lunenburg does not operate a sewer treatment plant. Approximately 86% of Lunenburg 
sewer is discharged to the City of Leominster, and the other 14% is discharged to the City of Fitchburg. 
In FY22 the following members served on the Sewer Commission; Dave MacDonald-Chair, John Reynolds-Vice Chair, 
Brett Ramsden-Clerk and Members Mike Deveikis and Mike Nault. The Commission continued to meet virtually via 
Zoom for all meetings and voted to begin each meeting with the Pledge of Allegiance.  
There are currently 915 properties connected to the Lunenburg system and another 327 which have access to Town 
Sewer but are not yet connected. During FY22, 14 new accounts connected to Town Sewer. The Commission billed out 
$1,021,525 in quarterly sewer bills and had $30,593 of prior year delinquencies added to the owners’ property tax bills 
for non-payment.    
During FY22, CTE (Chad Empey) continued to provide excellent service for the Operation & Maintenance of the Sewer 
Stations. The Commission approved funds for repairs to the Dana Street Station which included the replacement of the 
impellers, seals and gaskets and approved the replacement of the electrical panel at the Mass 2 station.  These upgrades 
are important to keep the stations running smoothly. The Commission voted 3/8/22 to approve the Year 3 contract with 
CTE, with a 4% increase to commence 7/1/22. 
DPW Director Rob Oliva worked with the Commission in FY22 to implement the Maintenance Plan for the sewer 
system. Clean Basins was contracted to camera and auger a blockage on Mass Ave. near Riley Road. In October 2021 
Green Mountain Pipe grouted the pipe joints on Elizabeth St. to address a long-standing Inflow/Infiltration (I/I) issue in 
3 services. Rob unfortunately left Lunenburg in March 2022, but the Commission welcomed Bill Bernard in May as 
Director.

In FY22, the Commission continued the annual permit program for the 20 Food Service Establishments with grease traps 
and interceptors on Town Sewer. The Commission once again voted to waive the Annual Permit Renewal Fee for the 
Grease Trap Program, as many restaurants were still struggling to gain back business after the Covid 19 closures. In 
February 2022, the Commission voted to drop the Annual Permit Renewal fees for establishments in compliance, 
effective with FY23 renewals.  Those who are not in compliance will pay $350 for internal traps and $650 for external 
trap renewals.  Grease Trap compliance continues to be a focus of the Commission as Fats, Oils and Grease (FOG) can 
negatively affect the smooth operation and maintenance costs of the sewer system.  While the Grease Trap Regulation 
focuses on commercial and public disposal, individual homeowners can also contribute a significant amount of grease 
into the system which increases everyone’s costs. Whether on sewer or septic, grease should not be put down the drain.  
While working on the FY23 budget, an 8% rate increase was approved by the Commission to cover increasing costs in 
usage from Fitchburg and Leominster. The rate increased from $12.65/100 cu.ft. to $13.66/100 cu.ft. and the minimum 
charge from $107.00 to $115.00/quarter.  The Commission continued working in FY22 with Flow Assessment to 
maintain and ensure the accuracy of the flow meters that measure usage going to Fitchburg and Leominster.  
The Commission also voted to update several policies for re-connection fees, prorating bills for change of ownership or 
new connections, easements and waivers. A public hearing will be held in FY23 to address the policy updates.  
The Commission met with several groups in FY22 looking to expand sewer. The Parks Commission requested to expand 
the Sewer Service Area (SSA) to include Marshall Park, which was approved at the November 2021 Town Meeting. 
Extensions for Upland Ave./Eastern Ave. and White St./Maple Parkway were discussed and preliminary designs, with 
estimates, were done by Wright Pierce. However, a survey of residents did not indicate the necessary 2/3rds interest in 
favor of the extensions. Other projects before the Commission  were an 8-unit condo at 711 Mass. Ave., lots  at 790-792 
Mass. Ave., and a marijuana establishment (Uma Flowers) at 43 Summer St.  A horse rescue facility and 20-unit 
residential project at 5 Chestnut St. requested to be added to the SSA. An article was added to the May Town Meeting 
but was passed over. The 200 apartment 40B project application on Howard Street was approved by Mass Housing to 
proceed in seeking approvals from the Lunenburg ZBA.  The project will seek a large expansion of the Sewer Service 
Area and Water District to bring utilities to the project.
