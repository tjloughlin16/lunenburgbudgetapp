BOARD OF SELECTMEN: Seated left to right - Lance D. May, Chairman Walter B. Keeler, Paul E. Ralston and Executive
Secretary Barry J. Stell.
An annual report traditionally should focus in on two areas of interest and concern to include 1.) a review of
highlights/accomplishments of the past year and 2.) the Board's goals and objectives for the coming year and beyond as we perceive
them to be as the Chief Executive Officers of the Town government. Understanding that Lunenburg is a tradition-oriented
community, we are pleased to submit the following.
The past year provided many challenges and much progress in many areas:
- The further updating of Board of Health regulations with particular emphasis on environmental issues and their
relationship to small lots, wells, etc.
- Continuation of Clean Lakes Diagnostic Study of Lake Shirley and the corresponding coordination with the engineering
firm of Metcalf and Eddy by the Clean Lakes Advisory Committee.
- The problems of the Town Hall, its structural problems and general run down condition, continued to take the attention of
the Board. In June, after a close inspection with the Building Committee, we found it necessary for public safety reasons, to cordon
off the front of the building until necessary structural repairs of the bell tower could be accomplished. This was completed by
August 1st. The question of renewal/replacement of Town Office facilities is still being studied by the Building Committee. They
are currently looking into all alternatives. Under the By-law change voted by the Town Meeting last year, one member of the Board
of Selectmen now serves on the Building Committee.
- The formation of a Town Council made up of representatives of all Boards and Committees was established. The purpose
of the Town Council is to provide a forum for improving communications and an opportunity to explore possible Town-wide goals
and objectives.
- Initiation of a Building Department with our own Building Inspector and Zoning Enforcement Officer. The fast evolving
growth that Lunenburg is and has been experiencing necessitated this change from the previous arrangement of a shared Building
Inspector/Department with the Town of Townsend.
- The receipt of an Incentive Aid Grant from the State Executive Office of Communities and Development along with the
Towns of Watertown and Sturbridge in the amount of $27,700 for a Capital Needs Prioritization process looking toward a State-
wide model that could be incorporated by other communities throughout the State. This process will utilize the Town Council as
the forum for discussion of the capital needs of Lunenburg.
- The establishment of a Commission of the Bicentennial of the Constitution made up of seven members whose purpose is
to provide a three year plan for Lunenburg as part of a nation-wide celebration of the founding of the Constitution and its meaning
to all Americans.
- The initiation of a Talent Bank whereby townspeople could sign up for possible appointment to the various
Committees/Boards and Commissions that make up town government. This has been a very successful program wherein we did
4
===PAGE 7===
appoint several persons from the applicants in the Talent Bank. We ended up receiving approximately 25 applications and the Board
interviewed all of them.
The above were in addition to the ongoing weekly attention to our duties including licensing, permit grants, hearings, various
site visitations, working with Police, Fire and Highway Departments on all kinds of issues, policy decisions, involvement in Board
of Health problems, working with the Planning Board as well as other Committees, participation in area and State meetings such as
Nashoba Associated Boards of Health, Worcester County Advisory Board, the Massachusetts Municipal Association, and others.
Looking down the road, we see the following as areas of need and high priority:
- A Town decision on the need and implementation of new and additional Town Office facilities.
- Creation of Police Lock-Up facilities in the Police/Fire Station as proposed by Chief Morin.
- Acceptance by the Town of the Charter Commission Report and implementation of same as promptly as possible.
- Continued progress on traffic control problems, specifically signalization of Route 13/Whalom Road and Main
Street/Lancaster Avenue/Massachusetts Avenue intersections.
- Further cooperation and coordination with the Lunenburg Water District over water issues and future of same. We should
be exploring the possible future incorporation of the Water District into the Town government structure, i.e. a Lunenburg Water
Department as water resources become more and more of critical Town-wide concern with our continued rapid growth.
- Residential landfill fees as well as increased commercial fees were initiated. We are much concerned about the life
expectancy of our present landfill and feel this move will at least partially accomplish two goals helping to offset the operating
cost of the landfill as well as not making our landfill overly attractive to commercial trash haulers. We will continue to explore
better solutions to our solid waste problems, perhaps participation in a resource recovery regional facility at MCI Shirley as a
possibility.
- Financial constraints continued to frustrate the Board as well as all department heads and indeed all who are charged with
any responsibility in our Town governmental structure. Until the State fully makes up the Proposition 2 1/2 losses, Lunenburg's
level of services and progress will continue to slide downhill. We will work toward the goal of seeking a more sound and adequate
financial base for Lunenburg.
- Board of Health issues are also of great concern to the Board, particularly the advent of individual and subdivision
wastewater treatment plants. At present there is little information or experimental data available to Boards of Health that we must
proceed with much caution in this area. Well regulation will also be a priority of the Board of Health in the near future.
In summary, we want to say "thank you" to our Executive Secretary, Barry Stell, and our competent staff for making our life
easier, and also to the many Committees and Commission members who serve SO effectively and diligently on behalf of the entire
Town. We do need and are grateful for this continued support and positive spirit.
Board of Health
Your Town participates in a unique public health organization, the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health. Nashoba is an
association of towns governed by your Town's Board of Health. Since 1931, Nashoba Associated Boards of Health, as agents to the
local boards of health, has provided a range of services to its member communities. As a reflection of changing times, Nashoba's
duties have expanded from traditional tasks, such as nursing visits and disease prevention, to increasing contemporary needs for
social work and environmental health protection. Nashoba is working to meet not only a wider range of services, but also to hone
the professional skills demanded in these specialty areas.

Board of Assessors
BOARD OF ASSESSORS: Seated left to right - Chief Clerk Jacqueline A. Keeler, Chairman William F. Reed, Bernard J. Paton
and Kathryn M. Herrick.
The Board of Assessors wishes to thank the voters for their cooperation during the past year in approving our requests for
funds at both the annual and special town meetings held in 1986.
The Assessors' tax-mapping project is under way with the contract awarded to William Bingham and Associates, Inc., of
Leominster for $27,300 to be completed by July 1 of 1987. This will satisfy all requirements of the Department of Revenue as to
the Town's tax maps.
We also appreciate the $15,000 voted at the special town meeting to allow the Board of Assessors to purchase the computer
hardware which will allow our participation in the Department of Revenue's Computer-Assisted Mass Appraisal program. In this
regard, we have concluded that we will conduct the January 1, 1988 triennial revaluation as an "in-house" project, assisted by the
Department of Revenue, Bureau of Local Assessment Division. We will include an article in the May 1987 town meeting warrant in
the amount of $75,000, a reduction of some $100,000 over our 1986 estimate, to cover the projected costs of the required
revaluation.
We will also, again, be requesting funding for a full-time assistant assessor and will offer a considerable reduction in our
revaluation article if this funding is approved. The need for a full-time assistant assessor will be supported by a recommendation for
filling the position by the Charter Commission report. We request your support of this request at the May town meeting.
The Town is experiencing a construction boom, and the real estate market is and has been extremely active. Sales of real estate
in 1985 amounted to a total of $15,400,000 with 165 arms-length sales being recorded. The assessed valuation-to-sales price ratio
equalled .71%. In the first nine months of 1986 we recorded $20,800,000 in sales of 124 units of real estate with an assessed
valuation-to-sales price ratio of .56% In two years the value of the average house in the Town has doubled.
The passage at town meeting last May of the town-house zoning bylaw has opened up the Town to another type of housing
construction, and as indicated by the current plans filed with the Planning Board, we can anticipate some 400 units to be in some
stage of construction in the coming year. All this construction adds up to a definite need for a full-time assistant assessor to handle
the ever-increasing work load, both in the office and in the field.

We have been forced, by the increased office work due to ever-increasing State reporting requirements, among other things, to
close the Assessors' office to the public on Tuesdays and Thursdays. We hope, with the addition of the computer, to increase the
efficiency of our office operation, and when the cumputer program is fully implemented, the office will return to normally open
status five days a week.

Auditor
In accordance with Massachusetts General Laws, Chapter 41, Section 50, I have examined the books and accounts of the Town
of Lunenburg for the period ended June 30, 1986. My examination included tests of receipts, all expenditures of money including all
original invoices and vouchers approved by the responsible department head on which money has been paid from its Treasury. The
Cash Book additions including school funds deposited in the General Cash were reconciled, and the balances were proved by
reconciling the bank balances with statements furnished by the banks of deposits, and such tests were made as I deemed necessary at
various dates during the year, and in my opinion they present fairly the balances in these accounts (see below). My examinations
were made in accordance with generally accepted auditing standards.
The books and accounts of the Tax Collector were tested and checked at various dates. Real and personal property and motor
vehicle excise taxes were audited. Collections were verified with deposits in the Treasury as shown, and receipts were checked with
the commitment records.
I reviewed payroll time sheets, checks register and deductions register, and minor discrepancies were disclosed and corrected.
Bank reconciliations were done for three separate months, all old outstanding checks were cancelled, in most cases, new checks were
issued and cleared.
The Town of Lunenburg has now accepted and converted its financial statements and accounting records to the revised 1981
Uniform Massachusetts Accounting System (U.M.A.S.).
My examination found other minor discrepancies which have been corrected. I certify this report states my opinion of the
accounting records and procedures in the Town of Lunenburg, and is correct to the best of my knowledge.

WATER RESOURCE COMMITTEE: Seated left to right James L. Deming, George B. Larkin and James

The Water Resource Committee was instituted by vote of the 1984 Annual Town Meeting under the control of the Planning
Board, and consists of the following members: Jim Deming and Donald Gillis representing the Lunenburg Water District, Jim Mann
and George Larkin of the Planning Board, Russell Harvey, Jr. of the Conservation Commission, and Scott Foster and Dexter White
as Members at Large.
The Committee was charged with the responsibility of exploring and locating sites, within the Town boundaries, which will
provide residents with a potable water supply for future use. To date the sum of $55,000 has been expended on this project -
$35,000 from the Town and $20,000 allocated by the Lunenburg Water District.
Work on the project was contracted to the D. L. Maher Company of North Reading (a ground water development company)
under the direction of Ted Morine, Senior Hydrogeologist. Jim Deming and Don Gillis of the Water District were the liaison
between the Committee and D. L. Maher Company. It should be noted that DEQE commended the Town for its foresight in
initiating this study.
Five sites have been found with the potential for being future water supplies and they are:
Site #1 ========================= Hickory Hills Lake - good potential - negotiations with the owner are in progress.
Site #4 - Lake Shirley - - excellent potential.
Site #6 - Lake Shirley - - excellent potential.
Site #7 - Reservoir Road - good potential.
Site #16 - Goodrich Street - - good potential.
It is the recommendation of this Committee that the 400 foot diameter area needed around these five sites be acquired and/or
protected in whatever manner deemed appropriate by the Board of Selectmen and Town Counsel as future potable water resources for
the Town.

Park Commission
Fiscal year 1987 will be most difficult as our budget was slashed by almost $10,000 or 22%. The actual reduction was
$7,000, but salary increases will result in a total decrease of $10,000 available for programs and services. This actual dollar decrease
will obviously mean a decrease in programs and services. The advent of Proposition 2 1/2 was a challenge. Your Park
Commissioners used this as a means to become creative in finding money, not only to maintain programs, but to increase them. In
fiscal year 1986 because of user fees, contributions, and sponsorships, $40,000 of Town money resulted in over $60,000 of
programs and services. No other Department in Town can boast of a return of 50 cents on the dollar. However, our success meant
failure as the Finance Committee determined that Parks and Recreation had a low priority. A high use of our facilities by
Townspeople and a record participation in our recreational programs, somehow translated into a low priority for budget
considerations.
These are not scare tactics or posturing, but further cuts in the Parks and Recreation budget will mean that more programs
will be discontinued. The Town Beach Program will be eliminated as further reductions in beach operations will be counter
productive. Beach fees have been tripled in the last five years, but this resulted in a 30% drop in use. Further increases will not be
enough to solve the budgetary difficulties. In order to salvage the Park and Recreation budget, the Town Beach Program may have to
be jettisoned. At this point, a final decision has not been made.
The Town's support of recreational activities is low in comparison with surrounding communities, and we have apparently
reached the limit on being able to beg, borrow, or cajole support. As always, we would like to thank all those who have labored for
Parks and Recreation, and those who have volunteered their efforts, and all that have participated in our programs.

LIBRARY TRUSTEES: Seated left to right - Carolyn C. Prescott and Jean M. Jones. Standing left to right -

The Library Trustees and staff felt that they had received an encouraging vote of confidence when the money needed to raise
salaries to an equitable level, and to increase the book and building maintenance budgets, was voted at the 1986 Town Meeting.
Although this amount fell short of the original budget request, it did provide the incentive to continue trying to offer ever better
services.
Another positive note was the allotment of $5,000 to obtain architectural planning for building renovation and remodeling. At
the time of this writing, the process of selecting an architect is underway and we hope to be prepared with the figures to request the
building funds at the 1987 Town Meeting. Some of the problems that prompted the original request are rapidly becoming more
urgent. The septic system repeatedly fails when the ground water level is high. Some of the emergency exits are wholly inadequate.
Storage and work areas planned for the level of service that was needed 25 years ago are impossibly crowded, and adversely affect the
amount and quality of service that can be provided now. Due to underfunding, library maintenance has been neglected. It is time to
correct the existing problems before they become worse. Lunenburg residents know too well the consequences of failing to maintain
a building. We are hopeful that the needed changes can be accomplished inside the existing building, keeping the cost within reach.
We would then be in a position to offer improved and expanding services in a sound, attractive structure for the foreseeable future.
Although the State bill, which would have made substantial construction funds available for libraries for the first time in
years, died at the end of 1986, it has been refiled and there is a good chance of recovering a part of remodeling costs from the State if
we are prepared with plans and figures. We are advised by the Massachusetts Board of Library Commissioners that letters to
legislators from local library supporters are a very effective way to influence the outcome of this bill.

===PAGE 18===
Circulation of library materials, although no longer increasing at the rapid pace of the early 1980's, continues to be brisk.
Books suitable for the youngest children are in growing demand, while adults are borrowing less. Total circulation was 54,876
items; 18,958 to adults, 1,154 to youth, and 34,764 to children. Circulation of videocassettes supplied by the Regional Library
System was curtailed while the Regional AV collection was being moved. The popularity of this service, before that time and since
it has been resumed, indicates a strong interest in having a local collection. We are applying for a competitive State grant to allow
expansion of this feature.
The retirement of Emily Foster in May, 1986, was a major change both for the library users and for the rest of the staff.
Emily worked at Ritter for 15 years, 12 of those years as Children's Librarian and then as Coordinator of Children's Services. Most
families could not imagine the Children's rooms without her, and the staff felt that they were losing their lodestar. The Children's
programs now firmly established are the result of Emily's commitment to meet whatever needs were expressed, and to incorporate as
many as possible of the services offered at larger libraries into the local picture. Four pre-school story groups, Author Day with the
schools, a tremendous summer reading program, and the library services for the Primary School are among the flourishing services
set up under her direction. The Trustees hosted a retirement party at the library where there were many heartfelt expressions of
appreciation. The new Children's Librarian is Karen Kemp. Her familiarity with the library as a borrower and long-term Town
resident, and as a most active member of the Friends of the Library made her able to assume the position with a minimum of
disruption. The transition has been smoother than we had thought possible. Children's Services continue to be the fastest growing
area of library service.
One of the Library's dependable resources for several years has been the Friends' group. This year they have again sponsored
the Children's Art Festival and most of the special summer events. They have raised funds through book, yard, and bake sales, and
memberships to provide museum passes, story hour materials, magazine storage boxes, and 143 paperback books awarded to
children completing the summer reading program. All of these activities, plus their volunteer help, makes the Friends' group one of
our primary assets.
Gifts to the Library, for which we are very grateful, have included donations in memory of Ralph P. Benjamin, C. Kimball
Francis, Charles Loufus, Albert J. Moisan, Sr., Daniel A. Violette, and Walter C. White. Four pictures were added to the Benjamin
lending collection. A donation from Lunenburg Memorial Fund was used to purchase an electronic typewriter which is of great
assistance in speeding the technical processing of new books. Massachusetts Arts Lottery funds awarded to the Library provided
additions to the lending picture collection. Arts Lottery funds received by the Friends were used for a pass to the Children's
Museum, and Author Day speaker, and a story-telling session.
Lunenburg residents show a growing need for the multifaceted services and advanced forms of information retrieval available at
larger libraries. At the same time, they still appreciate the more personalized, small-town atmosphere that has always been a part of
Ritter. Our goal continues to be to provide the best blend possible of old and new services, tailored to the unique needs of
Lunenburg.

The year 1986 was the Council on Aging's tenth anniversary of the Meals on Wheels Program, delivering hot, nutritional
meals to the home-bound elderly. We've come a long way since the Program's inception in 1976, when we had two volunteers
delivering twelve meals, three days a week. We presently have ten volunteers now delivering an average of five hundred fifty meals
per month. Again, as in the past, the success of this program has only been accomplished with the aid of a super team of volunteers
who have been so willing to give of their time.
We were fortunate to receive a formula grant from the Department of Elder Affairs this year, which will enable us to purchase
several, much needed, pieces of equipment for our Meals Program.
Many senior citizens have questioned the Council on why we do not have a congregate site in addition to the homebound
deliveries. However, the answer is simple. First, we do not have a Senior Center, much less any space in our Town to establish a
meal site. The Council's dream of the future is to seek funding for a suitable site for a building with ample parking which would
house not only a meal site, but include our Drop-In Center which could expand to more than three hours a week; and our Council
Office. The building could also be used for meetings for other elderly groups, such as the Friendly Seniors, a Booster Club to
support additional elderly programs, etc. If this ideal dream became a reality, the Lunenburg Council on Aging would qualify for
many more grants and benefits from the State Department of Elder Affairs and other State Agencies.
Our senior citizens are still praising the Transportation Program purchased from Einard Warpula, who picks his passengers up
at their door and delivers them to do their shopping, banking, and other appointments to doctors, dentists, etc. and safely home
again. This service is available on Tuesdays and Thursdays by calling Mr. Warpula a day or two ahead. The cost is fifty cents one
way or a dollar round trip.
We have two new members on our Council, appointed by the Board of Selectmen, Josephine Meloon and Ellen Kemp. We are
sure that their input will be an added asset to our Council.
The Council is making plans for the coming year to offer several different one-day bus trips and also a special entertainment
program for all Lunenburg Seniors.

During 1986 the Lunenburg Historical Commission completed the work necessary for applying for inclusion of Lunenburg
Town Center on the National Register of Historic Places. An imaginary circle was drawn around the center of the Town, including
Main Street, Leominster Road and Lancaster Avenue around the Common, and about a quarter mile in either direction on
Massachusetts Avenue. Having completed an inventory of all sites and buildings within this area, we hired a consultant, Gretchen
Schuler, an expert in this field, to prepare an application to the National Register of Historic Places for Lunenburg Town Center.
This was done during the summer, and has been submitted to the Massachusetts Historical Commission for preliminary approval.
On November 13, 1986, an informational meeting for property owners in the proposed National Historic District was held in
Turkey Hill Middle School Library, with about fifty people in attendance. Mrs. Schuler spoke to the group, explaining what it
means to live in a National Historic District, and answering questions. The response of those present was enthusiastic.
The Commission hopes to hear favorably from the National Historic Register in the near future.

Cable Advisory Committee
The Cable Advisory Committee meets on the second Thursday of each month at 7:00 p.m. in the Town Hall for the purpose
of monitoring the quality of cable service provided and the extent of license compliance evidenced by Adams Russell Cable Services,
the company currently licensed by the Board of Selectmen to deliver cable service in Lunenburg.
During 1986 the primary goal of the Committee, in addition to processing subscriber complaints and Town resident inquiries,
was to develop, conduct and evaluate a survey of those areas of the Town wired for cable service. The purpose for this survey was to
ascertain the quality of service the cable subscribers felt they were receiving from Adams Russell and to determine what interests or
concerns the subscribers, or potential subscribers, might have.
One hundred sixty-two (162) people who were cable subscribers and eighty-three (83) individuals who lived on streets wired
for cable but were not subscribers responded to the survey. Some of the findings were:

approval of the repair service
*
satisfaction with the kind of interaction
subscribers had with Adams Russell

other cable systems

Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District
The year was a year of transition for the Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School. This year has seen Stratos G.
Dukakis hired as the new Superintendent-Director, and the retirement of Charlotte A. Scott. Mr. Dukakis came to Monty Tech with
over eighteen years of experience in vocational education. He was formerly employed by Monty Tech from 1971 to 1981 as
Assistant Superintendent of Business Affairs.
The School opened this year with 1018 students. This figure is up from the previous year, indicating that students continue to
find the kind of education they are looking for at Monty Tech. During this year, Monty Tech was accredited by the New England
Association of Schools and Colleges. The accreditation was the culmination of eighteen months of effort on the part of the faculty
and staff credentials and effectiveness, curriculum content and appropriateness, and the School facility. Credit for this excellent
evaluation belongs to the hard-working Monty Tech staff. In addition, the Department of Education reviewed all of the vocational
programs, and has awarded commendations to all of the shop areas.
The Adult Education Program has also increased over 30% with the future prospects looking very bright. This year there will
be fifteen new programs and courses offered, bringing the total courses available to forty. One of the highlights of the year at the
School was the Drama Club's presentation of a musical version of "Scrooge". The production represents the renewal of interest in
dramatics at Monty Tech. "Scrooge" was directed by teachers Steven Johnson and Irene Moody. A new high tech program has been
instituted entitled "Principles of Technology", as part of the physics program. This highly regarded addition will upgrade the science
program at Monty Tech.
This year, Monty Tech participated in a statewide curriculum testing program for students in the 11th grade, and the results
indicated they did equal to or better than other regional vocational schools. A total of 17% of the Class of 1986 have gone on to
college. Students from Monty Tech are now attending Mount Wachusett Community College, Fitchburg State College, North
Adams State College, Central New England College, Cape Cod Community College, Johnson & Wales (R.I.) Culinary Institute of
America, the University of Lowell, and Boston University.
The Dean of Students announced in June that the School had completed the best year ever for attendance. In 1986, Monty
Tech established a seven member School Improvement Council under the Public School Improvement Act of 1985. Grants are
allocated to schools establishing the councils which will provide their schools with an opportunity to enrich their educational
resources.
Lunenburg's representative to Monty Tech, Charles J. Darling, in his annual report, states in part that Monty Tech is a unit
at work, learning, teaching, administering, maintaining and sustaining itself, supported by a great body of people, including the
Town of Lunenburg, all in a common effort to pass on our heritage to our young people. He hopes this common effort will
continue to grow between Monty Tech and the Town of Lunenburg.

Tax Collector
One of the duties of Tax Collector is to furnish a list of all outstanding town liens against a real estate property. Called a
certificate of municipal lien, this will list outstanding taxes, street betterments, tax title amounts, as well as outstanding water
charges of the Lunenburg Water District. Due to the lowering of mortgage interest rates, as well as the continuing high rate of real
estate sales in town, requests for these liens have more than doubled since 1983. This translates into 4 1/2 weeks work for my staff,
impacting their regular duties.
In spite of this additional work load, and through the temporary shuffling of personnel, we were again able to have a 100%
collection rate for Fiscal Year 1986 real estate taxes. This entails either actually collecting the taxes, or recording a lien against the
property at the registry of deeds. While most of the accounts are paid fairly soon, the town's position is protected in case of a sale
because title examiners can see that there are taxes outstanding. Additionally, those properties in tax title are charged interest at the
rate of 16%.
Town Clerk
Activity was high during 1986 in the Clerk's office due to the September and November state elections in addition to a
Special Town Meeting in October, and the Annual Town Meeting and Election in April. Much of that activity included training in
the operation of the new voting machines which were first used at the September primary. Forty regular and three demonstrator
units were purchased to meet the state's requirements, as well as a computer card reader and counter. This system showed it worked
at the November election, for we were all finished by 10:30 p.m. while other towns, still hand counting, didn't finish until 5 or 6
the next morning. We note that George Rollins of the Board of Registrars was unable to help out with the implementation of these
voting machines due to an illness. We hope to see him back soon.
The April Town Election race for Selectmen provided an opportunity to double check that vote. There was a 15 vote difference
between the two candidates and a recount was requested. Out came the law books and state regulations to find out what was the
procedure. While the results were exactly the same as the original count, the recount helped to maintain faith in the electoral
process.
Seeing that the town's records were slowly deteriorating, as well as the need for easy access to information, a fund raising
drive was started to begin to pay for restoration and microfilming of those records. A band concert was sponsored by my office in
the summer. Receipts from the sale of strawberry shortcake and baked goods have been added to donations from both town and out-
of-town residents. My thanks go out to all who have contributed so far. The fund raising continues and there will be articles on the
1987 Town Meeting warrant to assure that this project will continue.

Following are those records of Births, Marriages, and Deaths that either occurred in our Town or records which were forwarded
to this Town from other municipalities in the State. Lunenburg residents are urged to notify the Town Clerk if in-State records have
not been received and recorded. If a Birth, Marriage, or Death occurred outside the State, the same may be recorded in the Lunenburg

marriages which occur here but relate to licenses which were obtained elsewhere in the State, are no longer included in our official

5B INVOLVED IN THE STOCK MARKET
The students in Mr. Romano's math class were heavily involved in The Boston Globe Stock Market
Game this year. The game is part of The Globe's Newspaper in the Classroom Program. The game is
participated in the junior high/middle school division.

of each of the 272 teams enrolled from across New England.

answer questions from the children.
According to Mr. Romano, his students not only became more aware of the stock market but
developed an avid interest in the daily newspaper.

LANGUAGE PROGRAM IN KINDERGARTEN
Helping teachers respond to language needs of some kindergarten children has been the focus of
Language Specialist Francine Anderson. Working within the classroom with Teacher Susan Petrowicz and
Tutor Linda Sallee, she has been helping to implement the psycholinguistic approach to language (PAL)
into the K-6 class. The intensive language program uses the classroom as its setting and addresses
the whole child while providing academic relevancy.
The children are exposed to meaningful experiences which are then used as a framework to intro-
duce language through the use of five basic sentence patterns. Many of the lessons are developed from
everyday social experiences and listening activities. The concepts of exposure, recognition, comprehen-
sion, and production are the basic structure of the program.
The PAL program was begun because of the significant numbers of language-impaired children in
the system. It is currently being used in the lower resource room, and in the small group second and
third grade classes.

CURRICULAR PROGRAM FOR MIDDLE SCHOOL STUDENTS
Grade 6 and 7 students receive instruction in reading, math, English, social studies and science
for a total of 225 minutes each per week. Grade 8 students may select a foreign language for high school
credit, upon the advisement of guidance and teachers, in addition to English, math or algebra, social
studies and science. Each of these courses involves 225 minutes per week for the students. Students who
do not take foreign language, receive instruction in the basic skill areas of reading and writing in a
recently developed program called LAP (Language Achievement Program), at the eighth grade level. These
students may opt for foreign language at the high school.
Teachers of English classes at each grade level focus on written communication. Specially designed
folders are employed by all teachers and students to monitor student progress of writing skills and overall
writing improvement. English teachers in grade six participated in a program with their students, called
"First Editions," which enriched the writing as a process method of teaching writing. The program in-
volved a visiting artist and illustrator for the year. The program has been provided through a grant from
the Groton Center for the Arts. All students will have the final product, which is a book of their own
writing, for their own.
All students receive instruction in word processing to complete written assignments in English
classes. They are encouraged to complete other curricular assignments by utilizing the computer lab
as well.
Math teachers have made use of the computer lab with "drill and practice" activities on various
software. Social studies, health and science teachers have brought computer activities into their regular
curricular program. The computer lab contains fifteen Apple IIe's and five printers.
Special Education staff members employ the computer lab regularly for the purpose of tutoring
students in small groups.

The high school is continuing efforts to improve its foreign language curriculum. The high

and science were administered by the schools for the first time in April, 1986, as part of the Massa-

measure the effectiveness of curricular programs throughout the state, and for local school systems to
evaluate the effectiveness of their own curriculum. The high performance of Lunenburg students in the
three grades are attributable to the outstanding efforts of the teachers in carrying out the instruction
program in reading, mathematics and science.

Where Lunenburg's own testing program measures the performance of individual students, the
assessment testing of curriculum evaluates the degree to which the curricular program has been ab-
sorbed by groups of students. Lunenburg has never had the opportunity to compare its curriculum
effectiveness to that of other communities. The testing program will assist the local districts through-
out the state evaluate and possibly revise elements of curricular programs.
Some other important information was revealed by questionnaires administered to students before
the test was given. For example:
- In third grade, students who said that they spend as little as one half hour reading per
night, scored 84 points higher on this test than those who did not read at all.
- In third grade, students who said they watched TV for five hours or more scored over 110
points below classmates who watched for less than two hours.
- In third grade, 58% of the students said that someone at home listened to them read aloud
while 36% said that someone at home talked to them about their reading.
- In grade seven, 50% of the students said they spend one hour or more per day on homework,
and these students scored 60 points higher on the test than those who stated that they spent
one half hour or less.
- In grade eleven, 67% of the students said that they felt they needed math to get along well
in everyday life. These students had scores which were 60 points higher than those who felt
that math was not needed.
