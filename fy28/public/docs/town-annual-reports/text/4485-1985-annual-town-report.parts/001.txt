THE 1985 ANNUAL TOWN REPORT IS DEDICATED TO
THE 50TH ANNIVERSARY OF THE LUNENBURG FIRE
DEPARTMENT
1985 MARKS FIRE DEPARTMENT'S 50TH ANNIVERSARY
On March 20, 1934, the Board of Selectmen voted to appointa committee to "make an investigation of the
needs of the Fire Department." Prior to that time, there was an appointed Fire Chief, and Forest Wardens, but
no regular Fire Department. The only equipment was a Model A Ford truck with a portable pump, that was
primarily set up for forest fires.
The Committee recommended that the Town purchase a pumper and hose, that a siren be installed to call
fire fighters, that a volunteer fire company be established, and that a telephone be installed in the Fire Station.
On March 25, 1934, the first Board of Fire Engineers was appointed. They were:
William M. Harley, Chief
George H. Wakefield, Assistant Chief
Henry F. Hebert, Secretary

On May 15, 1935 a Mack, two stage, 500 gallon per minute pumper was purchased along with the
necessary and fittings for a total of $7,500. This truck was delivered on August 25, 1935, (This pumper
was traded in 1961 towards the purchase of new FWD pumper. It was recently purchased back by the
Lunenburg Fire Fighter's Association with the intent of restoring the pumper to its original condition.)
This Town Report is dedicated to the fifty years of tradition, dedicated service, and sacrifice that the
officers and fire fighters of the Lunenburg Fire Department have made to the citizens of the community. This
includes uncounted hours of lost sleep, time away from families, nights standing in the freezing cold, and
numerous cold or missed meals. This is an example of true community spirit that frequently does not exist in
larger communities. It is hoped that this tradition will continue for another fifty years,

Board of Selectmen
A horse and buggy is charming, but would you be satisfied with it as your principal form of
transportation? You could modernize by installing an engine to replace the horse and have something akin to
a Model T, but it's doubtful this would fulfill your needs either. Yet, we are struggling to make a horse and
buggy form of government (with modifications through the years which put it about a par with a Model T) do
the work of the space age. Lines of authority and communications have become blurred, and except when
extraordinary efforts and hours are expended, too many people end up doing their own thing without regard
for the whole. As the Chief Executive Office with responsibility, if not authority, for the entire town
government whether elected or appointed, the Board of Selectmen sees more of the problems and spends
change. more time dealing with them than any other one official or Board. In our considered opinion, it is time for a
In this regard we urge strong support of a Charter Commission and an open mind to substantial change in
search of greater efficiency and responsiveness in the operation of Town Government. The fact that "we
always did it that way" is just not good enough in the space age.
Also, the Building Committee will be presenting proposals for new Town Offices and a new Police facility.
Our present situation is unacceptable in terms of efficiency, working conditions, and safety. We strongly urge
you to listen well and make your decision looking to the future, not to the past.
Under the category of "responsible, but without authority", is time spent by the Board of Selectmen
unraveling certain unsatisfactory financial practices in regard to the weekly warrants, budgeting, and
assessing procedures. We feel the addition of a professional Town Accountant as part of a more efficient
reorganized town government will solve this problem.
The countless time spent dealing with the complications of growth and development have been
rewarding because of the spirit of co-operation of the many department involved, most especially a very
communicative Planning Board and Conservation Commission. The effort has just begun, however, and the
continued employment of consultants is essential. The Board of Selectmen is also proposing a Town Building
Department to be headed by a full-time Building Inspector for the coming year.
The Board of Selectmen does have authority to set policy for the Highway and Police Departments and to
a lesser extent the Fire Department--always subject to appropriation by the Town Meeting. Since the
implementation of Proposition 2½ the Selectmen have felt a growing concern about our ability to provide the
level of service we feel the Townspeople deserve, while at the same time meeting legal requirements. The time
has come to face up to the needs of these three departments which serve each and every resident of Town--the
young, the old, and everyone in between. As the number of persons and residences increase, the same level of
staffing and equipment gets spread further around; and although you are paying more, you as individuals are
getting less.
To address this situation, The Board of Selectmen and the Department Heads propose the following:
HIGHWAY DEPARTMENT
1. Add one full-time person and two summer student laborers. This is still short one full-time person and
one summer help from pre-Proposition 2½.
2. Fund all tree work in Highway Department to provide greater flexibility and a better opportunity for co-
ordination with utility company crews which work week days. This should result in more work
accomplished for the same cost.
3. Vote one lump sum for Highway Department operating budget allowing flexibility to spend where and
when the greatest need occurs.

REPORT OF EXECUTIVE SECRETARY TO THE BOARD OF SELECTMEN
Nineteen hundred and eighty-five marked the year in which the major responsibility for reviewing and
approving zoning applications and addressing zoning issues shifted from the Office of Selectmen to the
Building Inspector. I believe this adjustment in responsibilities has served to improve the efficiency of not only
the Office of Selectmen, but has improved overall management of zoning and enforcement. The Board of
Selectmen, serving as the Board of Health, still reviews those buildings applications which have a direct effect
on the capacity of the sewage disposal system.
I served as coordinator to two very active Ad Hoc Committees, Lake Shirley Advisory Committee and the
Computer Advisory Committee. At the 1985 Annual Town Meeting, the Town voted to appropriate $13,500
which, coupled with an identical amount raised by the residents of Lake Shirley, provided the local match of a
state grant totalling $90,000 to conduct a diagnostic and feasibility study of Lake Shirley. The Advisory
Committee was selected by the Board of Selectmen in September, and began the process for choosing a firm
to conduct the study. By year's end, the committee had reduced to five, the number of prospective candidates
for the study. Final selection is scheduled for January of 1986 with work to begin in March.
The Computer Advisory Committee has met steadily since March. During that time, the Committee, with
the assistance of a computer consultant, developed a request for bids package for a turnkey computer system
to service various Town departments, analyzed the bids received by the Town, and set up demonstrations by
the computer firms. The goal of the Committee is to present the voters of Lunenburg at the 1986 Annual Town
Meeting with a computer package which best meets the needs for automation for various municipal functions.
Within the realm of the Board of Health, regulations pertaining to sewage disposal were significantly
revamped. Regulations which duplicated State mandates were eliminated while new ones were promulgated
that clarifies the role of the Board of Health in examining the effect of building additions on individual sewage
disposal systems.
Briefly, other events which occurred in 1985: Holman Street bridge was reopended; the Selectmen gave
their signatures to the Housing Authority's application for four low/moderate income housing units; the
Selectmen submitted papers to the relevant State and Federal authorities seeking reimbursement for
expenses incurred by the Town as a result of emergency and clean-up operations during Hurricane Gloria;
and the Selectmen established a moratorium on issuing used car licenses.
Overall, 1985 was a very busy year for the Office of Selectmen, particularly with my increased duties as a
result of the acceptance by the Town of Chapter 41, Section 23A which authorize the Selectmen to assign to
the Executive Secretary any duties which they deem appropriate.
Town Counsel's Report
The real estate development boom that the Town is undergoing has led to a dramatic increase in
applications for building permits, requests for zoning determinations and interpretations, applications for
special permits and variances before the Zoning Board of Appeals, and a myriad of subdivision plans filed with
the Planning Board.
The time spent by this office on these land use issues has increased from year to year. This year easily was
monopolized by the "legal fallout" from the development boom which shows no sign of decreasing.
It is anticipated that the Town's efforts to identify, secure and protect a future water supply will become
the main focus of this office next year. The battle for control of the Town's land and water will continue to
require the full attention of the Town and its officials.

Lunenburg Water District
The Lunenburg Water District is a separate municipal entity as distinguished from the Town of
Lunenburg. It was established by an Act of the Legislature in 1939 in response to the growing need for a public
water supply system in the Town. It is operated and governed in much the same way as the Town, but it is
funded solely through its water revenue and it derives no money from the Town.
Three commissioners are elected for alternating three years terms. The election takes place at the Annual
District Meeting which is held the last Saturday in March. Any registered voter living within the geographic
confines of the District is eligible to vote. The Commissioners set policy, approve expenditures, set the rates
and appoint the working personnel of the District.
A budget is prepared based upon the anticipated revenue derived from the sale of water for the upcoming
year. The budget is presented to the voters and approved at the Annual Meeting. All additional major
expenditures are handled in the form of articles at that time. The District derives its operating expenses solely
through the sale of water and, by law, is prohibited from making a profit. If a surplus exists at the end of a fiscal
year, it must be put back into the District and is used to fund various capital improvement projects. The District
is and always has been self-supporting.
As you can see from the map on the next page, the District covers some 60% of the Town. The remaining
40% of Town is serviced by private wells or in some cases community wells. Additionally, a small section of the
Whalom District is serviced by the Leominster Water System. Over the past few years, the Town has become
increasingly aware of the water quality and quantity problems that exist in the 40% of the town not presently
serviced by the District. The District's primary responsibility is to provide service to its existing customers as
well as to provide for the needs of any additional connections within its boundaries. However, we also
recognize that as the sole source of public water supply within the Town, we have an obligation to look to the
future needs of the Town as a whole.
With this in mind, and knowing of the existing problems in other areas of Town, as well as the impending
need for additional water supply brought about by the increased development within the Town, the
Lunenburg Water District combined some of its funds with an equal amount of money supplied by the Town in
an all out effort to locate some additional sources of supply within the Town. These funds were combined and
then administered by a committee consisting of two members of the Water District, two members of the
Planning Board, one member of the Conservation Commission and two members from the citizenry of the
Town.
It is the Water District's sincere hope that this joint effort will eventually result in the discovery and
subsequent use of some additional sources of water. If the District is able to expend its sources of supply, it
then follows that we would be in a position to expand our boundaries and to provide service to some of those
areas in need while at the same time not adversely affecting our existing customers.

Special Water Resource Committee
At the 1985 Annual Town Meeting, it was voted to continue the Committee and to appropriate the sum of
$20,000 to conduct a search on a town-wide basis to identify a source or sources of water for public drinking
usage. The Committee interviewed and contacted various experts: geologists, hydrogeologists and the like. It
was decided that the Town would be best served by hiring the firm of D.L. Maher. This firm completed a study
of all areas of ground water potential within the Town. The report can be found at the Planning Board and
Selectmen's offices.
The above report identified 17 potential saturated glacial (gravel) sediment sites. A second study was
undertaken by the same firm to further define 5 out of the 17 sites before actual drilling was begun. At this
point $17,500 has been spent. The second report is also available for anyone wishing to read it.
The Lunenburg Water District at the same time allocated $20,000 to be spent in test drilling of as many of
the 17 sites as this money would afford. Nine sites were drilled as of December 31st and $18,000 was spent.
The Committee recommends that drilling and testing be continued until conclusive data is available. The 17
sites should be either eliminated, or defined as qualified for public drinking water.
The goal of the Committee continues to be protecting one or more sites capable of supplying the Town
with public water. Highest priority should be given to sites outside of the present wellfield's watershed. It is the
opinion of the Committee that the need for drinking water should be viewed not just as of today, but over the
longer time span: 50 or 100 years into the future. The Town of Lunenburg should consider this it's problem,
one critical to both our health and independence.

Auditor
In accordance with Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 41, Section 50 I have examined the books and
accounts of the Town of Lunenburg for the period from July 1, 1984 to June 30, 1985. My examination
included all the town officers and committees entrusted with receipt, custody or expenditures of money, and
all original bills and vouchers on which money has been paid from its treasury. The cash book additions were
verified, and the Treasurer's cash balances were proved by the reconciliation of the bank balances with
statements furnished by the banks of deposits, by examination of the saving bank books, and by actual count
of the cash in the office, and in my opinion they present fairly the balances in these accounts (see schedules
below). My examinations were made in accordance with generally accepted auditing standards.
The books and accounts of the Tax Collector were examined and checked. The tax, excise, and
assessments were audited and recorded. Collections were compared with the payments to the Treasurer as
shown by the Treasurer's receipts and the commitment records.
Consistent with the practices of many municipalities in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, the Town
has not maintained historical cost records of it's property, plant and equipment. Again, I recommend a
complete inventory and cost up-date be initiated. An inventory of contents (materials and supplies) was up-

Board Of Assessors
Massachusetts State law now requires every city and town to complete either a full revaluation or an
updating of assessed valuations every three years. The Department of Revenue, Bureau of Local Assessment,
is the enforcement agency for the State to insure compliance with the law. All revaluations or updating of
assessed valuations are completed with their supervision and their final certification of the new assessed
valuations.
State law requires certified assessed valuations to be within 10% of full and fair current market value, and
the failure to comply with the law results in the Department of Revenue's refusal to allow the city or town to set
its annual tax rate which, in turn, results in no revenues for the city or town. In effect, we cooperate or go
bankrupt.
In 1988 the Town of Lunenburg will undergo a full revaluation (mandated by State law). Your Assessors
will hire a professional revaluation firm to handle this job. All real and personal property will be viewed by the
revaluation firm and new values will be determined for the 1989 fiscal tax year. We estimate the cost of this full
revaluation to be approximately $200,000. Also, your Assessors were advised by the State Bureau of Local
Assessment that before the Town would qualify for certification of the, 1989 full revaluation, we will have to
obtain new engineered maps of the Town. Your Assessors will sponsor an article at the upcoming Annual
Town Meeting for $50,000 to acquire new maps of the Town.
The Town of Lunenburg should have a permanent full-time Assessor. There is too much money involved
for the Town to try and nickel and dime it any longer. There is value that is not being recorded and tax dollars
that are not being collected because the Assessor's office is still operating in the horse and buggy days.
When Santa Claus climbed down your chimney Christmas Eve and left you your 1986 real estate tax bill (3
months late) you may have suspected Lunenburg's good old tax department was not operating as efficiently
as possible.
If the Town had a permanent Assessor we would have been able to update our 1984 values without hiring
an outside firm to do the job for $20,000. Not only would we have saved $20,000, but, because the updating
would have been done by the local assessing department, the new values would be much more equitable.
One more thing, because it is difficult to receive certification from the State Department of Revenue,
Bureau of Local Assessment, and because there were many questions about the new values by local officials,
the tax bills that are usually mailed the first of September were not mailed out until the end of December.
Your Assessors estimate the lost interest to the Town by the late mailing of the tax bills, amounted to
$40,000,a loss that could have been eliminated with a full-time Assessor.
Therefore, we feel that it is in the Town's best interest to have a qualified permanent full-time Assessor.
The tax rate for FY'86 was set at 16.50 per thousand with the total valuation of the Town $259,128,312.00.

Planning Board
In Fiscal Year 1986, the Planning Board again addressed the implementation of the Town's Master Plan of
1983. The Board feels an urgency with respect to the direction of the Master Plan, which reflects the concerns
and wishes of the community as expressed via citizen's input in planning meetings. The urgency also comes
from fast-changing times that could render the Master Plan outdated before complete implementation. The
result of this could place Lunenburg in a position of not being ready to meet today's challenges to insure the
essence of the community.
In the first half of Fiscal 1986, the Board worked with Thomas Planning Services of Boston to update the
Subdivision Control Law, placing emphasis on environmental impact on the community. We further
developed a more cost-effective approach for the Town with Subdivision approval procedures.
Lunenburg placed water as one of its major priorities. This prompted the establishment of the Water
Study Committee in Fiscal 1985. The Planning Board attempted a protective hold on multi-developments to
ensure the safety of any possible water resource. The article was turned down at the 1985 Annual Town
Meeting. The Water Study Committee will write its own report, but the Planning Board, as their parent
committee, will continue to address the water issue in Fiscal 1987 and will work to protect any potential
source. We will be writing an Acquifer Protection By-Law.
In Fiscal 1986, the Planning Board was cognizant of the building growth in the greater Boston area which
includes Routes 128 and 495 and the Town's proximity to Worcester. We were forced to take a hard look at our
Blue Book (Protective Zoning By-Law) and while this book served the Town well in the past, it is not
responsive to present land use requirements and environmental concerns. We still feel our belief in the
protection of the Town in its rural character still holds true, but we realize also that our realistic responsibility
is to assess change and address it to achieve the objectives of the Zoning Enabling Act, Chapter 40A. This
Board feels a zoning by-law update can keep the community's essence while change is occurring through
development.
At the Special Town Meeting of December 4, 1985, the Board proposed several zoning articles. We
worked long and hard with Thomas Planning Services of Boston and our local committees and various
departments. Proposed change is always controversial. However, the Town Meeting did pass several
important by-laws. The lot frontage requirement was increased which will aid in good development and land
use. A by-law that permits town houses allows alternative housing to the single family dwellings now being
developed as larger homes. This by-law, and the more comprehensive and defined accessory housing by-law
which was passed, makes it possible for the young single, the married, the empty-nester, and retirees to reside
in Lunenburg. In addition, a good sign by-law was put in place. All of these by-laws have been approved by the
Attorney General.
The Board failed to gain support of a site plan revue by-law which is a good administrative tool in land
development. This necessitated a pass over of the article on the commercial by-law, Step 1. Presently, we are
working on a more comprehensive commercial by-law. This is important for the growth that is taking place.
Commerical development will be more extensive in Lunenburg in the next few years. We still feel it necessary
to protect the Town Center as it is the very heart of Lunenburg and, in our opinion, a well defined commercial
by-law will help.
At the time of the writing of this report, we are waiting to hear about our Grant application to the Executive
Office of Community and Development for our Strategic Planning Program. Also, before us is a sizeable
Subdivision entitled, "The Park at Houghton's Mill". The Board's approach was to implement the procedure
allowed us in the Subdivision Control Law and secure the services of a planning service and through them an
engineering firm. In addition, we involved all Town departments which would be concerned in the future of the
Subdivision. Through working with Thomas Planning Services as our consultant, and their engineers, Allen &
DeMurjian, we hope in the future to see a new part of our community developed in the best interest of new
residents and the Town of Lunenburg. No such undertaking, however, is without a great deal of work in the
area of study, agreement conferences, and detailed changes. This is necessary to place a project in the
community that meets the standards of good housing and secures reasonable protection for the Town in
future costs and environmental management.

Park Commission
Nineteen hundred and eighty-five was an excellent year for the Park Department. More than a decade of
frustration and decay was evident before corrective action was taken to reverse the process. After many years
of evaluation of alternatives, money was appropriated to repair the foundation of the beach house. The
deterioration of the beach house was SO complete prior to its repair that based on the recommendations of the

We wish to thank all the workers and volunteers whose efforts have contributed to the quality of life in our

Library Trustees
Having completed the celebration of the library's 75th year with an old-fashioned ice cream social in
August, the trustees have been "taking stock" and find many areas in need of attention. Years of inadequate
funds for building upkeep, and of inflation of prices, combined with constantly increasing use by the public,
have brought problems to the point that they cannot be ignored any longer. The last major updating of the
facility was in 1963.
While the library building itself is sound and attractive, it is not adequate for today's needs. Only the street
floor is accessible to the handicapped, but not when there is snow on the ground. Shelving and storage spaces
are filled to capacity. Artificial lighting should be improved. Part of the building is very hot in summer and cold
in winter, and wasteful of energy. Many of the furnishing are makeshift and do not allow the best use of space.
Upgrading of some of the building exits is needed. Lavatories, installed 22 years ago, are dank and odorous,
with septic flow problems. There is no space for new or expanded services.
The trustees have determined that many of the shortcoming of the physical plant can be remedied by
careful renovation, and possibly a very small addition. To that end, an article has been inserted in the warrant
requesting the amount of $5,000 to begin professional planning. If a plan can be found to allow handicapped
access to all floors, an application can be made for grant money. To postpone correction of the library
building problems much longer would be thoroughly irresponsible.
Demand for library services continues to be heavy, with 57,413 items circulated this year, including 21,763
from the adult section, 34,368 from the children's section, and 1,282 from the young adult. Four preschool
story hours are filled to capacity, and 4,883 books have been checked out by Primary School classes and
nursery classes during their bi-weekly visits. The summer reading program was more popular than ever, with
very brisk circulation of books and full registration-for the many activities and programs. Circulation of video
cassettes made available by the regional library system has become a popular new service. A different
collection, with a variety of titles, is sent to Ritter each month. One cassette at a time may be checked out for 24
hours.
Lunenburg is now participating in the Consumer Health Information Resource Project (CHIRP). A grant
obtained by Arthur Kissner of Fitchburg Library provides a health information network involving public,
academic and hospital libraries as well as several area health agencies. Lunenburg is receiving funds for a
number of new books on health topics and the services of a medical librarian as collection consultant as a part
of this program, which will enhance the library's ability to meet health information needs.
Acquisition of a Rainbow computer was considered when Digital offered one at half price. These plans
were shelved due to lack of a clear need and of interest on the part of potential users in a computer for public
use, plus the fact that a Rainbow would not be compatible with the regional library computer network. Future
automation of library functions continues to be studied, as well as the idea of a computer for public use.
The Friends of the Library have continued their good work with generous contributions of time and funds.
Among their gifts this year were: passes to the Fine Arts and Science Museums in Boston; attractive new
boxes for shelving back issues of magazines; Christmas wreaths; rental books; story hour supplies; and award
books, materials and programs for the summer reading program. Funds were raised by means of yard sales,
book sales, bake sales, and a boutique table, as well as a membership drive. The Friends, who are always
happy to welcome new members, have become an indispensable part of the library.
Gifts given in memory of Ralph Prescott Benjamin to the Lunenburg Memorial Fund have been used by
the library, at the request of his family, to establish a lending art print collection. A variety of prints are
available to borrow for 6 weeks at a time. Three original paintings have been given to the library to display in
memory of Benjamin by the artist, Nelde Drumm. Books have been donated in memory of: Lionel A. Grise, Jr.,
Robert J. Larkin, John and Helen Morin, and John Charles Thomas.

Building Committee
Town Hall problems and prospects have been the focus of a two year effort by the Building Committee.
We are currently consolidating information and recommendations for presentation to the Annual Town
Meeting.
In this past year, we contracted the services of an architectural and planning firm, Hammer Kiefer and
Todd of Cambridge. The consulting firm has done an excellent job reviewing building and space/use
problems of all town departments, boards and committees and has looked ahead to anticipate future needs.
In addition to looking at the situation in Lunenburg, the consultant was asked to survey town office
building and renovations throughout the state. The survey revealed that thirty-six towns have renovated or
added to their existing town halls in the past five years. Ten of these towns have converted existing town
buildings for Town Hall use, nineteen towns are now renovating or adding to existing town halls and seven
towns are going to town meetings with new construction or renovation plans. This survey provided the
committee with valuable information about town office solutions in other comparable towns.
Lunenburg town offices are grossly undersized, with several key committees, such as Planning and
Conservation boards housed in other locations, resulting in inefficiency and inconvenience. Because of
layout and structural problems, town offices are restricted to one floor of the Town Hall, approximately 3500
square feet. Average space need/use figures reveal that our present offices need 8500-10000 square feet for
proper workable office and record storage space.
Lunenburg faces severe problems with our existing Town Hall. The building has been allowed to
deteriorate to the point that major structure repairs are required. Besides structural problems and
overcrowding, there are building code issues, lack of security for vital records and inadequate meeting space.
These problems will increase dramatically within the next few years due to the rapid building and population
growth within the town.
In their space needs study, Hammer Kiefer and Todd singled out the Police and Fire Departments as
facing major building and space problems. The needs of the Police Department are being addressed by the
Committee this year. The Fire Department needs must be addressed within the next five to seven years.
At this stage, the Building Committee and Hammer Kiefer and Todd have consolidated information
gathered over the past two years, and are currently looking at several options. Two of which are: renovating
the existing Town Hall and building a new Police facility on Memorial Drive, or building a new Town Office
facility and renovate the present Town Hall for the Police Department. Architects figures are still preliminary,
but estimates will run over a million dollars for either project.
The voters at Town Meeting will face a critical decision regarding Town Office facilities, the sentimental
value and renovations of the existing building to meet the present needs, or the building of a new facility with
flexibility and room for expansion to sustain the future needs of the Town of Lunenburg.

BACK - AND ALSO FORWARD:
We tend to remember the good things and
forget the bad. So do the people who tell us about
those days.
Around the turn of the century horses provided
most of the transportation in cities and villages. No
one was writing environmental impact statements
then or we might have had to ban the horses even
before they were replaced by street cars and
automobiles. The carts they pulled had thin,
wooden or ironclad wheels that cut deep ruts in the
unpaved, poorly-drained streets when it rained. And
what did they do with the carcass when a horse died
on the street? In many areas, to avoid digging a big
hole, they hauled it to a barge and rolled it off into
the so-called pristine river. Another common
practice was to tote it to a municipal dump.
Ah, the dump! Even a stranger in town could
identify its location from its everlasting plume of
smoke and noxious odors. Some marksmen
remember shooting rats at the dump with their .22s.
Dumps were almost always located on low ground.
When the melting snow in the spring filled the rivers
and they overflowed into the dumps, the ground
came alive with rats looking for dry homes in
basements.
George G. Munyon, Jr., Highway Superintendent
Many claimed the auto would never replace the horse. But they reckoned without the innovative ideas of
Henry Ford. But even as little as a half-century ago many drivers still had to put their cars up on blocks for the
winter as they enviously watched their neighbors' horse-drawn sleighs skim over snow-packed roads. Since
then, snowfighting has envolved into a science.
The first time a street was paved no one complained about the inconvience. Memories of mud in the
spring and dust in the summer were too vivid. Today no one wants a slice of the criticism engendered by the
inconvenience and expense of repaving. Drivers know that their cars won't last forever but many seem to
expect their pavements to do so,
Public water supplies and sewers generally preceded the paving, with big cities, of necessity, getting
theirs before small ones. Even today people in many new subdivisions have to depend on their own wells and
septic tanks. Years ago, shallow wells and cisterns to capture rainwater became unsatisfactory when large
numbers of people lived in close proximity, tossing their garbage into the streets and using privies in back of
their houses. The average life span was less than 40 years, and mortality rates were four times those of today.
Summer was a bad time for small children. They were especially at hazard to the hot-weather diseases.
Good old-fashioned dysentery, cholera and typhoid. The practice of medicine is a noble profession. But the
people who have saved lives on a grand scale over the past century are the civil engineers and the plumbers.
For a long time a high water tank was the hallmark of a progressive community. But new generations were
born, people who took for granted the pure, clean water that flowed so effortlessly from the tap. They never
tasted the questionable water from a shallow well and they began to complain about "those ugly tanks on
spindly legs". Thus were born today's fluted and golf-ball standpipes. No matter, they store the same pure
water and also decorate the landscape.
Now it's time to move on. We have a rich heritage worthy of our pride. Let's continue the good work.
(Excerpts from editorial by R.R. Fleming, Editor-in-Chief AQWA Reporter)

CRIME RATE DROPS FOR 1985
In keeping with the national trend - crime decreased 4% nationwide - Lunenburg showed a significant
decrease of 9.5% in all crimes reported. This reduction is partially attributed to the participation of the
members of our community who report suspicious activity and traffic problems throughout the Town. With
this information, we are able to detect and apprehend offenders, prevent some crimes from occurring, and
patrol those areas of Town that are troubled when it does the most good, I urge the members of the community
to continue to call the Police Station with this valuable information.
Even though Lunenburg showed a 9.5% crime decrease, it is clearly evident that the workload of the
Police Department is increasing. The work quarters of the Police Station are quite small and inadequate. So
that the Police Department can continue to function in an orderly and professional manner, I recommend that
the Town start looking into a new Police Station. I am also recommending that one man be added to the Police
Department to replace Patrolman John Baker who was retired in October of 1985. John joined the force in
February of 1969 and served as a reserve patrolman until March of 1978, when he was elevated to a regular full-
time police patrolman. In October of 1979, John was struck down by a drunk driver. The injuries he received
were quite extensive and responsible for his having to retire from the Police Department. John was a very
professional and dedicated police officer. He will be greatly missed by all members of the Department. Best
wishes in your retirement, John.
With all the new homes going up in Town and sale of current property, the Police and Fire Departments
are conducting a program to make the residents of the Town aware of the importance of visably numbering
their homes. Unfortunately, a large percentage of homes in Lunenburg are either not numbered, numbered
improperly, or located far enough off the street that the number on the house cannot be read from the street. In
the event of a police, fire or rescue call, valuable time would be wasted by responding EMT's trying to locate a
home that isn't numbered. Someone's life or home may hang in the balance while we try to locate the home
without benefit of something as simple and inexpensive as numbers. It is suggested that numbers at least
three inches in height of a contrasting color be applied to the home in a manner making them most visible from
the road. If your home is far enough off the road that the numbers cannot be easily seen from the road,
additional numbers should be posted at the street near the driveway.
I would also like to notify newcomers to the community of our crime provention program and encourage
them to call the Police Station and speak with our crime prevention officer if they would like to have a
home/business security survey done.
I am pleased to report that through the efforts of all our police officers involved in traffic and speed
enforcement programs, Lunenburg has received a certificate of Traffic Safety awarded by the National Safety
Council. We finished in third place, competing with other communities our size (paved road miles and
population). Training continues to be a high priority of this Department with all full-time and reserve officers
participating.
Patrolman James P. Marino was appointed quartermaster for the Lunenburg Police Department.
Through his efforts, our Department entered a Best Dressed Police Department contest, with more than two
hundred police departments competing nationwide. We tied with four other departments for third place and
received a Certificate of Achievement in recognition of our distinctive uniform program and the positive image
it projects. I would like to thank the Knights of Columbus for their contribution and assistance in this
endeavor.
I would also like to thank the Memorial Fund for their donation to the Department of five speaker mikes for
our portable radios used by our officers.

Attorney's office and the Lunenburg Police Department.

Fire Department
During the year 1985, this Department responded to 523 calls for assistance. The number of calls
increased by 53 from the previous year. The calls were classified as follows: Motor Vehicle Accident - 19;
Water/Ice Rescue - 12; Medical - 4; Mutual Aid - 14; Box Alarms - 26; Rubbish - 6; Structure - 54; Hazardous
Materials - 4; Motor Vehicles - 27; Investigations - 23; Brush/Grass - 35; Service - 4; Telephonic - 10; Illegal
Burning - 10; Good Intent - 3; Outside Electricial - 15; Gas Leaks - 3; False - 3; Ambulance - 251.
Due to increasing population and the current building boom, we are experiencing a rise in our number of
calls and also our number of inspections. A lot of these inspections are mandated by the Building Codes and
Fire Prevention regulations. We find ourselves coming up short with not having enough time to perform all
inspections that are required. Paperwork generated by these inspections continues to be a problem as well as
records keeping. We must strive to handle these problems if we are going to continue to run an efficient Fire
Department. We work very closely with the Building Department and it is experiencing the same types of
problems.
Chimney fires continue to be one of our biggest problems. I ask that if you have any questions on chimney
or stove safety, please call me or the Building Inspector for safety information.
The best way to fight fires is to prevent them before they happen. Co-operation among homeowners,
builders, and the Fire Department working together and adhering the codes and by-laws, together with a
strong fire prevention program, can and will reduce fire loss and save lives. The best way to accomplish this is
through public education. This is the route we are trying to take. There is really no need of anyone having to
suffer the consequences of a fire if they practice good fire prevention in their homes.
Our Fire Prevention Program in the schools has unfortunately had to be put off this year due to a lack of
available people to carry on this program. Hopefully, this program will be implemented again in the future if we
can acquire the needed manpower. This program is very critical to the teaching of fire safety involving our
young citizens.
Space needs in our building continue to hamper our operation. When we replace apparatus we face a
problem of fitting this new equipment into our existing facility, as new apparatus, due to new technolgy, must
be larger and more sophisticated than the older trucks. As it is now, we are very cramped in our quarters. Our
present station is also lacking in adequate space for storage and work areas. We hope the study of housing
needs in Town will address this problem in the near future.
The Fire Chief's office issued 315 permits for various reasons such as smoke detectors, oil burners, gas
installations, etc. Approximately 500 to 600 additional inspections were done by the Fire Chief. The
inspections we do help insure safety to the general public and the homeowner.
The ambulance service continues to generate about one-half of our calls. We have 18 EMT's who man our
ambulance on a 24 hour basis. These people are all firefighters and maintain a very high level of medical
training. They are dedicated to serving the people of Lunenburg in this service as well as the firefighting
aspect of their job. This year, due to the age and number of runs it makes the 1977 ambulance must be
replaced. It is obvious in the ambulance business that the vehicle must be in top shape at all times. This service
is a vital part of our protection to the public.
The Fire Department is a vital cog in the public safety wheel of the Town. We have strived to provide the
Townspeople with the best of fire protection. The local Boards and the Townspeople have demonstrated in
the past their willingness to supply us with the tools and manpower to protect the Town. As our Town grows,
we have to keep our public safety agencies up to par. I would like to take this opportunity to thank all Boards of
the Town and all who make our job a little easier. I would also like to thank all the firefighters of our
Department for their cooperation and dedication to this Department during the past year.

On October 4, 1985, the Massachusetts Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters amended
the Code to prohibit the use of solders which contain lead in potable water supply systems in buildings. The
Inspector attended an educational seminar at the New England Laborers Training Center in Hopkinton where
instructions, demonstrations and proper enforcement of this new ruling were discussed. Test kits were also
provided to the Inspectors at the seminar.
Mr. Joe Risi, Executive Secretary of the Massachusetts Board, acknowledged that harmful amounts of
lead from containing solders used in potable water systems was a danger to public health and it is especially
hazardous to small children. In recognition of this fact, a lead limit of 50-parts per-billion by weight (PPB) has
been established for safe drinking water by the Environmental Protection Agency. This is to insure that lead
from water does not contribute more than 33% of the known daily permissible intake of lead for a one to three
year old child drinking one liter (2.12 pints) of water per day. Furthermore, after January 1986, lead-free solder
must be used on all new piping. A random test sample of a new soldered joint conveying drinking water
through copper piping will be part of the plumbing inspection. Violation of the Code will immediately be
reported to the Massachusetts Plumbing Board. This message was strongly conveyed to all Plumbing
Inspectors. In November, a letter from Fitchburg Gas and Electric Light Company was received requesting
that no new gas installation permits be issued unless a log sheet signed by Fitchburg Gas and Electric Light
Company was presented to the Gas Inspector by the plumbers and gas fitters. Exception to this rule would be
only the replacement of existing gas equipment.

HISTORICAL COMMISSION
The Lunenburg Historical Commission meets on the second Wednesday of each month in the Historical
Society Building where a file is kept containing minutes of meetings, communications from the
Massachusetts Historical Commission, and other pertinent materials.
During the past year, this Commission has completed the first phase of an inventory of historic sites in
Lunenburg. Following guidelines laid down by the Massachusetts Historical Commission, forty-eight homes,
public buildings, and places such as the Town Pound, Bandstand, Watering Trough, etc. have been
documented. The Town history "Lunenburg, the Heritage of Turkey Hills 1718-1978", written several years
ago in commemoration of Lunenburg's two hundred and fiftieth anniversary, was a great help in this
undertaking. Inventory sheets, including a picture of each site, were sent to Boston as a first step toward
applying for inclusion on the National Register of Historic Places, if this should be considered desirable.
Upon advice from the Massachusetts Historical Commission, it has been decided to concentrate our
efforts this year on the Town Center, with particular focus on the present Town Hall. The Lunenburg Historical
Commission is very interested in the preservation of this historic and beautiful building for use in some way. If
the Town Center area should be accepted by the National Register of Historic Places, it would be possible to
apply for state and/or federal funds towards the renovation of this landmark. The Commission recognizes this
building as one of many historically and architecturally significant assets of the Town of Lunenburg.

Town Management Study Committee
Members of the Town Management Study Committee are Melissa Cobb, Chairman, Diane Allen, John
Floros, Gail Wolejko, and James Van Buren, replacing Walter Keeler. This Committee was established in 1983
and charged with the task of investigating the establishment of a Selectmen-Town Manager form of
government. The past three years have resulted in the acceptance of Chapter 41, Section 23A of the General
Laws, establishing the position of Executive Secretary.
Based on the results of our survey, last year's Committee recommended that the Town form a Charter
Commission. The first step in this process was obtaining signatures of 15% of the registered voters at the last
state election. A total of 751 signatures were thus required and on October 31, 1985, the Board of Registrars
certified 828 signatures on petitions which had been circulated throughout the town. This enables the
question, "Shall a commission be elected to frame a charter for the Town of Lunenburg?" to be placed on the
1986 ballot. Names of those desiring to run for the elective position of member of the Charter Commission will
also be placed on the ballot. If the question passes, the nine candidates receiving the highest number of votes
are then elected to form the Charter. By state law, the Commission must be funded with a minimum of $2,000
and must complete its Charter Proposal within eighteen months. A review of similar-sized towns by the
Executive Office of Communities and Development indicates realistic expenses in the ten to fifteen thousand
dollar range. The Charter Commission's final report will be placed on the next regular municipal ballot after
the final report is completed.
We recommend a Charter Commission consider the following issues and/or changes in town
government:
- enlarging the Board of Selectmen;
- establishing the strength and nature of a Town Manager/Administrator position
- possibly separating the Board of Health from the Board of Selectmen;
- possibly consolidating and/or restructuring of Town departments;
- reviewing budget procedures to ensure efficiency;
- increasing communication among departments and boards.

Montachusett Regional Vocational
Technical School District
The year 1985 marked the fifth anniversary of the birth of Proposition 2½. What has happened to
Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School since 1980? Many things happened in 1985, and some
things did not happen that should have. It was hoped that three towns would be accepted into the District--
Orange, Petersham and Westminster. After five years of knocking at the door for entrance, Orange withdrew
its petition to join and is planning instead to join the Franklin County Technical School District in Turners
Falls. Unless Fitchburg decides to vote approval for Petersham and Westminster, they, too, may decide to
apply elsewhere. This would be a severe blow to Monty Tech since the School suffers from the same drop in
enrollment as is being experienced by other schools in the District. This would result in elimination of some
vocational programs offered at the School at a time when the economy is at an all time high in the
Commonwealth, and when well-trained young men and women are needed in trade/industry. This region
would sorely miss one of its most outstanding assets, a regional vocational technical school with a wide
offering of vocational problems.
The Committee, administration and staff have been busy with acquisition of new equipment,
implementation of new programs, honoring students for their accomplishments, embellishing the School
both inside and out, and generally, "fighting the good fight". The staff spent time and energy on self-
evaluation for the New England Association of Schools and Colleges.
The Superintendent-Director visited Finance Committee/Boards of Selectmen/Town Meetings/City
Councils seeking approval for Monty Tech of Section 12 (Equal Education Opportunity funds), and Section 13
(Professional Development) of Chapter 188, the new state Education Reform Bill. The two cities and seven of
the cities voted approval.
Mr. & Mrs. Daniel Proctor of Lunenburg won the drawing for this year's house building program, a project
conducted by Monty Tech each year. To date, ten houses have been built in the District, each in a distinct and
different architectural style. As usual, we are grateful for donations of equipment, supplies and materials from
businesses and industries. These materials have been put to good use in the various vocational shops.
Principles of Technology, a new science program, was developed and implemented, and has been a
source of great enthusiasm for the enrolled students and the instructor whose work made the offering
possible. This is a two-year program for Grades 11 and 12 built around the concept of a technically valid,
unified approach to the study of basic energy systems--mechanical, fluid, electrical and thermal. Grants have
been received in the amount of $213,597 allowing for opening of the Principles of Technology program,
robotics experience for electronic students, expansion of the dental laboratory technician program,
parenting/counseling classes for students, and new micro computers for science and library use.
In October, the Lunenburg Board of Selectmen regretfully accepted the resignation of Charles F. Valera
as Lunenburg's representative to the Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School Committee. Mr.
Valera served in this capacity since 1976 and his dedicated service to the Town is greatly appreciated by the
Townspeople. On November 18, 1985, by a joint convention of the Board of Selectmen and School
Committee, Charles J. Darling was appointed to serve as Lunenburg's representative until the next Annual
Town Election.
Your new Representative reports that Monty Tech is, at this time, healthily in transition in most aspects.
The District Committee is in the process of selecting a new Superintendent-Director to replace Charlotte
Scott who is completing a commendable performance at that post. New contract/agreéments have,
essentially, at this writing been agreed upon for both our teachers and custodians. Mr. Darling goes on to state
that the District Committee itself is comprised of excellent representatives, and in general "keeps the ship on
course". The buildings and facilities are in excellent condition and in being a part of Monty Tech, Lunenburg is
providing a vital piece in the educational puzzle confronting our young people today. There are currently 16
young women and 21 young men in attendance from the Town of Lunenburg. Mr. Darling urges the Town's
continued support of Monty Tech.

Purchase of Services Study Committee
Article 35 of the 1985 Annual Town Meeting voted to have a study committee to be appointed by the Board
of Selectmen and Moderator to study a possible amendment to the Town By-laws which would require that no con-
tract for the purchase of services amounting to $4,000 or more shall be awarded by a Town Department without first
going out for bid. Appointed to the Purchase of Services Study Committee were James Hays, Chairman; Joseph
Giovino, Secretary; and David Rodgers. Each member met with various Town officials to solicit input and recom-
mendations. The Committee feels that a regimented bid process for services would inhibit Town departments by
creating more paperwork and would remove from Town department heads the ability to make timely and cost-effective
decisions. Currently, State law dictates the bidding process for much the Town does, services is an exception. We
found most department heads now routinely go out for bid on services when a significant sum of money is involved.
It is recommended by this Committee that all Town department heads use the bid process forservices when they feel
it is in the best interests of the Town, and when it will save the Town money. We feel that setting a dollar limit
would not be practical and would remove the autonomy from our Town officials to act on our behalf. Any effort to
further encumber operations by adding another level of bureaucracy would be counter-productive. The Committee
recommends that no dollar limit be set, but that each department head, when they deem advisable, seek bids for
services that would result in a timely, cost-effective method of purchase.
