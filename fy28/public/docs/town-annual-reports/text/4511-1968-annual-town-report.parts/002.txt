Article 14. To see if the Town will vote to amend Section 9, Article IX, of the By-laws by deleting said Section 9 and in-
serting in place thereof "Earth Removal Ordinance for the Town of Lunenburg dated January 10, 1968", or act anything thereon.
VOTED UNANIMOUSLY THAT THE TOWN AMEND SECTION 9, ARTICLE IX, OF THE BY-LAWS BY DELETING SAID SECTION9
AND INSERTING IN PLACE THEREOF A NEW SECTION 9 AS SET FORTH IN PRINTED MATTER ENTITLED "EARTH REMOVAL
ORDINANCE FOR TOWN OF LUNENBURG, JANUARY 10, 1968", AS FOLLOWS:
ARTICLE I
1. Definitions for the purpose of this chapter
(a) "Earth" shall include soil, loam, sand, gravel, and stone.
(b). "Board" shall mean the Selectmen of the Town of Lunenburg.
2. Purpose
The purpose of this chapter is to protect the health, safety and welfare of the public by regulating the removal of earth
so as not to create dust, washouts, noise, ponds, and other hazardous land conditions.
3. Enforcement
Any person, firm or corporation wilfully violating, disobeying or refusing to comply with any of the provisions of this
ordinance shall be prosecuted under the terms of General Laws, Ter. Ed., Chapter 40, Section 21, Paragraph 17, and shall be subject
to a fine of not more than $50.00 for the first offense, not more than $100.00 for the second offense, and not more than $200.00 for
any subsequent offense. Each day during any portion of which such violation is allowed to continue shall be considered a separate
offense. The Board may revoke or suspend the permit of any person, firm or corporation holding a permit under this ordinance if such
person, firm, or corporation violates, disobeys, or fails to comply with any of its provisions.
4. Existing soil removal operations
Any person, firm, or corporation engaged in the operation of soil removal on the effective date of this Chapter may con-
tinue such operation for 30 days after such date, but shall, within said 30 days apply for a permit in accordance with this Chapter.
At the end of such 30 days the operation shall be governed by the terms of the permit issued or, if no such permit is issued, shall cease.
5. Validity
The invalidity of any section of this ordinance shall not invalidate any other section or provision thereof.
6. Permit required
No earth in an amount in excess of ten (10) cubic yards shall be moved from any parcel of landwithin the Town of Lunen-
burg to another parcel either within or without the Town unless such removal is authorized by a permit issued by the Board. No earth
in an amount in excess of ten (10) cubic yards shall be moved in less than ten (10) cubic yard quantities over any period of time,
deemed by the Board to be for the purpose of evading any of the provisions of this ordinance. No earth removal permit shall be issued
until an application therefor is filed with the Board and the Board has held a public hearing on the application, after publication of
the time and place, and subject matter sufficient for identification, in the newspaper having general distribution in the town once
in each of two successive weeks, the first publication to be not less than fourteen (14) days prior to said public hearing, and notice
of such hearing shall be sent by mail to owners of abutting property prior to said public hearing.
7. Application for permit
Application for such permits shall be accompanied by exhibits and documentation deemed necessary by the Board for the
proper issuance of a permit, which shall include the following:
a. Name and address of the legal owner of the land in question. b. Name and address of petitioner, if different, and
names and addresses of owners of abutting property. C. An adequate and sufficient plan of land prepared by a registered engineer
or land surveyor and indicating: tract boundaries, adjacent streets and roads, the limits of the proposed excavation, the locations
of all structures within two hundred (200) feet of said limits, original topography of five-foot contours, proposed final contours of
five-foot intervals, and the location and proposed use of all structures and buildings to be used in connection with the removal
operation. Any of the above provisions may be waived at the discretion of the Board, d. Statement of plans for the disposal of rock,
tree stumps and other waste materials, and for the drainage of the site and excavation during and after the removal operation.
-70-
===PAGE 74===
8. Reasons for denial
No permit for earth removal shall be issued if such removal will (a) endanger the general health, safety, or welfare or
constitute a public nuisance, (b) result in detriment to the normal use of adjacent property by reason of noise, dust, erosion or
vibration, (c) result in traffic hazard in residential areas, or congestion and physical damage to public ways.
9. Duration of permit
No permit for any earth removal shall be issued for more than one (1) year's duration, and may be renewed thereafter.
10. Control of operation
In approving the issuance of a permit, the Board shall impose all reasonable requirements which shall be deemed necessary
by the Board and may include: grading, seeding, and planting, fencing necessary for public safety and screening unsightly op-
erations, methods of removal, location and use of structures, hours of operation, routes of transportation of material removed, con-
trol of drainage, control of dust, disposition of waste material incident to the operation, due consideration for the preservation of the
town's natural resources including inland swamps and waters and the aesthetic values, and final appearance of the property on com-
pletion of the operation.
11. Bond of Security
The Board may require suitable bond or other security adequate to assure compliance with any of the provisions of this by-law.
12. Exemptions
This ordinance shall not be construed to apply to any such removal incidental to the construction or alteration of any
structure for which a building permit has been issued, or to the removal incidental to the installation of any cesspool or septic tank
for which a permit has been issued by the Board of Health, or to the grading or development of any approved subdivision or public way.

QUESTION NO. 3 - PROPOSED AMENDMENT TO THE CONSTITUTION
Do you approve of the adoption of an amendment to the constitution summarized below, which was approved by the General Court
in a joint session of the two branches held July 20, 1966, received 225 votes in the affirmative and 1 in the negative, and in a joint
session of the two branches held June 15, 1967, received 210 votes in the affirmative and 2 in the negative?
Summary - The proposed amendment of the Constitution defines the circumstances in which a Governor's inability to perform his
functions shall cause his office to become vacant, and it establishes a procedure for making the necessary determinations. The office
will become vacant following a declaration of disability by the Governor himself or by the Chief Justic and a majority of the Associate
Justices of the Supreme Judicial Court, or such other body as the Legislature may provide. The vacancy will terminate four days after
the Governor declares that no disability exists, unless (1) within that period the Chief Justice and majority of the Associate Justices,
or such other body provided by the Legislature, should make a contrary declaration and (2) within another stated period the Legislature,
by two-thirds of each branch present and voting, then determines the issue contrary to the Governor's declaration.
The same procedure will also apply to a Lieutenant Governor who in the case of a vacancy is performing the Governor's duties.
The proposed amendment also requires the election of a Governor for the unexpired balance of the four-year term if a vacancy
in the office of Governor as described in the proposed amendment continues for a six-month period expiring more than five months
prior to a biennial state election other than an election for Governor.

QUESTION NO. 4 - LAW PROPOSED BY INITIATIVE PETITION
Do you approve of a law summarized below, upon which the House of Representatives did not vote and upon which the Senate
did not vote?
Summary - The proposed law provides that the official ballot in the biennial State election for the year 1970 shall contain a
question asking the voters whether there shall be a convention in 1971 to (1) revise, alter or amend the constitution of the Common-
wealth on the following subjects, insofar as they relate to the structure of government, and no others: the Executive Branch; the
General Court; the Executive Council; the government of cities, towns and counties and their relationship to each other and to the
government of the Commonwealth; (2) simplify and rearrange the constitution, and (3) provide methods of amendment thereof. The
question will state that the convention shall not consider or propose any measure which relates to the Massachusetts Declaration of
Rights, the provisions of the Massachusetts constitutions concerning the "Judiciary Power '(other than provisions respecting the Executive
Council) or any of the excluded matters enumerated in Article XLVIII, II, Section 2 of the Massachusetts constitution. The question
will fix the number of delegates to the convention at 150, of whom 20 members shall be members of the General Court;the remaining
130 members shall be elected by the voters in the manner summarized below. The question will also state that the convention shall
conclude its business not later than 120 days from its first session.
The proposed law provides that if a majority of the votes on the foregoing question is in the affirmative, the convention shall be
held in July, 1971 to revise, alter or amend the constitution on the matters enumerated above only. The House of Representatives
and the Senate shall each elect as delegates 10 of its members, not more than 6 from each branch coming from the same political party.
Of the remaining 130 delegates, 10 shall be elected at large and 120 shall be elected from the 40 senatorial districts, 3 from each.
Provision is made for the manner of nominating and electing these 130 delegates, without party or political designation, for filling
vacancies occurring in the position of any delegate, from whatever source chosen, and for the conduct of the convention's business.
Any revisions, alterations and amendments of the Massachusetts constitution adopted by the convention shall be submitted to the
people at the State election in 1972 for their ratification and adoption in such manner as the convention may direct. If a majority
of the people voting thereon ratify and adopt such revision, alteration or amendment, the constitution shall be deemed to be changed
accordingly.
The proposed law also provides that if the vote on the question to be submitted at the biennial State election in 1970 is in the
affirmative, the Governor shall appoint a preparatory commission of 5 members to compile data to aid the convention in the discharge
of its duties. Each member of the commission shall receive compensation of $10,000 and the commission may expend a sumnot in
excess of $200,000, as the Governor may approve, for its expenses.

Extension Service
The 4-H Town Committee held their annual electionwith the following results: Mrs. C. Edward Stillman, Chairman;
Mrs. Walter Maki, Treasurer; Mrs. Robert Loughlin, Secretary; Mrs. Robert Logan, Publicity Chairman; and Mrs. Joseph
Chartrand, Mrs. C. Edward Stillman and Mr. Richard Dustin, Advisory Committee. The 4-H Town Committee met
several times during the year when the need arose.
Following are the 4-H Clubs that met in 1968 and their leaders: Beginner's Knitting Club, Mrs. Carl Blanchard;
Cooking and Sewing Club, Karen Ward and Christine DeBettencourt; Woodworking Club, Mr. Philip Pearson; Go-Go
Sewer's Club, Mrs. Ernest Haueisen; Kitchen Kapers Club, Mrs. Dean Gauntlett and Mrs. Roger Raboin.
The activities of these Clubs are briefly outlined as follows: The Beginner's Knitting Club made squares for an

Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical High School
This report is intended to summarize the development and progress of the Montachusett Regional Vocational Tech-
nical School District to date. The District comprises the following two citiesand nine towns: Ashburnham, Ashby,
Barre, Hubbardston, Fitchburg, Gardner, Harvard, Lunenburg, Royalston, Sterling and Winchendon.
On May 22nd, an initial Bond Issue request was presented to the communities in the District and the following
were the results: City Councils in Fitchburg and Gardner approved; Barre approved 77-41; Ashburnham disapproved by
85-83, two votes; Sterling voted approximately two to one for approval and the remaining towns chose not to call a
town meeting, thus approving. When a town disapproves, that request for the Bond Issue is nullified.
At the second request voted by the School Committee on June 24th, only two towns meetings, with Fitchburg
and Gardner unanimously approving via Council action. This time, however, Ashburnham at a large town meeting,
very strongly endorsed the School. However, Barre defeated the Bond Issue vote by 128-99, 29 votes.
The third request was made by the School Committee on October 23rd. At this vote, Barre approved and the only
other town having a town meeting, Ashburnham disapproved by a vote of 158 for and 196 against; thus defeating the
Bond Issue request by 38 votes.
Early in September, a special meeting with our area Legislators was held in the School Committee office, and it
was agreed that there would be bipartisan introduction into both houses of the Legislature of a bill that will make it
possible for initial construction; thus permitting the other communities to continue participating in the plans for the con-
struction of the Regional Vocational Technical School. In addition, it was agreed that our Legislators would support a
bill to modify existing legislation to make more equitable State grants for construction to the Regional School. Hence,
in December, legislation was introduced that would allow the School District Committee to vote out Ashburnham after
their defeat of the Bond Issue authorization and after their request to leave the District without having another round
of town meetings, thus permitting the Montachusett Regional District (with the approval of the Emergency Finance Board)
to issue the $6,500,000 Bonds. If this legislation can become a reality early in 1969, then there exists the possibility
of maintaining the original schedule to open in the Fall of 1970.
During this same period, endorsements for the School have been received from Unions and industrial, civic and
religious organizations. Fifteen Craft Committees were organized, representing 103 industrial ishments and Unions,
consisting of 204 participants. Their activities were demonstrated at the Annual Advisory Committee dinner held in
October. The purpose was to lustrate the structural relationship between the Craft Committee and the School Committee;
to apprise the School Committee of the role of the Craft Committee and recommendations with regard to shop, laboratory,
planning, equipment, scope and content of the craft courses, as it applies to local industry.
Since the purchase of oursite on Route 2A, near the Route 140 and Route 2 interchanges, in December of 1967 most
of our efforts have been concentrated on the school design and passage of our Bond Issue request. Prior to submitting
our request for the first Bond Issue authorization, our School Committee had extensive changes made in the design to
reduce the cost. The effort on the part of our School Committee was to create a school housing 1000 day school students
and 2000 adults, afternoons and evenings, but to develop these facilities so that the most modern teaching techniques
could be utilized in teaching academic as well as career programs. In May, as the result of the approval of the Bureau
of School Building Assistance of our Educational Specifications and our preliminary drawings, the School Committee

- SPECIAL EDUCATION TRAINABLE CLASS -
For some years, Mrs. Gladys Brown has taught the Special Class in Lunenburg. This class meets
the needs of those children who could not comfortably meet the challenges of the normal classroom.
Because of smaller class size, and the talents and training of the teacher, each child is given the
opportunity to work at his own level, and at his own speed. Despite this individualized approach,
it had become apparent that there were a number of children in our area who were unable to benefit
from this type of learning experience.
These children were for many years kept at home or institutionalized. In recent years, educators
have come to know that a great deal can be done to help them. Because the state now requires that
the educational needs of all children be met, special training programs have been developed to
instruct teachers in this field. The need for a class of this type is limited, but very real. This
year, for the first time in Lunenburg, we have met this need by instituting a Trainable Class. Plan-
ning and inauguration of the Trainable Class is a result of the cooperative effort of Groton,
Townsend-Ashby, and Lunenburg.
Miss Wilma Kibler, a recent graduate of the Special Education Division of the State College at
Fitchburg, is working with the six children enrolled in the class. There are two from Groton, two
from Lunenburg, and one each from Townsend and Ashby. The children range in age from seven to
thirteen. Educational costs for this program are shared by the participating towns.
Currently the class meets in a room on the lower level of the Annex Building. The children
benefit from a wide range of educational experience. Music and Art supervisors visit the class
weekly. The State College is interested in our program, and each week several students from the
Special Education Department of the college visit to observe, learn, and assist Miss Kibler.
Emphasis in the curriculum is on preparing the children to function safely and happily in
society. Although these children will probably never learn to read proficiently, they are taught
to recognize an "emergency vocabulary" which would include a variety of signs and signals they
might meet in everyday life. Social behavior skills are developed, and rudimentary knowledge of
phonics and number concepts is taught. Self-sufficiency in personal care is emphasized, and the
children are encouraged to develop to their fullest potential.
I wish to express my appreciation to the Superintendent of Schools, the School Committee, the
Parent-Teacher Association and all school personnel for their cooperation over the past year. I would
also like to thank the parents for their cooperation and understanding throughout the year and espe-
cially during the time of double sessions. I'm sure there has been much sacrifice on their part
during this period.

Superintendent's Annual Report
Someone has said, "The struggles of mankind as a whole produce the possessions of mankind
as a whole. Something for nothing is not to be found on earth." Such a statement has particular
meaning and application in the field of education, for we truly live in an age of continuing rapid
change in all phases of life which call for ever increasing effort to meet the constantly changing
demands. Man's knowledge is actually reaching to outer space, the narrow confines of knowledge are
constantly expanding, new concepts and fields of information are ever opening, --old concepts and
accepted standards are continuously being brought into focus by youthful challenge and questioning.
Moral, social, and religious standards which were once accepted as inviolable have in many cases
become question marks. With such rapid change and apparent state of flux in our country, one can-
not be charged with the responsibilities of guiding, helping and preparing youth for future life
without recognizing that there is perhaps greater need than ever for all of us--School Committee,
Administration, Teachers, parents and young people--to work together to try to mold and develop
greater understanding of our wonderful heritage and the continued need to protect and preserve it.
In such soul-searching times, it would be unrealistic to assume that youthful questioning,
elements of dissent, and even experimentation with drugs would take place in other parts of our
society yet would leave Lunenburg unscathed. During 1968, we did experience our difficulties--the
legality of the compulsory flag salute was brought into question; the use of drugs reared its ugly
head in a limited manner yet sufficiently to alert all of us to the dangers to the youth of a won-
derful America. Horace Mann, the founding father of the Public Schools in America, used as a fun-
damental foundation of the Public School in a Democratic society the need to develop in the school
program the basic elements of good citizenship. This belief is still valid and basic to a success-
ful Democracy and will never be out-of-date. I believe firmly that we must continue both by precept
and example to inculcate good citizenship as a basic part of public school education. Dissent
within the framework of the law is welcomed, for in that there often comes much needed progress.
To cope with such growing problems in the area of Sex and Drugs, a Committee under the
leadership of Mr. John White, Middle School Guidance Counsellor, along with six staff members, was
set up to develop means of integrating into the schools' curriculum a course which could discreetly
handle the subjects of Sex and Drugs. At this time, plans have sufficiently progressed to a point
where a meaningful, well-organized program will be implemented within the curriculum with the open-
ing of the schools in September, 1969. Concurrent with this undertaking, joint meetings of school
personnel, police and clergy were held to coordinate efforts in dealing with this potential new
drug problem. In addition, parent and youth meetings were held during the year designed to help
all of us to communicate and better understand the scope of the problem and how to handle it. The
Parent-Teachers' Association was very active in helping to provide meaningful speakers and group
sessions dealing with the Drug Problem.
The effects of this unsettled period of struggle in Vietnam reached closely at home to all
of us with the untimely passing of former Lunenburg students -- Byron Johnson, Wilbur Williams,
Glenn Hubbard and Richard Brooks. We can only hope that their supreme sacrifice may help lead to
true brotherly understanding in the world in the not too distant future.
The New Middle School and Its Effect on the Lunenburg School System
The new Middle School by the beginning of 1968 had been under construction for about seven
months. The completion date had been set for September 3, 1968, so that the need to concentrate on
its integration within the school system was upon us. In addition, the impact of opening the new
school meant that many changes would occur at all levels of the school system made possible by the
elimination of overcrowded school housing conditions. The plans for such proposed progress were
conceived two to three years previously during the planning stages for the new school. The time
had come to finalize and refine these decisions to implement these objectives. A first step was to
install Mr. Floyd Barney as the new Middle School Principal beginning the duties of his assignment
on January 8, 1968. Mr. Barney, a highly respected teacher and Assistant Principal, with many years
of devoted service to the schools began his assignment enthusiastically.

While Mr. Barney was devoting his efforts in refining the Middle School Program in con-
junction with the proposed staff, studies were concurrently proceeding as to the effect the new
facilities would have on the Elementary and Secondary levels. Mr. Dustin and the Elementary staff
proceeded in the study of this area while Mr. Carey and his staff did likewise at the Secondary
level. As a result of these detailed studies, the School Committee after much consideration, made
certain decisions based upon Administrative recommendations.
First of all, the Committee voted to approve organizing the new Middle School somewhat
differently than what is usually done at these grade levels because the facilities provided were
unusual for grades 5, 6, and 7. Specifically, these students would now be grouped by ability level
in subject areas allowing the student the opportunity to move somewhat faster in the discipline in
which he was strongest and more slowly in the area where he was weakest. This concept also meant
exposing a child to more than one teacher rather than remaining in a self-contained classroom. After
five months of working under this system, we are encouraged that the correct decisions have been
made. However, final evaluation will not be reliably achieved until we have operated on full ses-
sions for about a year so that valid comparisons can be made. It is indeed an exciting new depar-
ture for us. More detailed explanations of this can be found in the Middle School Principal's
report.
Secondly, with the provision of added classrooms and specialized areas, the way was opened
for relieving overcrowded squeezed conditions, thereby allowing us to implement tentative plans
developed during the planning stages for the new school. These plans included the following:
A. Attempt to reduce class sizes at all grade levels in accordance with the
Guidelines recommended and adopted by the Willis-Harrington Report of
1965.
B. Initiate a Public School Kindergarten Program concurrent with the plan-
ned opening of the new school in September. Such a program had been
taken into consideration during the planning stages for additional
school space. The School Committee and the Superintendent felt that
the State mandate for compulsory initiation of Public School Kinder-
garten in 1972 did not make it suddenly educationally desireable at
that magic time. Rather they believed sincerely that Kindergarten is
an importantpart of Public School education and should be implemented
as soon as possible. They believed firmly in the merits of pre-school
education in a society where the Junior High student is now learning
many of the things formerly reserved for the High School student with
similar acceleration evident at both the High School and College
level. The School Committee having made its decision to implement a
Kindergarten Program, then set about to find three qualified Kinder-
garten teachers. The system was fortunate in securing the services of
a veteran primary teacher to coordinate the program, in the person of
Mrs. Marilyn Kibling, who further enhanced her knowledge of the area
by attending a full summer session of study the outstanding Elliott
Pearson School of Tufts University. With such enthusiastic leadership
and up-to-date background, we have been able to initiate a Kindergarten
Program in keeping with the very latest concepts in pre-school educa-
tion. We are proud of its progress to date.
Thirdly, the removal of Grade 7 from the High School provided sufficient space to allow
opportunities for adding such courses as Advanced Chemistry, Spanish IV, Economics, Sociology,
Accounting, and Analytical Geometry and Calculus which had been waiting for implementation to up-
date our school offerings in order to keep us truly competitive and to aid us in our efforts to
prepare for the forthcoming re-evaluation of the Lunenburg High School in the Spring or Fall of
1969. Pre-requisite to visitation of a committee from the New England Association of Colleges and
Secondary Schools to examine our curriculum and facilities. a self-evaluation by our own staff had
to be set up. The entire High Schoolstaff has been divided into committees for differentareas of the
school program and have been working diligently on this considerable assignment of critically look-
ing at our educational program since September 1968. The Self-Evaluation is involved, detailed and
requires much efforton the part of the Administration and Staff of the High School. A more detail-
ed explanation of this process is contained in the High School Principal's report and is must read-
ing for helping you to understand how your secondary school program is rated.

Fourthly, a decision was made to add another Guidance Counsellor in order to enable the
school system to maintain approximately a ratio of 400 pupils to one counsellor at the Secondary
level and 500 to one at the Elementary level. In addition, a Special Class for Trainable Handi-
capped Children was initiated to enable the school system to meet the educational needs of these
children as required by law. It is an impressive sight to see this class in action.
Fifthly, a Perceptually Handicapped survey was initiated and plans made to meet the needs
of this area.
During the past two years or so all of us have been hearing increasingly more about the
term Dyslexia. Most of us knew little about the problem, yet we were aware of the fact that despite
the best efforts of highly trained reading experts, we not infrequently encountered the youngster
who despite average intelligence failed to learn to read beyond a second or third grade level. As
a result, this student could not succeed in formal school work and found himself in an increasing
humiliating position as he grew older. Fortunately, we have now begun to learn to cope with this
problem, identified as being perceptual motor handicaps, or Dyslexia, It is estimated that from
three to five percent of the school enrollment may be thus handicapped with varying degrees of
severity. To cope with this problem, a committee was set up under the direction of Mrs. Bethel
Tapply, the School Psychologist, to survey and discover the scope of the problem. In addition,
school personnel have been informed of the problem both through conferences and in-service work.
Mrs. Frances Bradstreet, a Master Teacher in our school system for the past eight years, has as-
sumed the task of working with students discovered to have this handicap. It is felt that this
program will do much to help make life's opportunities much brighter for some youngsters than had
previously been possible, for without the ability to read, learning progress is greatly inhibited
and made almost impossible.
The implementation of all of the foregoing plans led to the total addition of twenty (20)
professional staff members to the school system. With committee decisions made on these matters
finally by late 1967 and early 1968, it was necessary to proceed to find the additional twenty
(including three Kindergarten teachers) specialized personnel as promptly as possible to insure
getting the best quality teachers. By May, practically all recruitment was completed, which enabled
us all to look forward enthusiastically toward the prospective opening of the new school in Septem-
ber and the resultant implementation of our new programs. Unfortunately, it became obvious in early
July that the new school would not be ready for occupancy in September. All staffing plans for the
entire school system had been based upon its opening. The School Committee suddenly found itself
grappling with the problem as to how schools were to operate without the facilities of the new
school. After weighing all alternatives, the most sensible solution seemed to dictate that grades
1-7 should go on double sessions in September, 1968 thereby permitting the High School to implement
its enriched programs unhampered, yet at the same time this action would permit the Middle School
to start in September with its new program as outlined above. Such a plan would make the transition
to the new school at the time it opened as smooth an operation as possible. All of these decisions
by your School Committee were difficult ones, since the Committee and the Administration were
acutely aware of the inconveniences which would be forced on a large segment of our community. Yet,
the decisions had to be made and were deemed to be the lesser of the evils. At least when the new
school opened in February, 1969, the transition would be relatively smooth with the program affect-
ed only positively by increasing the length of time children would be in school.
During all of this time, no one was more disappointed by the failure to completethe school
by September than the School Building Committee. They had met regularly and had tried continuously
to expedite the school's completion under extremely frustrating conditions. With the aid of the
Superintendent of Schools, specifications were drawn and bids sought for the extensive task of
furnishing and equipping the school, a task in itself time consuming for the administrative staff
and the Building Committee. Decisions were made to equip the school as economically yet as effi-
ciently as possible within the framework of the funds available. Much sincere appreciation goes
from all of us associated with the schools to the Building Committee Members for their long suffer-
ing and persevering efforts in providing this beautiful and exciting new facility.
The School Budget
Such decisions as outlined above naturally affected the School Budget and contributed
towards increased costs which no one wants or appreciates, yet the decisions made were basic to the
continuation of quality educational opportunities in Lunenburg.
-98-
===PAGE 105===
Times and conditions have changed dramatically in Education in the past two or three years,
particularly. Under law now the School Committee is legally obligated to collectively bargain on
wages, hours and conditions of employment with the teaching personnel or any other employees who
desire to organize. This is the third year of such negotiations which during the past year involved
many added School Committee meetings over a five month period. In addition, the Fair Labor Stand-
ards Act setting up minimum wages and hours was made applicable to all non-teaching School Depart-
ment personnel while it exempted all other Town employees. Such compulsory changes in the policies
of operating the School System have resulted not only in greater costs to the Community, but has
also resulted in at least doubling the amount of time required for School Committee meetings. One
additional alarming side effect of this is that it very likely will be increasingly difficult to
obtain the services of devoted lay citizens to serve on School Committees throughout the Common-
wealth. The operation of a School System has indeed become a big business continuously requiring
increasing time and cost as well as involving greater complexity.
Despite these dramatic changes in the demands of our educational system, the method of
financing Public School Education moves blithely on in much the same manner that it has for a hundred
years or more. The bulk of the support for education still comes from taxation on the local home-
owner. It is true that the State Sales Tax has helped relieve the local burden somewhat, yet the
actual State receipts from the Sales Tax have enabled the State to pay only about 69% of Lunenburg's
entitlement for State Aid. About 90% of School Expenditures consist of Salaries, Fuel, Lights,
Transportation or fixed costs leaving only about 10% of the budget where cuts can actually be made.
It is my strong feeling that the time has come in Lunenburg and all of the Communities of the State
to take firm, collective action on finding improved methods of financing Public School Education.
It is time for new thinking and bold progressive action, if we are to provide the type of leader-
ship needed to provide the necessary funds to meet the financial demands on Government as they are
today. I firmly believe there is need for greater communication among all departments of the Com-
munity and an increased need to understand our mutual problems so that new approaches may be found
to provide the means necessary to meet the demands of the times.

On December 22, 1968, Mrs. Margaret A. Fiske, a retired language teacher in the Lunenburg
Public Schools, passed away at the age of 81. For thirty-five years of service to the schools, she
had positively affected the lives of the boys and girls who came under her firm, demanding guidance,
tempered with human warmth. Many live on whose future lives will be richer for having been influ-
enced by this devoted teacher.
Conclusion
On October 28, 1968, Mrs. Marion M. Benson, Chairman of the School Committee, resigned to
move her residence to Green Bay, Wisconsin. Mrs. Benson was a remarkably dedicated person in ful-
filling her responsibilities as a member of the School Committee. She was a "doer" whose loss to
the School Committee has been strongly felt.
As the Superintendent of your Schools, I alone can accomplish very little without the con-
structive help of all of those associated with the schools. For a hardworking, devoted and under-
standing School Committee, I am most grateful. Without their encouragement and understanding, I
would have found it increasingly difficult to survive.
For the cooperative willingness to give of themselves on the part of the Principals,
Teachers, Clerical, Custodial and Lunchroom personnel, I am most appreciative. All of us have
conscientiously worked together with the considerable help of the taxpaying citizens and parents in
an effort to make your schools a symbol of pride to the Community.
Respectfully submitted,
RALPH W. BROWN,

Lunenburg Jr.-Sr. High School
Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School is in the
process of being evaluated this year by the New
England Association of Colleges and Secondary
Schools, a self-governing organization of educa-
tional institutions, the purpose of which is to
develop and maintain sound educational standards.
Membership in this organization signifies accredi-
tation, which means that a secondary school or
college has been found to follow basic educational
policies, practices, and standards.
Lunenburg High School received its first ac-
creditation in 1954 and was first evaluated and
accredited by standard criteria in 1959, which
criteria was established by the National Study
of Secondary School Evaluation and approved by the
New England Association of Colleges and Secondary
Schools.
There are six official, regional, accrediting
WI agencies throughout the United States, approved by
the Department of Health, Education and Welfare of

For continued membership in, and accreditation by, the Association, a school must conduct a
thorough self-evaluation at least once within every ten-year period. This self-evaluation requiring
nearly a full year of local staff participation in study, examination, and written reports of all
aspects of the school's facilities and total educational program, must then be examined by a commit-
tee of educators selected by the N.E.A.C.S.S. The visiting committee of 20-25 educators spends three
consecutive days within the school and community and submits a report of its evaluation to the Super-
intendent of Schools, members of the School Committee, the school staff, and to the N.E.A.C.S.S.
As a result of this final report to the
N.E.A.C.S.S., a school or college may (1) be granted
continued membership and accreditation for a period
of ten years, (2) be placed on periodic probation,
or (3) be removed from the list of accredited schools
for failure to satisfactorily meet the standards of
the N.E.A.C.S.S. The membership status of every
high school and college is recorded in the annual
bulletin of the Association which is sent to all
institutions of higher education in the United
States.

ance, and necessity of evaluation and accreditation.

On September 5, 1968, a Middle School program for grades 5, 6 and 7 was initiated and put
into operation by resorting to double sessions and by using the Elementary School facilities. By
the time this report is in print, we hope to be in the new school with its fine new facilities.
There is no question that double sessions are a hardship on parents, students, teachers, bus
drivers and many others. However, double sessions enabled us to implement a Middle School program
so that when the new school is ready, all the schools can make the transition to a normal school
day with a minimum of confusion.
What Is A Middle School?
The question is often asked, "What is a Middle School?" Mr. William A. Alexander, an early
proponent of the Middle School, defines it as "a school providing a program for a range of older
children, pre-adolescent, and early adolescents that builds upon the school for earlier child-
hood and is linked to, but different from the school for adolescence." Mr. Emmett L. Williams
states, "the Middle School consists of the organizational and program arrangements for pupils
ranging in age from 9 through 14 -- ages 10 through 13 being the most usual." Definitions of the
emerging Middle School concept could probably fill this town report and perhaps none of them
would be suited to the needs of Lunenburg.
We were faced with the question, "how can we best serve the educational needs of the pupils
in Lunenburg?" In organizing the Middle School program we also had to keep in focus the fact that
the present school programs were doing an excellent job in preparing our youth.
With these facts in mind, an attempt was made to combine the best features of the self-
contained idea of the Elementary School with the best features of the specialization of the sec-
ondary school. Also, the Middle School is not a separate entity in the school system. Continuity
in our educational policies must be preserved and fostered from Grades 1 through 12.
The Middle School Program
Although, we have retained much of our excellent reading program and our traditional subject
disciplines, we have attempted to build flexibility in our program. We have moved away from the
traditional self-contained one teacher concept toward limited departmentalization, and ability
grouping by subject area rather than by a single group in all disciplines. Under this program,
it is quite possible that a child could be in an average group in one discipline, but in a higher
group in another discipline area.
It also exposes the child to many more teachers and students throughout the school year. New
disciplines have been added to the Middle School program which we feel will make school more
pleasant and meaningful to the students. Students in grade 7 are now being exposed to French or
Spanish. With our shrinking world it is almost necessary to have some command of a second lan-
guage. Not all of our students will pursue a language on the secondary level, but if they should
decide to do so, they will have a better foundation.
We have also added Home Economics for girls and Industrial Arts for boys at grade levels
5, 6, and 7. In addition to the educational aspect, programing Home Economics and Industrial Arts
for youngsters at this age level should bring them into touch with everyday experiences that are
necessary for a richer life.
Educational, scientific, and technological advances have brought about changes in the home
and family pattern which need to be met. The Home Economics program is attempting to keep pace
with these advances carries responsibilities which extend beyond the classroom situation.
The development of individual student potentialities in terms of special abilities, interests
and needs must be met through the use of diverse learning experiences and instructional procedures.
New learnings are stimulated with emphasis being placed on enlarging concepts, acquiring facts,
and applying generalizations, as well as developing desirable attitudes and values.

Home Economics in recent years has moved far beyond the earlier emphasis on cooking, sewing
and certain other homemaking skills which were designed to help only a select number of girls.
Today the curriculum includes all aspects of family living such as: child care and guidance,
clothing and personal appearance; food for the family; health and home care of the sick; housing,
home furnishing and equipment; and personal, family and community relationships. This new func-
tional curriculum assists the family in dealing with the needs and circumstances of their times.
Home Economics can be effective only as it alleviates problems and aids one in adjusting to the
changing times.
Industrial Arts is basically the study of the tools, materials, and the procedure of industry.
At the Middle School we are primarily concerned with orientation to shop terms, safety, and the
responsibility of working with a group. We are also concerned with creating a flexible program
which allows for the wide range of student interest.
This year, because of limited space and equipment, we have been working mainly with wood.
Most of the students are able to meet with some degree of success working in this media. In the
near future we will be including metal, plastics, electronic equipment, and basic technical draw-
ing, hoping that during the three years the student is at the Middle School they will have expe-
rience in all areas.
We are presently working with many of the academic subjects trying to show the relationship
of all the subject areas and Industrial Arts, always giving the student the flexibility to learn
new and exciting skills or the chance to work with new materials,
Middle School Guidance
In the normal process of growing up, children meet obstacles that seem insurmountable to
them. A good guidance counselor is often the helping hand they need to help them hurdle these
obstacles. Unfortunately, there is no measuring device to determine the great amount of good that
guidance counselors do in their everyday work. Newspapers and courts usually publicize only the
failures of our society.
Guidance counselors work in close harmony with administrators, teachers, school nurses and
many others in performing their everyday work in the best interests of our children.
We are very fortunate to have a guidance person in the Middle School who is not afraid to
work long hours and leave no stone unturned to help a child. I am referring to Mr. John I. White
who works on the assumption that an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. At the present
time, he is studying all means of how we can implement programs to help curb some of the problems
that plague today's society; especially problems that are harmful to our youth. We feel that
these problems must be met head-on and at an early age.
Changing Education
I have deliberately avoided trying to give the impression that the Middle School has arrived
at a firm program. The Middle School concept is relatively new and change in education is inevit-
able. However, we all know that everything new is not necessarily good. We will not jump on the
band wagon simply because of a proposed new concept in education. Our program will move forward
and change only when it is in the best interests of the students of our town.
On behalf of the Middle School personnel, I wish to thank the Citizens of Lunenburg, the
School Committee, Mr. Brown, the Middle School Building Committee, Principals, Supervisors, and
teachers of the entire school system for their patience and understanding. Without their effort
and cooperation, it would have been difficult to implement our Middle School program.
Respectfully submitted,

Lunenburg Elementary Schools
The 1968-69 school year opened with grades Kindergarten through grade seven on double ses-
ions and sharing the elementary school facilities in both the Thomas C. Passios School and the
Elementary Annex. Although double sessions are not conducive to the best type of an educational
program, it is felt that by careful planning we have adapted the program for maximum learning
under the circumstances.
It has been gratifying to me to see the cooperation displayed among the staff members in
sharing the rooms and materials and also the cooperation and understanding shown by Mr. Barney
and his staff in using the same building. The entire school personnel have worked together as a
team and the results have been rewarding.
With the opening of the Middle School, Kindergarten, Grades one, two, and three classes of
grade four, will be housed in the Thomas C. Passios Elementary School, and grade three, will be
housed in the Elementary Annex. The other four classes of grade four will be in the Middle
School with grades five, six and seven.
