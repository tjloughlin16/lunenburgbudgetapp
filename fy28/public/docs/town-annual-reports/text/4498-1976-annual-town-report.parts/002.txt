QUESTION NO. 4 - LAW PROPOSED BY AN INITIATIVE PETITION
Do you approve of a law summarized below, which was disapproved by the House of Representatives on May 5, 1975, by a
vote of 179-46, and on which no vote was taken by the Senate before May 7, 1975?
SUMMARY - SECTION 1 of the act inserts a new chapter 164B into the General Laws and establishes a Massachusetts Power
Authority, a body corporate and politic with seven members appointed by the Governor to staggered six year terms. The Authority
is to establish and operate a bulk power supply system to supply wholesale electric power to utilities throughout the Commonwealth.
The primary purpose of the Authority is to supply the Commonwealth with powerwith the minimum adverse impact on the environment.
The Authority isalso authorized to engage in research and development of new sources of power, new siting techniques, and methods
of environmental protection.
In carrying out its responsibilities, the Authority is authorized to adopt by-laws; adopt an official seal; maintain offices; sue
and be sued; construct or acquire facilities either within or without the Commonwealth; issue revenue bonds and borrow money in
anticipation of issuance of revenue bonds; acquire real and personal property; employ professional, managerial and other employees
deemed necessary and fix their compensation to be paid solely out of revenues of the Authority; appear before other government
agencies; apply for and receive federal or other grants of funds; and enter into contracts and agreements.
The Authority will buildand operate all new generatingar transmission facilities in the Commonwealth and has the option to
urchase existing facilities through negotiation, condemnation, or eminent domain. After an initial two-year period, no other utility
may construct a new facility unless the Authority certifies that if lacks, the capability to finance the facility and the facility would
further the purposes of the act.
The Authority will finance its activities by issuing revenue bonds. The bonds will be exempt from state taxation, but will not
be backed by the full faith and credit of the Commonwealth. Power will besold to other utilitiesby contract but no special discounts
or bonuses to promote the increased use of power may be given. Public hearings are required on all major contracts.
The Authority is required to develop a master, 20-year demand study and siting plan within 18 months of its incorporation,
to be updated each succeeding year. Sites will be selected in accordance with the Electric Power Facilities Siting Council Act of
1973. The Governor and the community in which any facility is to be located must affirmatively approve the facilitybefore it can
be constructed.
The Authority will be subject to all applicable federal and state environmental standards and must obtainall necessary federal
and state permits and complete all necessary environmental impact statements.
The Authority will be exempt from taxation but will make payments in lieu of taxes to cities and towns in an amount equal to
the tax which would be paid if the Authority's real and personal property were owned by a private electric utility company.
The Authority is forbidden from engaging in promotional or image advertising. The Authority has the authority to bargain
collectively with its employees and is subject to the provisions of Chapter 150 of the General Laws, which governs the conciliation
and arbitration of industrial disputes. Employees of the Authority are not subject to the civil service law and rules. Employees of
utilities displaced by the activities of the Authority have first préference in employment by the Authority.
SECTION 2 of the act amends section 43 of Chapter 164 of the General Laws to provide that if a city or town votes, in ac-
cordance with the provision of Chapter 164, to establish a municipal utility and acquire the facilities of the utility currently serving
the community, and the utility refuses to sell its property to the city or town, that the Department of Public Utilities will establish
a fair price for the facilities, and the utility will be required to accept the price determined by the department and tender the deed
for the facilities to the city or town.

Tax Collector
Approximately 20,000 tax bills and notices were mailed and processed during Fiscal 1976. Of $2,450,637
in Real Estate Taxes committed, $66,670 was abated, $17,476 was added to Tax Title, $11,786 in Railroad taxes
was placed in Litigation, $4,315 was refunded, and payments of $2,274,316 were received, leaving a balance of
$84,704, or 3 1/2%, uncollected as of June 30, 1976. This is the same as the corresponding percentage uncollected
as of the end of the previous Fiscal Year. There were no prior year Real Estate or Personal Property taxes outstanding
as of the end of Fiscal 1976.
Motor Vehicle Excise receipts totalled $324,711. Although notices mailed to residents who have Fitchburg
mailing addresses, warning them against paying their Motor Vehicle Excise to the City of Fitchburg, have proven ef-
fective in that a considerable number of Fitchburg bills have been forwarded to the Lunenburg Board of Assessors for
reprocessing, Fitchburg is still billing and receiving some portion of Lunenburg's Motor Vehicle Excise revenues. This
is because the State carelessly sends the bills to the city or town shown on the mailing address, as opposed to the actual
place of principal garaging. Whalom residents who have not already done so are urged to return excise bills received
from the City of Fitchburg and request that they be reprocessed to Lunenburg.

It has been 2 1/2 years since we changed from a "Calendar" to a "Fiscal" Financial Year. The primary pur-
pose of this change, which was legislated by the State, was to preclude the necessity of borrowing large sums in
anticipation of revenue. For this to be accomplished, the State adopted a special 18 month (January 1973 through
June 1974) accounting procedure, which allowed cities and towns to build up their surplus funds and to maintain a
higher level of surplus than was maintained under the Calendar Year accounting system. Many cities and towns, fail-
ing to understand how the new system was supposed to work, used up all their surpluses, andare now back to borrowing.
As can be seen on the Balance Sheet, Surplus Revenue in our Town totalled $619,398 as of June 30, 1976.
This is the result of judicious use of our surplus funds, the availability of which removed the necessity of borrowing
and permitted investments. Interest saved on monies not borrowed and earned on funds invested during the last year
alone will have the effect of offsetting over $100,000 worth of expenditures in the coming year. Preliminary calcula-
tions with regard to next year's budget point to a substantial increase in the tax rate. It is hoped that our Town ad-
ministrators will fight off the temptation to drastically reduce our surplus in an effort to ward off such an increase, for
this action would mean that from this time on our Town would be forced into borrowing, and in the long run this can
only result in greater increases in the tax rates of future years.
I have made a careful examination of the books of the Tax Collector
and Town Treasurer, and find the same to be correct and in good order.
(s) James R. Bascom, Auditor

Montachusett Regional Vocational
Technical School District
The size of "Monty Tech" and its ability to enroll those students who have shown an interest in coming to our
school still remains a problem. As a result of the increased numbers of applications, the Administration and School
Committee have been attempting to develop an equitable student enrollment process. The building utilization has
maximized in order to provide Vocational Education to as many as possible, hence, the budget has provided for an
Afternoon Program which has been designed for students from the sending district who are not enrolled in this school
to develop career skills. The emphasis has been changed from exploratory activities to job and career preparation.
The Evening Program has accommodated approximately 2,000 adults. Our day-school program numbered
993 High School students, 16 post-graduates in Trade and Industrial programs, 144 Allied Health post-graduates, and
27 Early Child Care toddlers.
Since our waiting lists have been increasing yearly, advantage has been taken of an opportunity for a project
proposal under the Economic Development Administration for an expansion of the school. While the opportunity, at
this writing, is still present, the probability of approval is less than 10% of the 800 E.D.A. applications. Neverthe-
less, the School Committee and Administration are taking every possible advantage they can in applying for these funds.
Our Craft Advisory Committees have been meeting on a regular basis, thus having a direct interrelationship with
local industry and the specific programs at the school. They are providing input into course content, recommenda-
tions for equipment, cooperative study stations and, thus, act as additional quality control for the programs being
taught.
As in the past, graduation exercises have grown in participation and, hence, three graduations to accommodate
friends and relatives were held for the High School graduates, Allied Health Careers and Post-Graduate programs.
Of the 353 total graduates, over 90% either received employment in related fields, went on to higher education, or
joined the service.
The special orientation for new students was designed with student/parent counseling after aptitude testing and
two days spent with incoming students prior to the start of regular classes. Career objectives, course requirements,
school regulations, opportunities within the school, and ancillary services were discussed with the students.
Teacher workshops were designed to cope with the many changes and regulations as a result of legislation at

ENROLLMENT ANALYSIS - - The Future
This past fall, Dr. John Marshall, an educational consultant who at one time was Associate
State Commissioner of School Building Assistance, completed a comprehensive pupil enrollment study
and projection for Lunenburg. Dr. Marshall reviewed records and materials from the Town Hall and
the State Department of Education, had discussions with representatives from Montachusett Regional
Planning Commission, examined past enrollment studies, and personally toured our facilities. The
following is an excerpt from his report:
"A safer conclusion (referring to his charts and statistics that reflect downward
enrollment trends) is the more general one indicated on the charts: grades 1-4 had
about 180 pupils per grade in 1965, have about 150 per grade now, and are likely to have
about 125 per grade by 1982; grades 5-8 have now about 180 per grade, and are likely
to have about 150 per grade by 1980 and 125 per grade by 1985; grades 9-12 are likely
to drop from about 180 per grade now to about 150 per grade by 1984.
"All the projection lines point downward. Does this mean that Lunenburg will have
no further school housing problems? Not at all!
"While the immediate future seems likely to go in the directions indicated, a long-
range forecast looks different. Remember that the projections reported here reflect
declining birth figures during a period of economic stringency. The Montachusett
Regional Planning Commission forecasts for Lunenburg a growth in population from 8158
in 1975 to about 10, by 1985. Such growth would mean that school enrollment, in-
stead of continuing to drop, would turn upward in the 1980s.
"Among the factors encouraging community growth are access to jobs, shopping, and
recreation; good schools; and a liberal amount of residentially-suitable land. Lunen-
burg has these, and is bound to share in area population growth when it occurs.
"Specially pertinent to current problems is what these projections mean for utili-
zation of Lunenburg's school plant facilities. A change under consideration would have
the elementary school housing grades K-4, the annex grade five, the middle school
grades 6-8, and the high school grades 9-12. These moves are quite feasible. The need
for establishing staff and community readiness suggests the fall of 1978 for them.
"I would wrge continued liaison, through the local Planning Board, with the Monta-
chusett Regional Planning Commission, so that Lunenburg can be aware of regional
changes that will have local impact; and I would urge careful review of enrollment
change and periodic updating of projections in the light of new factors that may come
to light."

SCHOOL COMMITTEE GOAL STATEMENTS - -
This past fall, the School Committee participated in a twelve-hour workshop for the purpose
of drafting a list of goal statements and priorities for the Lunenburg School System. Attempting
to establish "benchmarks" for education in Lunenburg, the School Committee is currently soliciting
reactions to its goal statements from students, teachers, parents, and administrators. Since
a
management-by-objectives approach to personnel evaluation is being followed by all supervisory
personnel in our school system, the School Committee felt it was important to articulate a broad
statement of goals which would help set directions for education in Lunenburg schools. With this
thought in mind, the following draft was prepared by the School Committee -
FUNDAMENTAL ASSUMPTIONS:
To provide guidelines and principles from which decisions can be made and upon
which judgments about the success of various parts of the system can be judged, the
following convictions are proposed. It is accepted that a school system's effective-
ness depends on the quality of the interaction of all of its components--school
committee, administration, staff, students, and community. The (ideal) school system
provides a climate of mutual respect in which members of the system can achieve the max-
imum potential for human growth.
Specifically, the characteristics and features of the components may be defined as
follows -
LEARNING ENVIRONMENT AND STUDENT:
The learning environment should provide for motivating, challenging, demanding, and
exciting experiences in enjoyable, safe surroundings. It should recognize, encourage,
and provide for the uniqueness of each student's needs and capabilities.
THE COMMUNITY AND PARENTS:
The community should be involved, aware, informed, and supportive. It should feel a
sense of pride and reward for its support.
THE SCHOOL COMMITTEE:
As one component of the system, the committee recognizes that it should be responsi-
ble to the needs of parents, students, and the community-at-large.
THE ADMINISTRATION:
The administrative staff of the system should consist of competent individuals who
are sensitive, flexible, compassionate people. They should constantly seek effective,

system. They should also be open and receptive to suggestion and initiate communica-
tion in their efforts to detect and correct observed weaknesses.

The members of the staff should be perceptive, competent, and committed. They should
express a sensitive, positive and mature attitude. They should be responsible and
humane in their actions. Educators should be receptive to criticism. Their treatment
of students should be fair, impartial and caring.

In the spring of 1975 the Lunenburg School
Committee sent a questionnaire to all community
residents who were seventeen years of age and
older. The intent of that undertaking, as stated
by the School Committee, was to determine what
Lunenburg residents wanted in and from their
schools.
In the Annual Town Report last year, it was
reported that initial information received from
the questionnaire did change School Committee
opinions (both individually and as a group) to
be more in line with majority Community opinion.
Further, indepth study of the School Committee
over a six-month period of time which included
the fall of 1975 and spring of 1976 found that
School Committee actions and perspectives were
also influenced by data gathered in the ques-
tionnaire.
A log of School Committee meetings showed
that several actions related to questionnaire
data were taken in regular, executive sessions
and negotiation meetings. In total, 118 School
Committee actions occurred in forty different
- Dr. Burton E. Goodrich, Jr. -
School Committee settings. The actions were
related to a wide range of topics (i. e., staff-
ing, budget, class size, negotiations, student
attendance, guidance, communications, etc.). It appeared that the comprehensive nature of the
questionnaire provided opportunities for application of data to a broad spectrum of School Commit-
tee business.
Several School Committee perspectives about the questionnaire process and data emerged in the
six-month study. Following are some examples:
1. In general, opinions expressed by the Community were expected by the School Committee.
However, in some cases, Community opinions did identify topics of special interest
and concern that were not expected by the School Committee.
2. The School Committee mostly concerned itself only with the majority opinion although
the Committee was knowledgeable of different opinions expressed by sub-populations in
the Community.

The School Committee did not perceive the questionnaire data as being a mandate for
action.
5. In most cases of School Committee decision-making that involved questionnaire data,
the questionnaire data was not a primary factor in influencing the School Committee.

ALTERNATIVE RESOURCE ROOM - -
The Alternative Resource Room (A. R. R.) was designed by a group of local administrators,
counselors, and teachers, and implemented in the high school this year for the purpose of helping
those students who have difficulty adjusting to the school environment and requirements. The
behavior exhibited by these students includes truancy, repeated disruption of classes, defiance,
inability to follow through on tasks and assignments and the problems of coping with large group
situations.
In order to try to remediate the aforementioned problems, the staff of the A. R. R. carries
out the following activities: counseling, academic tutoring, and small group discussions focusing
on certain common identifiable patterns of behavior.
As students are referred to the A. R. R. by teachers, counselors, or administrators, teachers
of the students are consulted to determine the extent and nature of the disruptive behavior and to
obtain academic information for tutorial help. Teachers are frequently consulted in order that
the A. R. R. teacher/counselor might determine whether the student is progressing satisfactorily
when not assigned to the A. R. R. room.
Students spend from two periods per week to two periods per day in the resource room, depend-
ing on the remediation deemed necessary. An exception to this procedure occurs when those students
whose behavior warrants an in-school suspension are assigned to the A. R. R. for three full days.
The student is counseled during the in-school suspension and all of his assignments are
obtained from his regular teachers and completed with tutorial help in the resource room during
the period of suspension.
In many cases, suspension out of school is non-corrective and frequently generates greater
antagonistic behavior patterns. Hence, the new efforts and trials of some in-school suspensions.

ACADEMIC ACHIEVEMENT -
The Class of 1977 has received recognition for outstanding scholastic achievement this year
from the National Merit Scholarship Corporation. As a result of tests administered in 1976 to
over a million high school juniors in the fifty states, three of our students were recognized as
semi-finalists, placing them in the top half of one percent of the senior class students within
the Commonwealth of Massachusetts. Upon the local school's recommendation based on academic
achievement, in all probability these students will become finalists. The students so honored for
their own scholastic application, interest, and attitude are: Grant Bergman, Carolyn Heimberg,
and Jeffrey Janell.
In addition, seven other senior students received Letters of Commendation as a result of
their scores on the same series of tests. This recognition placed these students among the top
two percent of all secondary school seniors throughout the country. This number of students from
a public high school of our size is extremely significant. Lunenburg High School has never before
placed more than three or four students in this national scholarship competition in any one year.
This outstanding accomplishment brings great credit to the students, the school staff, and the
community.
NEW GRADUATION CREDIT REQUIREMENT - -
After a year of faculty study and discussion, the School Committee approved an increase in
the number of academic credits needed for graduation. Effective with the Class of 1980 to be
declared eligible for graduation, high school students will need to complete nineteen (19) units
including the successful completion of four years of English and the completion of one year of
American history and four years of physical education. Eighteen (18) units will remain the
requirement for the classes of 1977, 1978, and 1979.

GERMANY '77 - -
During 1976, the Lunenburg Music Aiders, the parent support organization for our student
musicians, launched the ambitious project of sending our high school band and chorus on a per-
forming tour of Germany during the period of April 14 to April 23, 1977. Highlighting the trip
will be a visit to Luneburg, a German community that might well be considered our sister city in
Europe. A concert by our musical ensembles and a cordial civic exchange will be the feature of
our visit to this beautiful Medieval community. Our trip throughout Germany is being considered
by many as an integral part of our own community's 250th birthday.
The performances of our band and chorus will afford our young musicians with the priceless
opportunity of representing their town, state, and country in the wholesome manner that music
provides. They will also reap the many cultural benefits inherent in travel itself. In addition
to the performances, these young people will also see a large part of Germany, visiting many
historical sites and enjoying new experiences in another country that make one a better world
citizen.
The Lunenburg Music Aiders have been working diligently on this project since last summer and
are now very close to the realization of their goal. The community owes a debt of gratitude to
the co-chairpersons of the Ways and Means Committee-Mrs. Carl Smith and Mrs. Walter Gendron.
