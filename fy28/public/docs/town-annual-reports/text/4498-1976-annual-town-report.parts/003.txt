Report of Special Needs Director
"Some people make the world more
special just by being in it"
- G. W. Douglas
In my second year with the Lunenburg
schools, I would like to report the progress the
community has made in serving children with
special needs.
In accord with federal and state legisla-
tion and whenever possible in the interest of
the child, the educational placement of the stu-
dent is within the school district. That basic
goal is further supported by the directed educa-
tional practice that children shall be inte-
grated with other children who may be different
in some way. When the placement of a student is
out-of-district, such placement is as close to
home as possible and this is clearly indicated
in this legislation and is implemented in the
required approval of certain placements by state
and regional boards.
SPECIAL NEEDS POPULATION - -
In the laws of Massachusetts, Chapter 766
of the Acts of 1972 requires the school system
to evaluate children with possible special needs
between the ages of 3 - 21 who reside within the
community. Estimates of special needs children
in the United States and other countries range
- Floyd Benitz -
from 10% to 22%. In Massachusetts, the Depart-
ment of Education has set 12% as the anticipated
proportion of youngsters in each community who
require evaluation and services. According to that guideline and based on the total Lunenburg
schools population of 2094, we may expect to evaluate and serve 251 special needs students in a
-110-
===PAGE 113===
year. At the October 1976 report date, we were instructing 147 special needs students. Though
less than the guideline this is more than a 100% increase over the previous year.
The State guideline does not consider the contrasts in communities across the Commonwealth.
In consideration of the relatively favorable economic and health conditions in Lunenburg, it is
expected that the special needs population will level off at the minimum level of 10% or approxi-
mately 200 pupils.
In addition to the remarkable increase in number of children served this year, it must be
noted that faculty who have regular teaching schedules with children are at the same time studying
new children, consulting with teachers and parents, monitoring students' school performance,
assisting parents in home instruction, directing volunteers and advancing their own training. This
special education staff is steadily evaluating children referred throughout the year.
The children of Lunenburg now being served generally have moderate to severe special needs or
combinations of difficulties warranting service. As the programs move forward and embrace about
200 students, it is expected that moderate to mild special needs will be identified. There is a
team evaluation along with parent input to determine special need and even a mild special need is
by definition a special need to be followed by an educational plan and service. That service is
usually through the regular classroom teacher involving consultations and modifications in the
regular program.
CORE EVALUATION - -
No educational legislation in Massachusetts history has generated as much documentation and
record-keeping in order to serve the child according to the law. In this respect, compliance with
the law has the net effect of either employing sufficient professional personnel to prepare reports
or of losing some direct service to the child in order to prepare records and reports and attend
child evaluation meetings. Similarly, the kinds of expertise and number of persons represented at
such meetings is directed by regulations and frequently this has the effect of a minimum of five
staff hours in meeting with a parent to plan for a child.
There is hope that required state procedures will be reduced as a result of study andhearings
this year. Meanwhile, staff will work with the many forms that require professional attention and
serve as many children as possible.
EARLY INTERVENTION - -
It is generally accepted among health and education professionals that the earlier children
with special needs are identified, the better the opportunity of meeting the special needs. In
accord with the law and the practice of early identification, we screen three and four-year olds
every spring wherein the parents think the child may have special needs. A subsequent comprehen-
sive evaluation of selected pre-schoolers results in services and placement according to individual
need.

Retirement
Completing almost fifteen years of ser-
vice as a member of the secretarial staff, Dot
retired from her position as Secretary to the
high school principal effective December 31st.
She had served as Senior Clerk/Typist in the
high school office from 1962 to June 2, 1969
at which time she was promoted to the position
of Principal Clerk with duties as school
treasurer and principal's secretary. Dot was
one of those conscientious, dedicated, loyal
and dependable people who was held in high
esteem by all members of the school staff.
Her sensitivity, understanding, even tempera-
ment, zest for life and sense of humor were
qualities that contributed greatly to her
success as a school secretary.
On December 9th, the secretarial staff
honored Dot by treating her to dinner and pre-
senting her with a camera that surely will be
put to a great deal of use as Dot loves to
travel.
On December 18th, an Open House was held
where a large gathering of friends including
present and former staff members presented Dot
with a "live" money tree to show their appre-
ciation for her many years of service, her
friendship and her untiring efforts on behalf

The School Nursing Program in Lunen-
burg is done by the Public Health Nursing
Service, part of the Board of Health. The
staff of four registered nurses are also
URSING
responsible for the visiting nurse ser-
vices to the town.
REPORT
Janet Pace is the Nursing Supervisor;
Shirley Carpenter is the full-time nurse
at the Passios Elementary School; and
there are two part-time nurses--Jean
Hobbs at the Senior High School and Marie
Richards at the Turkey Hill Middle School.
Dr. Joel Neuschatz is the School Physi-
cian.
The focus of the school health program is on preventive health measures and early detection
of possible health problems. Vision and hearing screening is done annually on all students, with
parents being notified of any failures; and with referral forms from the doctors being returned to
the school SO we will best know how to help the child. Physical examinations are required on all
children entering school; on all students involved in the junior-senior high sports programs; on
students being evaluated under Chapter 766; on students with frequent complaints or absences; and
transferring students. Pre-school students are also screened, i. e., all those entering kinder-
garten the following fall, and any three or four-year old for which a parent requests a screening.
When necessary, home visits are made by the nurses concerning physical problems and Chapter 766
follow-ups. The nurses are always available for parent conferences either by phone or at school.
A new postural screening program is being
developed for 1977, under the guidance of the
Massachusetts Department of Public Health. Chil-
dren in grades 5 through 8 will be screened by
the physical education teachers and nurses for
any postural problems such as scoliosis. If
detected early, these conditions may be treated
easily and successfully.
