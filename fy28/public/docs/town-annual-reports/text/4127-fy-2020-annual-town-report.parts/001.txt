                                                     In Memoriam 
Elisabeth "Sis" (Ryan) Montuori, 87, of Lunenburg, died peacefully July 28, 2020 surrounded by her 
loving family. Her husband of 65 years, Lawrence "Lawrie" Montuori, died January 16, 2020. Sis worked 
alongside Lawrie as bookkeeper of Lunenburg Oil Co, the business they operated together for 36 years. She 
was a talented seamstress, working out of her home. Sis was a faithful member of St. Boniface Parish and St. 
Boniface Women's Guild. For years, she oversaw St. Boniface's annual Red Cross Blood Mobile, earning 
awards for the high volume of donations. She volunteered on the T.C. Passios Elementary School 
Community Council, Lunenburg High School Football Boosters and the Council on Aging. Sis had a passion 
and talent for cooking, interior decorating, entertaining, and gardening. She had an unconditional love of life, 
family and friends. She leaves a son, three daughters, a half-brother, eight grandchildren, six great-   
                                   grandchildren and several nephews and nieces. 
 
 
Bessie H. (Platt) Lindsay, 90, passed peacefully on July 30, 2020. Bessie was married  
to the love of her life, Edmund K. Lindsay, who sadly left us in 2001. Bessie was born in Shandaken, NY, 
daughter of the late William and Shirley Platt and one of seven siblings. 
Bessie was proud of and dedicated to her community. Her volunteerism was extensive including at the Eagle 
House and the Lunenburg Senior Center and the Lunenburg Friendly Seniors, where she was also a member. 
Bessie was incredibly active and loved to have fun with family and friends! 
 
 
Douglas K. MacMillan, 88, died August 7, 2020. He was the owner of Cherry Hill Dairy Farm. He and 
his wife Marilyn celebrated their 64th anniversary on May 5th. He was born in Sweetsburg, Quebec on 
November 22, 1931. He went to a one-room school in Bolton Pass until grade 6 and then attended 
Knowlton Academy in Knowlton, Quebec. He moved to Lunenburg in 1951 with his parents and ran 
Cherry Hill Dairy Farm, eventually becoming the owner.  Douglas was a country music fan and he always 
had the radio on and he could be heard singing all over the farm. He loved telling jokes, doing crossword 
puzzles, playing cards and visiting family and friends. He was a member and a director of the 
Massachusetts Cooperative Milk Producers Federation, a longtime member and a director for 9 years of 
ASCS, a lifetime member of The Grange and a member of the United Parish of Lunenburg.  
 
 
Bruce W. Smith, 75, passed away August 26, 2020. He was a member of the first Lunenburg High                      
School football team and a 1963 graduate of Lunenburg High School. Bruce served in the U.S. Air Force 
from 1963 to 1969 and then attended Northeastern University. He returned to the Lunenburg Football field 
as an assistant coach from 1979 through 2004. Bruce was a Correctional Officer for the MA Dept. of 
Corrections for 15 years. He enjoyed coaching the Lunenburg Blue Knights and acted as a father figure to so 
many of his student athletes. Bruce had a positive impact on thousands of athletes which included many 
enjoyable years with the Lunenburg Bengals as well as Lunenburg Youth football. Bruce was inducted into 
the Massachusetts High School Football Hall of Fame as Assistant Coach of the Year in 2002 after 23 years 
of dedication. Bruce loved spending time with his family and friends, and he lived life to the fullest. The                                                                       
most important thing in his life was his family and he spoke about them often.  
 
 
Donald F. Bowen, 88, passed away on October 6, 2020. Don was born in Boston on December 21, 
1931. He attended high school in Boston and graduated from Northeastern University in 1962. Over his 
career he worked in sales for a number of high-tech companies. He served in the U.S. Navy between 1950 -
1951. He moved to Lunenburg in 1980 and loved his home on Lake Shirley. He was active in his 
community and served as a board member for the Lunenburg Zoning Board for 30 years, the latter years as 
Chairman.  
Don loved spending time with family and friends and he lived life to the fullest. He was a loyal and 
generous friend and a loving and dedicated father. The most important thing in his life was his family.  
He is survived by his daughter Dawn-Jean and son-in-law Drew; son Mark and daughter-in-law Donna; his 
grandchildren, Justin, Nicole, Danielle, Anthony, Jacob and Alexandra and his great grandchildren, Liam, 
Grace and Mae.

Lunenburg was settled in 1718 and incorporated in 1728. It occupies a land area of approximately 26.4 
square miles. Lunenburg is located in north central Massachusetts approximately 43 miles from Boston.  It is 
bordered on the west by the Town of Ashby and the City of Fitchburg, on the south by the Town of 
Lancaster and the City of Leominster, on the east by the Town of Shirley, and on the north by the Town of 
Townsend. 
Local legislative decisions are made by an open town meeting consisting of all registered voters in the town. 
Subject to the legislative decisions made by town meeting, the affairs of the town are generally administered 
by a five member Select Board and a  Town Manager. Local school affairs are administered by an elected 
seven member School Committee while local taxes are assessed by a three member Board of Assessors, all 
are appointed on an at-large basis for staggered three-year terms. 
Lunenburg is a semi-rural community located in northern Worcester County.  Three water bodies account for 
somewhat of a clustered approach to the town's historical development.  Lake Whalom and the former 
Whalom Amusement Park delineated the Whalom District from the remainder of Lunenburg.  This district 
was developed as a housing base for many factory workers working in the mills and factories of Leominster 
and Fitchburg.  In many ways the characteristics of the Whalom District closely reflect characteristics of 
these cities more than of the rest of Lunenburg. The Hickory Hills area, around Hickory Hills Lake, was 
developed as a private community comprised largely of vacation cottages.  The third body of water is Shirley 
Reservoir, a public water body also developed with mainly vacation cottages that have now been modified to 
year-round residences.  
 
Commercial and industrial development is permitted in several areas of town, primarily along the two major 
arteries (Routes 2A and 13) and the town's peripheries, due largely to the close proximity of Leominster's 
and Fitchburg's sewer and water systems. Although many farms and orchards had slowly developed into 
single family housing, farming continues on in Lunenburg. In 2013 the Annual Town Meeting voted to 
establish an Agricultural Commission to represent, preserve, and enhance farming in the community and to 
administer the “Right to Farm” bylaw.   
 
The 2015 Lunenburg Town Meeting established the Architectural Preservation District ("District") bylaw to 
regulate construction, demolition or renovation of existing "Contributing Property" structures as defined by 
the bylaw within an established bounded district.  The bylaw was introduced at Town Meeting along with the 
Village District Bylaw, which created an overlay zoning district to encourage mixed -use commercial and 
residential development.  While the Village District Bylaw encourages the economic vitalization of 
downtown Lunenburg, the Architectural Preservation District Bylaw ensures that it will not come at the 
expense of the town's historical character and integrity.

REPORT OF THE TOWN MANAGER 
Fiscal Year 2020 
This year, as I write this report on the achievements of fiscal year 2020, my mind immediately goes back to 
March of 2020 when all our lives were turned upside down. We can all relate that this past year has been a 
roller coaster and everyone has faced personal and professional challenges that no one could have foreseen. 
From a local government standpoint, there were a myriad of obstacles and uncertainties facing cities and 
towns across our nation that our local and school leadership team also faced, such as providing a continuity 
of services to our residents and businesses during a pandemic, food insecurity for our youth and elders, re-
opening measures that adequately protected our employees, students and the public, remote learning for 
students, remote public meetings, a vast array of changes in state and federal legislation that affected our 
daily operations, and the list goes on. The town department heads and school leadership teams and our staff 
came together during this time and met these challenges head on. As a town we witnessed attitudes of 
perseverance, innovation, and an overall desire to help our residents, students, and businesses throughout 
this pandemic. I want to recognize the immense thought and effort that town and school staff put into how 
we provided services this past year and how everyone rose to the challenge.   
During fiscal year 2020, in addition to all the responses that were needed in light of COVID-19, there were 
many topics addressed and accomplishments to highlight: 
 In July of 2019, as part of the annual goal setting process, the Selectmen reviewed their goals from FY19 
and formalized their goals for FY20.  The goals the Board identified for FY2019 included identifying, 
defining, discussing, and finalizing the criteria to be used in determining the use and/or re-use of the 
Town Hall, Ritter, old Primary, Brooks House, and TC Passios and to propose at least one project to the 
2020 Annual Town Meeting regarding the use and/or reuse of the old Primary School.  The Board 
identified the criteria used in determining the use and/or re-use of the aforementioned buildings in the 
fall of 2019 and discussed the future use of the old Primary throughout the year and continued to narrow 
down the focus for the future use of this building into 2020. The Board made progress on determining 
the future use of the old Primary School property as a future open space/passive recreation space with 
additional parking that is lacking in the center of town.   
 
 The Town Manager’s goals for FY 2020 included developing and implementing steps towards the 
redevelopment/reuse of 925 Massachusetts Avenue, implementation of a recycling contamination 
enforcement program, and planning/implementing accessibility improvements. All of the action items 
identified for each of the Town Manager’s goals were completed. 
 In July and August the Board met multiple times to finalize a Special Event Policy. The Board also did 
their annual review of all their policies and adopted a new Buy Recycled Policy and made various 
updates to the existing policies.  In September the Board adopted a Social Media Policy. 
 This year was another productive year for infrastructure projects in Lunenburg.  Besides the continuation 
of the $4M Pavement Management Plan on local roads, the Town continued to work with the 
engineering firm on the Pleasant Street Bridge Project that is expected to be complete by the spring of 
2022.  The DPW also undertook culvert repairs on Northfield Road and Sunset Lane and repairs to the 
Townsend Harbor Bridge.  The Summer Street Road Reconstruction project that was awarded in 2018 
was substantially complete by the fall of 2019.

 The Board and the Town Manager worked with Montachusett Planning Commission on a Walkability and 
Parking Study for the Main Street area and the Whalom area.  A report was presented to the Board in

 In the fall of 2019 the Town Manager worked with a consultant to apply for grant funding through the

plan for climate change and implement key adaptation actions for resiliency.  The planning grant applied 
for is a necessary step in order to apply for construction costs to implement projects that are identified 
during the planning phase.  The Town received a Municipal Vulnerability Preparedness (MVP) Planning 
Grant and hired a consultant to guide the planning process, including holding the required all day 
workshop, listening session, and to complete the vulnerability assessment and develop an action oriented 
climate resiliency plan.   Communities who complete the MVP program become certified as an MVP 
community and are eligible for action grant funding to implement projects in their action plan.  At the 
conclusion of this planning phase the Town submitted the action plan to the State, which was 
subsequently approved.

Conservation Commission 
The Conservation Commission is a seven member commission, appointed to 3 year terms by the Select 
Board, whose duties are codified under the Conservation Commission Act MGL c. 40 § 8C. The Commission 
enforces the Massachusetts Wetland Protection Act MGL c. 131 § 40 and Chapter 239 of the local by-laws 
for the protection of wetlands. 
The year 2020 was as last year, one of a very busy and active agenda containing both public hearings and 
meetings for a wide range of projects from commercial development, subdivisions and small single family 
upkeep projects and larger scale industrial/commercial proposals despite the limitations set by the COVID-19 
pandemic. The Conservation Commission’s work continues into the year 2021 to work on clarifying the land 
use policy to provide consistency with the original by-law and to find those sections that may be now out of 
date and need to be modified. This effort is estimated to be completed by spring of 2021. The Conservation 
Commission has implemented revisions to the local wetland regulations, which have been in effect for over 
one year to date and have served the interests of protecting valuable community natural resources very fairly 
and effectively.  
The Commission also held numerous inspections with the commission administrator on weekends prior to 
meetings to familiarize themselves with the upcoming proposals that would appear on their agenda. The 
Commission continues to work with the town on familiarizing itself with the upcoming storm water mandates 
from the EPA and working towards compliance both by procedure and with any necessary codification as 
well as aiding with the community implementations the “MS4” regulations. 
The Conservation Commission continues to implement forest stewardship plans using grants as outlined in 
the last two annual reports. The stewardship plan for the Hollis Road property is oriented toward enhancing 
bird wildlife habitat in conjunction with the Mass Audubon Society as part of the DCR "Forestry for the 
Birds" program.  The forest stewardship plans provide environmental management plans which include 
timber-stand management. The Commission retained Gary Gouldrup of New England Forestry Consultants 
who is a noted forester with years of experience working in Lunenburg and the adjacent towns/cities serving 
municipalities and private landowners. The Commission continues to hold public forums on the 
implementation of its open space policies and timber-stand management. The Commission expects to 
continue to apply for and receive funding from the Commonwealth to author stewardship plans for our 
Conservation Areas.  
The Conservation Commission also has commenced planning for the forest management of the RAYNO 
property and worked with the Open Space Committee to complete the acquisition of the Saliba Property on 
Howard Street adding significant acreage of open space.  Copies of the current environmental stewardship 
plans are available on the Conservation Commission website at:   
https://www.lunenburgma.gov/files-docs/18-05-21/forest-stewardship-plans 
The Commission was awarded a Recreational Trails Program grant to provide color coded trail markers and 
coordinated “You Are Here” trail map signs to expand the safe use of Lunenburg’s existing trail network. 
Regional trail system map signs would show how trails are interconnected among multiple properties. The 
project will also provide informational kiosks and emergency access gates.  During 2020 trail maps were 
developed for eleven conservation properties.  These trail maps will be posted on a Conservation Commission 
Trails website, on informational kiosks on the properties and on “You Are Here” trail map signs at trail heads 
and trail intersections. 
The Commission has also continued to work closely with Lake Shirley and Hickory Hills on the 
implementations of their lake management plans.  These plans set goals and action items designed to give a 
better picture of the overall health of the lakes and define various strategies to ensure continued protection of 
these valuable resources.

DEPARTMENT OF PUBLIC WORKS 
The Department of Public Works combines the resources of the Highway, Parks, Cemetery, Sewer, Facilities 
Departments, and Vehicle Maintenance services into one department organizing and scheduling personnel and 
equipment to best serve the residents of the community through the effective delivery of an extensive scope of 
services with a high degree of efficiency. 
“The mission of the Department of Public Works is to protect, preserve, and improve the Town’s 
infrastructure and related assets in a manner which meets and exceeds the current and future social 
and economic needs of the community, to contribute to a healthy, safe and quality environment for the 
Town’s residents and visitors, and to provide a cost effective and environmentally sound means of 
disposing of solid waste and wastewater.” 
DEPARTMENT PHILOSOPHY 
Honesty and integrity are absolutes. They characterize the only forum in which anything worthwhile can be 
accomplished. 
Efficiency: Make every effort to proceed correctly the first time. It may take a bit longer but will not require 
repeated attempts. 
Loyalty up and down the chain of command is essential to success.  
Every employee is essential.  Those at the top are support people.  The real work is done by the men and 
women who drive the trucks, operate the equipment, turn the wrenches, and provide a seamless flow of 
paperwork. 
DEPARTMENTAL OBJECTIVES 
Public Works Staff is either working or on call twenty-four hours a day three hundred and sixty-five days a 
year prepared to respond to any weather, infrastructure, roadway, or facility related emergency that may arise. 
We strive to enhance the awareness of the value Public Works provides to our Town, and ensure others are 
aware of our contribution. This is not for an expectation of praise, but rather to make sure efforts are not 
dismissed as they merge into the fabric of our everyday expectation of service. 
Major events, particularly in the form of devastating storms, over the past years have highlighted the fact that 
Public Works has a seat at the table and is a major player in emergency management as first responders.  
Town Officials, Police, and Fire acknowledge that Public Works is critical in order to facilitate the required 
services of others. We must continue to build on this awareness. 
This Department must reach out to the public we serve, continuing to make them aware of the fact that the 
common services they receive do not just happen, but rather are the efforts of a dedicated staff of individuals, 
service companies, and vendors.  Safe roadways, wastewater control, solid waste collection and recycling 
operations, tree removal, storm water management, grounds maintenance, winter snow and ice removal, 
cemeteries and parks are necessary services supporting everyone in the community.  We will continue to 
engage the public in order to set reasonable expectations and gather support for annual budget requests, 
capital requests, and specific projects.

ENVIRONMENTAL 
Storm Water 
As a component of the aforementioned “debt exclusion” approximately five hundred thousand dollars of the 
four million in total has been earmarked for storm drainage including but not limited to repair of the existing 
collection system, and new construction.  We have already exceeded this amount and continue to perform 
necessary repairs to the town-wide drainage system. 
Before roads are paved, sub surface drainage is surveyed and necessary repairs are addressed to avoid paving 
over problems. In the future new Federal and State storm water regulations will likely force the Town to 
become more proactive albeit with no additional identified source of funding. 
At the conclusion of winter and again in the late fall drainage structures in close proximity to open water are 
cleaned and inspected.  Public Works maintains an aggressive schedule of street sweeping and catch basin 
cleaning, the front line of storm water management. 
Wastewater 
Working with the Sewer Commission, the DPW continues to be responsible for the day to day operation and 
maintenance of the wastewater collection system.  Maintenance of the collection system, inclusive of 10 pump 
stations, is outsourced to a specialty vendor but continues to be the responsibility of the DPW.  Business 
management, inspections, and permitting remain in-house and the responsibility of the DPW. 
Tree Removal   
Storm damage together with the natural attrition creates a backlog of tree felling and pruning that typically 
exceeds the Department’s allotted budget for tree removal.  The Department strives to fulfill each resident 
request but must prioritize work based upon the perceived degree of danger to residents and property.  With 
the establishment of a Hazardous Tree Survey, a more efficient approach to defects has been established 
through prioritization.  Currently, the amount of work far exceeds the funding available for it.  As this is the 
case, the DPW will continue to act in a reactionary manner as needs arise.  All work aside from brush 
removal, chipping, and the felling of small diameter trees will continue to be outsourced due to the necessity 
of a bucket truck or crane and a licensed professional.  Each spring DPW staff works to remove and dispose of 
woody debris within the Town right-of-way along with a Town-wide program of selective curbside mowing 
and vegetation clearing.  An aggressive program by Unitil in recent years has lessened the burden of the Town 
by removing those trees that may cause an interruption of electrical service to the community.

Landfill 
The capped, closed landfill off Young’s Road remains in compliance with the inspection protocol mandated

Winter Operations 
Snow and ice present a major challenge.  The safety of the public depends on the ability of the DPW to 
maintain our roadways at a high level of safety despite the severity or the duration of snow and icing events.  
To that end, we have developed a winter operations procedure based upon a detailed plan with flexibility to 
accommodate the unpredictability of each storm.  The expectation of immediate service is high and must be 
delivered to every part of Town in a timely manner.  
Departmental preparedness begins with successful implementation of the capital plan.  Financial support is 
necessary to provide effective fleet rotation insuring minimal interruption of service due to breakdowns and 
repairs.  In the fall, salt is delivered to the DPW facility. Lunenburg is in a consortium with 14 other 
communities in an effort to procure the lowest possible price.  From late summer to early fall vehicle 
maintenance staff inspects and prepares the trucks, spreaders, and plows for winter operations.  During the 
construction season road crews address right-of-way issues, overhanging branches, and pavement defects in 
order to eliminate obstacles to snow removal.  Each division within the DPW is involved in winter operations.

FINANCE COMMITTEE REPORT 
Fiscal Year 2021 
The Annual Town meeting is an important event for community members to come together and vote on 
warrants that directly impact how we manage our Town. Under this umbrella is the town budget. Throughout 
the year the Town Manager and Department Heads carefully review expenses and have thoughtful 
conversations about how to best serve the town. While developing the budget they considered funding, 
contracts, and mandates, as well as what the community wants and needs to thrive. A target budget was 
finalized and presentations were in the process of being made by the Town Manager and Department Heads 
through several Finance Committee forums. The process came to a halt when we were struck by the 
Coronavirus Pandemic. Suddenly, people were staying at home and being out of work. This was followed by 
an immediate reduction in normal tax revenue (which continues to dwindle). This means our projections for 
State Aid, local receipts, and other revenue have been decreased (down $300,290 from the pre-COVID19 
budget). To account for this shortfall, and anticipate future revenue shortfalls, the budgets of several 
departments and plans were reduced to leave a $1,055,950 surplus. Even with this, the budget maintains level 
service funding for all departments. Some significant reductions from the target budget are:  
Lunenburg Public Schools $192,157 
Capital Plan   $664,333 
DPW                   $102,000 
Additional Firefighter  $95,359 
Additional Police Officer             $84,664 
 
It is important to note the whole budget will get a fresh review for the fall Special Town Meeting. By that 
time, clearer revenue projections should be available. It could mean that more revenue will come in, or it 
could mean that we need to use the surplus to stay afloat. While we need to implement a budget now, but there 
will be opportunities to make changes to the budget in the future. The total requested budget appropriation is 
$38,658,115. Armed with all that information, the Finance Committee recommends the budget as put forth by 
the Town Manager. It is clearly acknowledging that the budget does the best is can with the resources 
available. The budget, as a whole, is conservative, relying on only available tax levy and state aid for 
operating and capital expenses. It also makes deposits into our stabilization account to allow for future use 
towards unanticipated expenses or to be available for other funding needs that could arise due to COVID19. 
The OPEB allocation, the deposit we make into a fund to pay for future retiree benefits, is also being re-
allocated to the stabilization fund. Each department also played a crucial role in making the budget work. As 
was seen during the budget presentations, all departments follow a sincere mission to serve our community.  
They have goals and initiatives to constantly improve their services to the town. It is with a heavy heart that 
some of the work toward these goals will have to be put on hold. We are grateful for the grace with which 
departments responded to the impacts of the Coronavirus Pandemic. 
While the budget ultimately boils down to dollars and cents, there are factors not easily captured by money but 
are worth mentioning as a way to look holistically at the financial picture of our Town, not just as a response 
to COVID19. 
 The largest part of the Town budget is the school budget. It is 57% of the Town’s expenses, at 
$22,029,972 (including Monty Tech assessment). Additionally, the Town budget includes $1,076,000 in 
other school-related costs such as School Retiree Health Insurance. This is all significant dollars but still 
falls short of what is proposed by the school department for best serving our students. Projected School 
State Aid (Chapter 70) funding changes resulting from the Student Opportunity Act have a projected 
negative impact on Lunenburg. In fact, state aid increased at a smaller rate (down 1.1% or $78,483 less

Albert Einstein is credited with the statement: “The only thing you absolutely have to know is the location 
of the library.” And after 2020, our Lunenburg Public Library is in more virtual locations than ever, making 
it easier to access resources in our community. Our fiscal year 2020 could hardly have held more stark 
contrasts. Our circulation went from being some of the highest rates in years to our doors being closed 
during the spring and the pandemic. The unexpected challenges which arose were met by our library staff’s 
creativity, adaptability, and problem-solving. And in response to those challenges, we have vastly expanded 
our virtual presence for the library. So, even if you don’t know the physical location of the library in 
Lunenburg, you should be able to easily find us with an internet connection. 
In a year that concluded with the rise of the COVID-19 pandemic in the world, our library responded 
quickly and consistently to continue our services during unprecedented change and challenge. Just like the 
people we serve, our staff demonstrated strength and resilience as we put our community’s safety first while 
continuing to meet the needs of that community.  
Between July 2019 and February 2020, the circulation of our physical material was 14% higher than the 
same period a year previously, on track to be the highest in years. During the same period the number of 
visitors rose only slightly. That means people were borrowing more books per visit than the year before and 
that the library improved our selections to provide even more books and materials that are relevant and 
important to our community.  
In March 2020, we closed our doors to the public in response to the pandemic, and our staff began to adapt 
our services to be delivered virtually. Though we did not circulate physical material while we were closed, 
our electronic resources saw a huge jump in use. At the conclusion of June 2020, our e-book and e-
audiobook usage was 150% higher than the previous year as a result of the change in behavior during the 
last three months of the fiscal year.

additional licenses for e-books and e-audiobooks and made them available through the Overdrive Libby 
app. We also relied on the Friends of the Lunenburg Public Library to purchase several additional electronic 
resources, including Beanstack and CreativeBug. Beanstack allows us to organize and administer virtual 
reading challenges, while CreativeBug is a collection art and craft videos and courses. Both have been very 
useful and beneficial to the library community. 
One of the highlights of the year has to be the result of a collaboration that started before the pandemic and 
concluded while we were closed to the public. Hannaford Corporation and their community support 
program donated $3,500 to a renovation project that has resulted in a much improved children’s area. We 
added a new shelving unit, combined with new seating arrangements and display space that has created a 
“tween” area that should provide a safe and welcoming space for those in-between “tween-agers” who are 
too young for the teen room, but may not have felt fully comfortable in a “children’s” room.  
All in all, the challenges and obstacles of the pandemic represent an opportunity for the library to better 
position itself for our community. We hope that our increased virtual presence will continue to be beneficial 
to our users. Our staff has continued to work incredibly hard to adapt and to serve. We are here for you and 
we thank all of our community partners and look forward to continuing to improve!

FY20 ended as a difficult year for PAC, and the community at large, due to COVID19.  W hat started out as a

production switcher and plan additional improvements in FY21.  We don’t know what the future of video

What is the Senior Property Tax Work-Off? 
 
The Senior Property Tax Work-Off Program offers income eligible senior citizens age 60+ an opportunity to 
have an abatement applied to their property tax bill. Participants work in various town departments doing 
jobs such as general office work, custodial, library shelving, Meals on Wheels, kitchen assistance duties, etc., 
to earn the tax abatement. 
 The 2020 tax abatement maximum is $1,500 and applied to your 3rd quarter real estate tax bill. 
 
Eligibility Requirements 
 Applicants must be 60 years of age or older 
 Must occupy and be the owner of the property as of July 1st 
 Receive a property tax bill in their name or their spouse’s name (60 years+) 
 Meet the income guidelines of (proof of income is required):  
     one person household - $50,000   
     two person household - $75,000  
 Earnings are exempt from state taxes 
 Earnings are subject to OBRA if participants are not receiving a pension from either Worcester Regional 
Retirement or Massachusetts State Teachers Retirement 
 Final amount earned will be applied to the 3rd quarter real estate tax  
 All new applicants are required to have a CORI background check  
 All Property Tax Workers must re- apply and be accepted annually for participation in the program

                                                                 SEWER COMMISSION 
The Sewer Commission bears the responsibility of overseeing the Sewer Enterprise Fund, planning for the 
future, and developing and implementing rules, regulations and policies. Under the Town’s Charter, 
responsibility for the day-to-day operation, care and maintenance of the sanitary sewers are under the 
supervision of the Town Manager who delegates the responsibility to manage and maintain the operational 
side of the system to the Department of Public Works’ Director. The Town of Lunenburg does not operate a 
sewer treatment plant. Approximately 85% of Lunenburg sewer is discharged to the City of Leominster and 
the other 15% is discharged to the City of Fitchburg. 
The five-member Sewer Commission was originally established at the Special Town Meeting on May 6, 
2006.  In August 2019, John Reynolds resigned from the Commission and there were 2 vacancies. Former 
Commissioners Dave MacDonald and Mike Nault stepped up to fill the positions on September 3, 2019. At 
the September 24, 2019 meeting, the Commission elected Joan Pease as Chair, Ryan Stober as Vice-Chair 
and Carl Luck as Clerk. In January 2020, Joan and Ryan gave their resignations due to personal reasons, 
and Carl Luck became acting Chair. The Commission welcomed back John Reynolds and new member 
Brett Ramsden in February 2020. With a full board, the Commission elected new officers on 3/10/20 – 
Dave MacDonald-Chair, John Reynolds-Vice Chair and Brett Ramsden-Clerk.  
The Covid-19 crisis resulted in shutdowns during March 2020. The Town of Lunenburg closed all town 
offices with employees working from home. In June offices reopened by appointment only. All meetings 
were held virtually via Zoom for the rest of 2020.  The Town Caucus was cancelled and the Town Meeting 
and Town Election were rescheduled to June. The Commission had 4 openings on the Town Election ballot 
and the following were elected: Dave MacDonald – 1 yr. term, John Reynolds- 2 yr. term and Brett 
Ramsden and Mike Deveikis – 3 yr. terms. At the June 23, 2020 meeting, the Commission re-elected Dave 
MacDonald-Chair, John Reynolds-Vice Chair and Brett Ramsden-Clerk. 
In July 2019, the Commission began the negotiations with Small Water Systems Services (SWSS)  for the 
FY20 continuation of the contract for the Operations and Management (O&M) of the 10 pumping stations, 
emergency generators and sewer infrastructure. There were concerns about the increase in callouts and that 
the pump stations were not running as reliably as in the past. The extra repairs generated invoices that 
exceeded the contracted amount. The Commission met with SWSS in November 2019 and February 2020 
to iron out issues with the service and invoicing. In March 2020, SWSS notified the Commission of their 
intent to terminate their contract effective April 9, 2020. DPW Director Jack Rodriquenz secured Chad T. 
Empey LLC(CTE) as interim O&M contractor. CTE employee, Al Packard, took over maintenance of the 
pump stations and reliability greatly improved, with few callouts. In April 2020, the Commission began 
working on a scope of services for the RFP for the FY21 O&M Contract and put it out for bid in July 2020.   
 There are currently 898 properties connected to the Lunenburg system and another 340 which have access 
to Town Sewer but are not yet connected. During FY20, 24 new accounts connected to Town Sewer. The 
Commission billed out $864,269 in quarterly sewer bills and had $44,864 of prior year delinquencies added 
to the owners’ property tax bills for non-payment.  Four properties were assessed a privilege fee in FY20. 
Three properties assessed were residential. One assessment was for a commercial building. Privilege fees 
help to pay down the debt owed by the Town on the original sewer project. 
During FY20, the Commission worked with the residents at 189 Cross Road for a sewer extension, which 
was approved at the Fall Town Meeting. The Commission is always willing to discuss a potential sewer 
extension when the applicant can show that challenges exist for standard septic systems. 
The new Irrigation Policy went into effect on August 1, 2019, requiring participants to upgrade to a radio 
capable meter by July 1, 2020.  All Irrigation Meter Program customers will be charged an annual fee of 
$50 to participate but can receive credit on their quarterly sewer bills based on their Irrigation meter 
readings. In April 2020, in light of the Covid-19 crisis, the Commission voted to move the deadline for 
installing radio-compatible equipment to September 1, 2020.   
In FY19, the Sewer Commission contracted with Wright Pierce to develop a Maintenance Plan to insure the 
smooth operation of all 10 sewer pump stations. Wright Pierce conducted a thorough evaluation of the 
equipment and maintenance currently done at the pump stations. A draft Maintenance Plan, which included 
an asset management spreadsheet for tracking all maintenance performed, was presented to the Commission  
===PAGE 79===
79 
 
in December 2019.  With input from the Commission, Town Manager and the DPW director, Wright 
Pierce finalized the plan in spring 2020.   
The Sewer Commission developed a scope of work for an Inflow/Infiltration (I/I) 3rd party review, aimed 
at identifying the best use of resources to lessen the I/I entering the sewer system.  The project was 
awarded to Woodard & Curran and work started January 2020.  The Woodard & Curran team compiled 
data from the pump stations and past projects to determine possible sources of I/I. They presented their 
findings on 6/9/20 to the Commission.  A final report with recommendations and an Excel billing tool will 
be presented in FY21.  
In FY20, the Commission continued the annual permit program for the 20 Food Service Establishments 
with grease traps and interceptors on Town Sewer. Due to the financial strain on restaurant owners due to 
the Covid-19 crisis, the Commission voted to waive the Annual Permit fee for the Grease Trap Program. 
The Commission continues to require regular cleanings and submittal of maintenance logs from these 
establishments as Fats, Oils and Grease (FOG) can negatively affect the smooth operation and 
maintenance costs of the sewer system.  While the Grease Trap Regulation focuses on commercial 
disposal, individual homeowners can also contribute a significant amount of grease into the system which 
increases everyone’s costs. Whether on sewer or septic, grease should not be put down the drain.

BOARD OF HEALTH 
The Board of Health Office is located in the old Ritter Library building on the corner of Mass. Ave. and 
Leominster Road.  Our office is on the upper level at the end of the hall on the right.   
The Board of Health, with the assistance of its Agent, the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health, continued  its 
mission to maintain its focus on public health and safety issues during 2020, reviewing Title V Inspection 
Reports, building permits, septic and well permits, nuisance investigations, housing inspections, complaints 
and general public health related issues as well as resident concerns.    
In addition, shortly after the beginning of the year the Board was tasked with responding to the emerging COVID
-19 pandemic.  In accordance with Governor Baker’s Emergency Orders, we are tasked with implementing and 
enforcing emergency orders.  The Board of Health assisted residents and businesses in understanding ever 
changing guidance and compliance with those orders.  We conducted compliance checks and responded to 
complaints.  As the pandemic continued our office assisted residents and business in understanding testing, 
quarantine and necessary compliance.  Because of its size, Nashoba Associated Boards of Health had been able 
to get limited amounts of vaccine and has been able to hold several clinics, vaccinating First Responders, i.e. 
Police, Firemen and EMT personnel from each town while following State requirements.  Clinics have also 
been held for residents over 75 years of age or who suffer 2 or more debilitating medical conditions. In the 
meantime, follow recommendations. Wash hands frequently, wear a mask, and keep six feet rule. 
Homeland Security and Emergency Preparedness planning is ongoing.  Meetings continue between our 
agents, other Town departments and regional entities to develop a plan for not only Lunenburg but with our 
partner towns in our region.   
This year, we processed applications for Septic Permit Lot Tests, permits for new construction, new wells, 
repairs or upgrades to septic systems and well permits.  The Board continues its vigilance with regard to septic 
systems, wells, food service inspections, housing and nuisance complaints and its general mandate to protect 
the environment and groundwater of the Commonwealth.   
The Board of Health provides numerous other services in cooperation with our Health Agents, the Nashoba 
Associated Boards of Health. In addition to our environmental services, nursing services are provided to our 
residents.  This year clinics provided our residents with inoculations for Flu.  
The Town of Lunenburg participates in the Central Mass. Mosquito Control Project.  Mosquito Control 
monitors the mosquito population in Lunenburg and advises of any potential diseases such as Eastern Equine 
Encephalitis Virus and West Nile Virus and sprays frequently to control the population during the warmer 
times of the year.  Residents who want to have their property sprayed can do so by emailing to:  
cmmcp@cmmcp.org, calling (508) 393-3055, completing a request on their webpage at:  http://
www.cmmcp.org , or calling the Board of Health office.  This service is free of charge to residents. 
  
With our Nashoba Nursing and Hospice partners we provide in home visits to residents recovering from 
illness and Hospice care and counseling to those in need of this service. Please contact our office in the Ritter 
Building for more information.     
Please remember that our Board Members volunteer their time and efforts on behalf of you, the Citizens of 
Lunenburg. If you have something that you think we can help you with, we are at your service. Contact the 
Board office at 582-4146 ext. 430.
