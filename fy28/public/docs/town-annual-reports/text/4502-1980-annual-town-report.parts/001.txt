1918 - 1980
Sherwood was born in Lunenburg and was a graduate of Lunenburg High
School, Class of 1937. He was a former Fire Chief and was Chief of
Police from 1954 to 1968 when he retired. During the period 1973 to
April 1979, he served as Chairman and Member of the Board of Selectmen.
Sherwood gave over irty-two years of dedicated service to our community
and devoted his time and knowledge not only to matters of municipal
government, but also to the many civic and professional organizations
of which he was a member.

In Memory of
Edward H. Riley, Sr.
1895 El 1980
Edward H. Riley, Sr. was born in Townsend, but lived in Lunenburg
most of his life and was well known for his articles in the media con-
cerning the history of Lunenburg, and was a member of the Lunenburg
Historical Society. He retired in 1960 after forty-seven years with
the Simonds Saw & Steel Company. During the period March 1933 to

Board of Selectmen: left to right - August N. Misner, Chairman Rudolph Masella and Ann P. Hall.
Astherole of local municipal officials becomes more complex, the Lunenburg Board of Selectmen hasnot in the
least been spared the disruptions that have taken place In the orderly conduct of Town affairs. In the forefront this
year, the strain on departmental budgets was attributed to Increases in the cost of gasoline, heating oil, high inflation
rate, and now Proposition 2½.
The predicament of Town officials in this regard is not unlike that confronting everyone today, and the mea-
sures being taken to cope with the situations are not dissimilar to those adopted by many folks within the Town and
in the nation as a whole. Departments have been encouraged to conserve, to be alert for opportunities to reduce
costs, and to make every effort to operate more efficiently. We trust that each and every one who is Involved In
Town government, whether Town official, administration or employee, recognizes that in today's climate those In
public service to a greater degree than ever are being held accountable for their performance. Local government
is closer to the people than State and Federal government, therefore, It is the first to be called to justify its decisions
and conduct.
Because of the steady growth of the Town, the Selectmen have been concerned about the present structure of
Town government. During the past year, a Study Commission approved by the voters of the last Town meeting, has
been reviewing the varlous Town Departments.
As more services are demanded to meet the concepts of a progressive and expanding community, costs must
Inevitably rise. The Board of Selectmen feels that the rising costs for all services in all departments must be given
their rightful priority. With each passing year, the operation of government creates greater challenges which l'e-
quire greater effort on the part of all charged with public responsibility.

Alleviation of these problems cannot be accomplished by government alone. The solutions are often elusive,
and the application of technology not always possible. The ultimate solutions will require the fullest possible com-
mitment from every segment of our community. Today, more Lunenburg citizens must strive to become actively in-
volved. It would be repetitious to list in detail the events of the past year on a day to day basis, and even to list
capital projects, as these are all covered by the reports of the many Town Committees and Department Heads else-
where in this Report. Their quality and effectiveness is enhanced by your participation.
Government and its services are essential to the life of a Town; however, the economic development of our
community, historical preservation, the environmental protection of our precious natural resources, and the quality
and level of municipal services still depend on all of US in Lunenburg: business, civic groups, clubs, labor and the
general public both young and old. Lunenburg continues to need its citizens to serve on committees, vote on election
day, and make known their opinions to officials through polls, referenda, and by attending hearings and meetings.
Our heritage is the domestic town meeting; let's preserve and cherish this inheritance, let's broaden and enrich the
meaning of active participation and commitment to the community in improving the quality of life for all.
Acting in the capacity of Local Licensing Authority, fifteen licenses were issued for the sale of alcoholic
beverages, sixty-five one day alcoholic beverage licenses were issued, and various public hearings were held in
connection with licensing matters.
The Board of Selectmen wishes to take this opportunity to express its appreciation for all the help and assist-
ance which we received from the various Boards and Committees who generously gave their time to the community
without compensation, and especially the Department Heads and Town employees, and the many interested citizens
who submitted constructive suggestions and comments.

Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors: left to right - Retired Secretary Ine I. Pihlaja,
Chairman William F. Reed, Kathryn M. Herrick and Bernard J. Paton.
The Board of Assessors wishes to note the retirement of Mrs. Ine 1. Pihlaja, Secretary to the Board for the past
six and one-half years, prior to which Mrs. Pihlaja worked in the Tax Collector's Office. We wish Ine good luck in
her retirement and will certainly miss her in the Assessors' Office. Mrs. Jacqueline A. Keeler has been retained to
fill the vacancy and will be available to assist the public during posted office hours. The Assessors' Office will no
longer be open between 12:00 Noon and 1:00 P.M.
The year 1980 saw the passage of Proposition 2½ and the Assessors have eliminated a half-day clerk-typist
position to comply with the 20% budget reduction requested by the Finance Committee. As a result, the office will
have to be closed during the lunch hour. The second effect on the taxpayers of the passage of Proposition 2½ was a
reduction in the excise tax rate from $66.00 per thousand valuation to $25.00 per thousand valuation on automobiles.
In the determination of your 1980 tax rate, the Assessors, with the approval of the Department of Revenue,
concluded that Proposition 2½ would be approved and that the Town's income during the second half of Fiscal Year
1981 would be reduced by $320,000.00 due to the excise tax reduction. The results of the voter's action at the
Town Meeting and the reduction in income was a $9.15 increase or 18% in the tax rate. The 10% across-the-board
increase in assessed valuation of all property resulted in a new tax rate of $54.00 per thousand valuation. Proposi-
tion 2½ should result in a 15% reduction in property taxes this year if not modified by the State Legislature.
The Assessors will be requesting funds this year from the Town Meeting to revalue the Town. We have been
informed by the Bureau of Local Assessment of the Department of Revenue that we must revalue all property in the
Town and be certified by the Department of Revenue prior to classification. We have also been informed that this
revaluation must be done by a professionally qualified firm, approved by the State, and must include all property
in the Town. In the event the funds are not voted at Town Meeting, the Department of Revenue is threatening to
go to Superior Court to obtain a court order to obtain the services of an appraisal firm and revalue the Town, all
costs to be deducted from next year's Cherry Sheet returns to the Town. This is the situation at this time and your
Assessors are continuing a discussion with the Department of Revenue to avoid or reduce the costs of an revaluation
to the Town.

Planning Board
Planning Board Minutes October 10, 1950: "A discussion was held relative to subdivision rules. It is still
pioneer work and we are the pioneers and we are really laying a road thru a wildemess." While much progress in
Lunenburg has been made in the thirty years since this statement, the Planning Board is constantly faced with prob-
lems and questions that seem never to have been asked before, let alone any answers easily available. The Board
attempts to find solutions by coordinating with the Selectmen, Town Counsel, Conservation Commission, Zoning
Board of Appeals, Highway Superintendent, Building Inspector and State and Regional Agencies.
The aerial maps that were done in May 1950 have proved to be a valuable aid to all planning processes. The
maps show where roads were as of that date, and the Board thereby has on easier task proving what roads can be built
upon without becoming a subdivision. This is a question which is arising more often as land costs increase. As re-
quired by law the Board has processed plans submitted that are believed not to require subdivision approval. There
appears to be no major increase in the division of land in Lunenburg in 1980.
A 25-lot subdivision was proposed at the end of Gilchrest Street with a provision for extension to Island Road.
A preliminary plan was presented, reviewed by the Board, and approved with certain reservations. However, the
applicant withdrew the subdivision before a definitive plan was submitted. The applicant plans to develop the prop-
erty as frontage lots and not as a subdivision.

At the May 1980 Special Town Meeting, the Planning Board supported and the Town voted in a Zoning Map
change adding approximately 150 acres of P.J. Keating Company property on Leominster-Shirley Road to the Com-
mercial Zone. The Board determined that the rezoning was in compliance with the requirement that the land be
zoned for usefor which it is best suited. The Planning Board also sponsored an article for funds to update the Town's
Master Plan, last done in 1961. The Board moved to pass over the article in the belief that a regular Town Meeting
was the proper forum. Although we believe that this supdate is necessary to assess the effects of I-190, water quality
concerns, future growth, as well as the effects of Proposition 2½, the Board has decided to postpone any request for
funds until 1982 due to the uncertainty surrounding 2½. We are searching for alternative funding as well as services
and input from the Montachusett Regional Planning Commission at a lower cost than a private consultant.
Also at the Special Town Meeting there were articles for acceptance of several streets in Hickory Hills. The
Planning Board recommended that the Town not accept the roads unless they met a reasonable standard. The Town
voted not to accept the streets, and the Planning Board appointed an ad hoc committee to establish the necessary
standards. Representatives from the Board of Selectmen, Hickory Hills Landowners, Inc., Highway Department and
Planning Board met every month from June until December, The committee utilized current rules and regulations for
new subdivisions and adapted them to cover existing streets. The Planning Board has adopted the ad hoc committee's
report.
The Planning Board accepted with regret the resignation of Joseph J. Burns. This vacancy was filled by the
appointment of Patricia C. Lewis who was appointed by an unanimous joint vote of the Planning Board and Board of
Selectmen until the next town election.
Chairman Robert J. Ebersole attended a conference in November of the Massachusetts Federation of Planning
Boards held in Framingham. The Board is currently working to update various zoning provisions and the Subdivision
Rules and Regulations. Our files are being updated by the addition of a card catalog cross-reference system to allow
for easy and complete access to our records.

The following is an excerpt from a pamphlet from the
United States Department of Agriculture Animal and Plant
Health Inspection Service and pertains to the gypsy moth.
"The gypsy moth is a foreign insect that has become.
one of the United States' most important forest pests. A
single defoliation cankill some softwood trees; two or more
defoliations can kill many types of hardwoods.
Gypsy moths became established in this country in
1869, after specimens imported from Europe escaped from
a laboratory in Massachusetts. The gypsy moth became a
pest becauset country did not have the natural enemies
that help keep the pest in check in Europe and Asia.
In 1968, gypsy moth infestations were observed for
the first time in the Newark, New Jersey watershed. Over
the next two summers, nearly 18,000 acres of trees sus-
tained repeated 75% to 100% defoliations on nearly all
susceptible tree species. By 1971, New Jersey foresters
reported more than 1,000,000 oaks, 39,000 eastern hem-
locks and 8,000 white pines killed as a direct result of
gypsy moth feeding.
Since the first gypsy moth infestation more than 100
years ago, the pest has spread to all six New Englandst
Richard W. Brown, Tree Warden
Although gypsy moths spread naturally, they are also car-
ried long distances by hitchhiking with goods and people.
Recreational vehicles and outdoor household effects moved from infested to uninfested areas are potential sources of
spread. To stop gypsy moth hitchhiking, owners should check the undersides and exterior areas of motor homes and
travel trailers. Homeowners should check all outdoor furniture, toys, and firewood before moving from infested
areas. Any egg masses, caterpillars, pupae, or moths found should be removed and destroyed.
State, Federal and local governments cooperate in limiting gypsy moth spread. Combinations of several con-
trol methods are often used to achieve the best possible blend of effective control and environmental protection:
Quarantines - USDA and related State quarantines are enforced to restrict movements of timber and timber products,
woody plants and nursery stock and other hazardous articles to keep gypsy moths from hitchhiking to new areas.
Parasites and Hundreds of thousands of natural enemies are reared each year, then released to attack and destroy various
stages of gypsy moth. These parasites were originally imported from foreign countries where they are some help in
keeping gypsy moth numbers down. Parasite species are carefully screened prior to introduction to make certain they
will not endanger beneficial insects and the United States ecology. Sex Attractant we The use of the sex attractant
as a possible control for the gypsy moth is still under investigation. Microbial Insecticide - A commercial formula-
tion of a naturally occurring pathogen of gypsy moths is applied with limited effect to knock down moderate insect
populations. Pesticides for Several chemical products are registered with the Environmental Protection Agency and are
used to protect trees and stop artificial spread by hitchhiking insects."

Park Commission
The Summer Recreation Program for 1980 was a great success. Under the fine leadership of our Director,
Richard Stachowicz, the children of Lunenburg once again found plenty of variety to keep them busy during the
long summer days. He was ably assisted by Karen Lindsay at the Wallis Park Playground and Cindy St. Arnaud at
the Center Playground. In addition, the Arts and Crafts Program under the direction of Ann O'Sullivan and Brenda
St. Amaud was a huge success. The quality of work and enthusiasm of all involved, once again resulted in good
attendance daily at our playgrounds.
The Men's Softball Program had another good year with R. A. Powell and Webnik winning their respective
divisions, and Lakeview Club winning the Playoff Championship. Our thanks go out to the Lunenburg High School
for the addition of a second field. This eliminated the necessity of using Marshall Park. The Park Baseball League
enjoyed another successful year under the direction of Bill Hughey.
Junior League Street Hockey and Basketball ran smoothly under the direction of John Long. The Summer High
School Basketball Program was again successful. Leominster Recreation and Oakmont Recreation won their respective
divisions, and Leominster won the League Playoff Championship.
The Girl's Softball Programs were again successful thanks to Chuck Wennerberg. The Summer Recreation Pro-
gram concluded another year with its fifteenth Annual Muscular Dystrophy fund raiser. A total of $283.22 was col-
lected for this most worthy cause. The Town Beach was ably directed by Jerry Beloin, and the Swim Instruction Pro-
gram was again a huge success.
The Life Course was completed and officially dedicated in July. The Park Commission wishes to thank the Life
Course Association under the direction of Brian Roberts and Pat Slattery for donating this course to the citizens of
Lunenburg. Many organizations, businesses and individuals donated to this fine civic project. In addition, many
hours of labor were required to install the exercise stations along the course layout. To all who donated their time
and/or funds, we say, "Thank You". The people of Lunenburg will have many years of enjoyment ahead.
Our Commission would like to thank all who in any way helped to make our programs so successful. To Bob
Garneau, who retired this year, we wish many years of health and relaxation ahead. Once again, special thanks
go out to our Town Clerk, Mary Padula, and her fine staff.
In closing, the Commissioners would like to appeal to the Townspeople to bear with US this year as the ef-
fects of Proposition 2½ take hold. We shall do everything possible to minimize the impact that the reduced budget
will have on our recreational programs.
The Park Commission consists of Chairman Armand E. St. Arnaud, Richard A. Kinney and Richard J. Bishop.
Our Superintendent is Richard P. Bickford.

The year 1980 has been one of both change and growth for the Ritter Memorial Library. John Ramsay, Librar-
ian since 1974, left the Library to become Director of the Auburn Public Library. During his tenure in Lunenburg,
he was responsible for many improvements in service, including the rearrangement of the Library which provided both
a separate children's room and a meeting room for community groups. John was given a very successful send-off
party in February during the Children's Art Festival, when the Board of Trustees presented the Library with free passes
to the Boston Museum of Fine Arts in John's honor. In March, Diane Polk became Librarian after serving as Director
of Children's Services at the Levi Heywood Memorial Library in Gardner.
One major change during 1980 was the creation of a special collection of books for young adults. The new
"Y" section, serving middle and high school age patrons, is located on the second floor in an area recently painted
and carpeted. All new materials were purchased with income from Library trust funds. The main floor was also given
a new look when materials were rearranged to provide an attractive display area for new adult books.
During the summer a drainage problem on the lower level was corrected, allowing the bathrooms to be reno-
vated. A walkway was also installed around the front of the building, in conjunction with a special parking space
in back, making the Library accessible to handicapped patrons unable to use the stairs at the main entrance. Sev-
eral new services were introduced in 1980: a book delivery service for home-bound patrons, a typewriter available
for public use, and a 16mm film projector for loan to patrons. A plain paper copier was installed to provide better
quality copying service. The Lunenburg Community Council presented the Library with a Community Calendar which
lists all community activities occurring each month. Monthly delivery of a collection of books to Pearl Brook housing
was continued.
A wide variety of children's programs was offered during the year under the direction of Emily Foster. The
pre-school story hour has continued to grow during the year and about 75 children currently enjoy this service. A
very successful Christmas party was held for this group in December. All story hour materials were purchased by the
Friends of the Library. In February, the Friends sponsored the Children's Art Festival which is a cooperative effort
between the Elementary School Art Department and the Library, and provides an opportunity for children to exhibit
their art work. A puppet show was offered during February vacation, and an Easter Craft program was held in the
spring. The summer program, which featured a "Monster Masher" Reading Club, was especially popular. Summer
activities included movies, puppet shows, workshops, a magicshow, adisplay of armor from the Higgins Armory,
a visit from make-up artist Murl Daniels, and gymnastic and roller skating demonstrations. During the fall, a Chil-
dren's Film Festival was held and Karen Kemp directed a Christmas ornament workshop. Each Primary School class
visited the children's room every other week for a story hour and to take out books.
Many programs were offered for adults under the sponsorship of the Friends. An "Interest Series" was held in
both spring and fall, with programs on a variety of topics from crafts to vacation planning. Several "International
Night" programs were offered, arranged by Emily Foster and Lorraine Mendoza. The countries covered included
Australia and Africa presented by John and Cynthia Walker, Italy by Vincent and Diane Glannetti, Iran by Stanley
and Barbara Chittick, and Switzerland by Alfred and Cecile Chevaller. The Friends held a yard sale and a "Book
and Bake" sale for the benefit of the Library. The proceeds were used to pay for several of the summer programs and
to purchase a free pass to the Boston Children's Museum.
The Library received several gifts during the year. The Conservation Commission purchased a book of animals
In memory of Harold Harley. The Woman's Club gave a gardening book in memory of Lila Culver. The Memorial
Fund purchased a number of books in memory of both Lila Culver and Minnle Martin. The Leominster Savings Bank
donated several copies of Sylvia Porter's "Money Book", and many other patrons donated a number of books, both
for addition to the collection and for the Friends' book sale.
The Library participated In resource sharing projects with other area libraries. Through a federal grant ob-
tained by the Fitchburg Public Library, our Library conducted a community survey during the spring and summer to

Highway Department
Equipment supervisors know that preventive
maintenance is perhaps the most important aspect of
their jobs. A good preventive maintenance program
keeps equipment operating as efficiently as possible.
It uncovers defects before they lead to unanticipated
failures, with disruptive effects on the services that
the machine is providing. When a machine is oper-
ated until it breaks, costs soar and productivity de-
clines. The costly investment is squandered.
The value of a community's streets, as mea-
sured by the cost of replacing them, is likely to be
far greater than that of any other asset the community
owns. If it's reasonable to protect the value of the
community's equipment fleet by a preventive main-
tenance program, how much more important is it to
institute a similar program for an asset that would
cost far more to replace?
A typical preventive maintenance activity is
the placing of a seal coat on a pavement that is be-
ginning to show signs of allowing water to penetrate
to the subgrade. Placing the seal coat postpones the
date when major reconstruction of the roadway will
become necessary.

By vote of Town Meeting 1980, some comprehensive changes were made in the Salary Administration Plan,
notably the Vacation section, which was revised in such a way as to avoid creating a large unfunded liability for
the Town, but without removing any benefit from the individual employee. The Salary Schedule was also revised,
not only by a cost-of-living increase, but also by a streamlining of the schedule so as to bring minimums in line
and remove excessive step-rates. If was noted at the time that certain other sections of the Plan warranted review,
and to these sections the Committee has turned Its attention during the past year.
The most Important section reviewed during this period was the Sick Leave provision, which has been studied
in depth, both from the point of view of protection for the employee and from that of cost to the Town. As a possible
alternative to the present system, various forms of income-protection insurance were looked into. However, we
find that few towns carry such insurance, primarily because the premiums are extremely high for such Insurance as
would fully protect the employee, and such insurance as the Town might comfortably afford is less than adequate in
coverage. This being the case, the provision has been left as is, notwithstanding some deficiencies.
The Articlebeing presented by this Committee to Town Meeting 1981 contains wording aimed at clarification of
two sections, but nothing constituting a change in administrative policy. In arriving at a cost-of-living adjustment
to the Salary Schedule, the Personnel Committee, like every other administrative body, has been faced with the Impli-
cations of Proposition 21, and what isbeing proposedis the result of necessary compromise. To their Immense credit,
Department Heads and individual employees have reacted to the situation in a spirit of total cooperation. No re-
classifications havebeen proposed and there has been no campaigningon anyone's part for the kind of Increase that
might be dictated by burgeoning inflation. It would seem that Lunenburg's personnel, whether they were in favor
of Proposition 2½ or not to begin with, have determined to work at living with it.

Conservation Commission
As we enter the decade of the eighties, all of US are being made more aware of the importance of the most
precious of our resources clean, clear water. The Conservation Commission pledges to do its utmost to protect
and preserve this basic necessity of our lives.
Weather forecasters have warned US of an extended period of drought in the next few years. Wetlands are
nature's storage area for this precious resource and the Conservation Commission has been directed by State law to
protect their wetlands.
Out of the twenty-four regular and special meetings held this year, the Conservation Commission held twelve
public hearings to aid in the implementation of these laws. Over twenty-five requests to determine the applicability
of the Wetlands Protection Act were also responded to. Each request requires on on-site inspection. If on inspection
determines that the law is applicable, a hearing is scheduled. These hearings result in an order of condition. The
Commission responds to applications, holds hearings and issues orders of condition as rapidly as possible to minimize
the waiting time of the landowner to start his project.
When it has been determined that a hearing is required, the landowner must pay the $25.00 fee. This fee
goes directly into the Town treasury. The Conservation Commission hires on outside impartial secretary to prepare
the necessary legal advertisement, send registered notices to the abutters, and to record the minutes of the hearing.
The average cost per hearing is $20.00 and is taken from the Commission budget. In 1980 the Town treasury took
in $300.00 in fees, the Conservation Commission spent $240.00 from its budget this seems a little unfair. This
Commission will abide by the dictates of Proposition 2½ and is cutting its budget of $965.00 by 20%.
The Conservation Commission enjoys the cooperation of the Building Inspector, Austin R. Fitzsimmons, and
wishes to thank him for this cooperation. We also wish to thonk the Board of Selectmen and the Highway Superin-
tendent for their counsel and help during the past year.

Inspector of Plumbing & Gas Fitting
For the year ending December 31, 1980, a total of
188 inspections were completed. The breakdown is as fol-
lows: 53 Plumbing Permits for new dwellings; 48 Plumbing
Permits for remodeled homes; 3 Permits for solar installa-
tions; and 4 Permits for commercial buildings. There were
76 Gas Permits for various gas appliances and 4 Gas Permits
were issued for commercial buildings.
Consumer health and safety are the main concerns of
your Inspector of Plumbing and Gas Fitting. The following
are some guidelines which the Inspector believes will be
helpful to consumers.
Wood burning stoves and boilers cannot be connected
to the same chimney where a gas appliance or gas boiler has
been installed. Fuel oil heaters and boilers can be vented
into the same chimney where gas appliances are installed.
Before the Plumbing Inspector can sign the Certificate of
Occupancy, all new dwellings must fulfill the requirements
of a rough and finish test inspection in compliance with all
Thomas E. Mortis
provisions of the statutes, rules, and regulations which re-
late to the Plumbing Code.
It is imperative that all solar heat installations be accompanied by a Plumbing Permit which may be obtained
at the Town Hall. This will give the Inspector an opportunity to examine the solar system for proper and safe instal-
lation for the consumer. The principle of solar heat is the transfer of thermal energy from one liquid to another. In
case of tube failure or improper valve placement, toxic liquids could possibly contaminate a portable drinking water
supply. To prevent such a disastrous situation from occurring, it is advisable that the homeowner protect himself by
requiring a Plumbing Permit from the solar heat installer before any work is done on his property.

Dog Officer
During 1980 the Dog Officer received and in-
vestigated 605 calls. At this time there are approx-
imately 1, 140 dogs licensed in Lunenburg - due in
large part to summer and fall notices being mailed
out and numerous telephone calls. Citations are be-
ing issued at the time of any violation of the Restrain-
ing By-law which was voted on and passed at the
1979 Annual Town Meeting. The fines are: $10.00
for 1st Offense and $25.00 for each subsequent of-
fense. Citations are also being issued for unlicensed
dogs with a fine of $15.00. During 1980 a total of
428 citations were issued by the Dog Officer.
In three instances, the Dog Officer after due
investigation, found it necessary to ask that Perma-
nent Restraining Orders be placed on these dogs due
to the serious problems they had created in Town.
The Dog Officer in a few instances to remove a
few dogs from the Town completely in order to insure
the safety of the citizens of Lunenburg.
Also, 69 stray dogs were picked up in Town
during the year. Fourteen dogs were destroyed due
to untreatable llnesses, 9 dogs were claimed by own-
ers, and the remaining 46 dogs were given away to
persons who can provide good homes for these stray
animals. The Dog Officer works diligently in find-
ing the right home for the right dog and truly be-
lieves that every good dog deserves the chance to
live.
Kathleen M. Comeau, Dog Officer
There is still a continuing problem with dogs
whose owners work and allow their dogs to run loose
and unattended. These dogs cause frequent aggravation to other residents, and in many ways become public nul-
sances. Often some of these dogs are picked up by the Dog Officer severely Injured and in some cases the dogs are
found dead in the roadway. In the past six months there have been 23 dogs killed on our roads,
The problem of dogs running loose In the school yard has Improved. The rule is still in force by School offi-
cials and the Dog Officer that no dogs will be allowed in the school yard at any time. The safety of our children
is vitally important, and since this is a constant problem, the rule will be strictly enforced at all times. The Dog
Officer checks the school grounds twice a day, seven days a week, and school officials and personnel are also
assisting in this matter. There will be no exceptions.
The Dog Officer wishes to remind all dog owners that should your dog become lost, there Is no possible way
for anyone to know who the owner of the dog is unless the dog has proper tags on Its collar. If all dogs were 11-
censed and wore identification tags, there would not be one lost dog In Town. Such a dog could be promptly re-
turned to Itsowner and tax dollars would be saved, as the Dog Officer would not have to spend many hours feeding
and caring for stray dogs.
The Dog Officer wishes at this time to say thank you to the Board of Selectmen, Town Clerk and the Town Hall
staff for all the assistance given her, and also to the Police Department for their assistance. A special thanks to the
people in Lunenburg who have cared enough to take a dog In when Injured or lost and have taken time to call her
and in many of these cases have saved the dog's life. The Dog Officer is available at all times no matter how small
the problem may be and will do her best to help each and every resident in Town.

Government agencies, planning boards and individual citizens are recognizing that access to the arts is not
only the right of every human being, but also that the arts can contribute significantly to the solution of urban and
rural programs. The State government, therefore, established a State Arts Lottery.
The Lunenburg Arts Council under the State Council voluntari serves the community by providing anetwork
whereby arts activities can be developed, supported and delivered at the local level. This group serves as a consor-
tium of local cultural institutions or acts as a clearinghouse for information andresources to assist local arts programs
to become more effective.
The State Arts Lottery Council has established guidelines for cities and towns for use of Arts Lottery funds. The
local Council determines If applicants for funds comply with these guidelines; certifying payment to and overseeing
the ultimate use of the funds by eligible recipients.

Most of US have for a long time suspected that the wheels of government do not turn very quickly and our
suspicions have been more than upheld as the 1979 timetable has stretched from fall of 1979 to spring of 1980 to
spring of 1981. In addition to the usual governmental red tape, our Engineers, Dufresne-Henry Engineering Corp.
of Springfield, Vermont, have had to contend with a Presidential freeze of EPA fund disbursements which occurred

study can be conducted during late spring of this year. We anticipate at least one public hearing will be held this

Financial Report of Treasurer & Tax Collector
As the Town Report goes to press, Lunenburg Officials are still
laboring long and hard to determine to what extent particular budgets
will be cut to accommodate Proposition 2½. To assume that these
decisions will be devoid of bias and will necessarily represent the best
interests of the citizenry is to believe in Santa Claus and the Easter
Bunny, for we are speaking of a highly political process.
FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT: I like to think that my experience
in, and knowledge of, municipal finance lends credence to the thoughts
and recommendations which this report puts forth with regard to the
implementation of Proposition 2½ in our Town. In view of 2½, I was
asked to speak at the Mass. Municipal Association's Annual Meeting,
and to the Annual Meeting of Mass. Finance Committees, on the
Financial Management of Massachusetts Cities and Towns. Using Lun-
enburg as a model, my was to demonstrate that good financial man-
agement and sufficiently staffed municipal finance offices can generate
hundreds of thousands of additional revenue dollars. As President
of the Mass. Collectors' and Treasurers' Association, I served as Dean
of the Tenth Annual School for Collectors and Treasurers held at the
University of Massachusetts where together with the State Director of
Accounts, Harvey Beth, I headed up a Cash Management Colloquium.
Mary L. Padula
I ampresently serving as a member of an Advisory Committee to Improve
Local Government Financial Capabilities created by the Executive
Office of Communities and Development and was chosen to represent
Massachusetts in a "Needs Assessment Workshop" conducted in Chicago by the National Municipal Finance Officers
Association to assist HUD in determining the future course of its Financial Management Capacity Sharing Program.
I have been asked by the Massachusetts Municipal Association, of which our Selectmen and Finance Committee are
members, to participate in a pilot Executive Transfer Program wherein financial managers in towns which have already
developed "impressive" cash management programs are linked with executives of other communities who are desirous
of more information in the Financial Management area. My best credential and the one of which I am most proud,
is having managed the finances of the Town of Lunenburg for the past 28 years, for throughout the State, Lunenburg's
financial management is considered impressive and is ranked among the best.
It is assumed that Lunenburg taxpayers, by their overwhelming vote on the subject, do want and expect
Lunenburg officials to make every attempt to comply with the 2½ Mandate, and do want and expect to be provided
with the very best Government possible within the constraints of Proposition 2½.
In recent years, new emphasis has been placed on the need for improved Financial Management in view of
what is deemed to be the precarious financial condition of some Massachusetts cities and towns. This year, even
more emphasis is being placed on Financial Management because of Proposition 2½ and there is a growing concern
that cuts in financial budgets will result in loss of income that will far surpass the small amount of savings realized
by reducing financial office staffs. At present, Lunenburg's tax bills go out on schedule, tax collections are right
up to the minute, our accounts receivable do not include any back property taxes, our tax titles are foreclosed as
quickly as possible and we cash manage to the hilt. The 33% reduction in staff contemplated in the Town Office
will hurt every department in town, as appreciably less income will be generated. Tightening bootstraps, stream-
lining procedures, eliminating waste can offset the elimination of 1½ positions ($14,500 budget savings) without
reducing the amount of income generated over a 12 month period. However, the elimination of 1½ additional staff
people to cut the budget another $14,500 will result in a reduction of income that will far surpass the budget savings
realized by making such a cut, because the areas that generate the most income are the most expendable. That is
to say, we have to perform a prescribed number of accounting functions, we have to pay bills and people, we are
required by law to carry out certain basic tasks with regard to vital statistics, elections and public records and
by-laws. Whereas we have to collect taxes and accounts receivable we are not compelled to collect them fast.
Whereas we have to safeguard town money and properly account for same, we are not required by law to place it on
interest or in any way invest idle funds. This means that when this office cuts back to a level which probably is
quite average, because most city and town finance offices are unbelievably understaffed, we will lose thousands
and thousands of dollars more than will be saved by making such cuts.

With regard to the management of idle funds, it has been determined that on average Lunenburg invests 102%
of its idle cash. This is because very carefully monitor our cash flow, watch the float, and fragment our warrants
making separate deposits to cover each division. We will earn $200,000 in interest this year which will mean an
additional $65,000 in free cash to offset F.Y. 82's tax rate. Even though there is a lot of pressure on cities and
towns to cash manage, the average city or town invests in the vicinity of 60% of its idle funds. If we invested at
that level our earnings this year would be cut more than $70,000. With three less people in the office, our invest-
ment yield would be average and would stand to lose $70,000. In addition, our property tax levy closeouts would
fall back a year, thereby postponing the collection of an additional $75,000 to $100,000. Good cash management
does not merely relate to how a treasurer invests his idle funds. It touches on and influences almost every move a
tax collector and a treasurer makes. Any extra clerical help properly utilized in the cash management process will
pay for itself several times over in extra investment earnings.
FREE CASH is a Town's surplus revenue less any outstanding property taxes. It is used at Town Meeting to
offset amounts that would otherwise be raised by taxation. How much free cash should reserved in a city or town
to obviate the need for short-term borrowing, to provide a reserve, to guard against unforseen situations, or to
enhance the financial well-being of a community (the real estate of individual property owners is, after all, pledged
against such well-being or lack thereof) is an age-old question. A bonus to communities which opt to "keep money
in the bank for a rainy day" is the added income that can be generated in the form of investment earnings. More
emphasis is being placed on reserving larger amounts of free cash, the concensus among persons involved in Municipal
Finance seems to have established $500,000 as a reasonable reserve for towns our size.
The following chart demonstrates that during the last five years Lunenburg has closed the year with an average
of $550,000+ in free cash and has appropriated an average of $275,000, or about half that amount, reserving an
average of $275,000. One of the biggest factors in the rebuilding of free cash is the underestimating of receipts.
In Fiscal '81, instead of building up an excess due to the Excise Tax, we anticipate a deficit of $40,000. This is
one of the biggest factors contributing to the low estimate of free cash available for appropriation in 1982 to offset
F. Y. '83 budgets. (See last column below $294,849 total, less $200,000 minimum reserve would leave only
$94,849 for appropriation) I strongly recommend that we use $265,000 (instead of $365,000) at the April Meeting
so that we can build back to at least the $200,000 mark for use in F.Y. '83 to offset amounts that would otherwise
be raised by taxation.
