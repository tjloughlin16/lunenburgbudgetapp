YES, 63 NO, 70 AMEND THE TOWN BY-LAWS BY ADDING THERETO À NEW ARTICLE XXI, AS FOLLOWS:
GENERAL WETLANDS PROTECTION BYLAW
SECTION 1: Purpose The purpose of this bylaw is to protect the wetlands, related water resources, and adjoining land ares in this municipality by
controlling activities deemed by the Conservation Commission likely to have a significant or cumulative effect upon wetland values, including but not
limited to the following: public or private water supply, groundwater, flood control, erosion and sedimentation control, storm damage prevention, water
pollution, fisheries, shellfish, wildlife habitat, recreation, aesthetics, agriculture, and aquaculture values (collectively, the "wetland values
protected by this bylaw").
SECTION 2: Jurisdiction Except as permitted by the Conservation Commission or as provided in this bylaw, no person shall remove, fill, dredge, build
upon, or alter the following resource areas within 100 feet of any freshwater wetland, coastal wetland, marsh, wet meadow, bog or swamp; within 100 feet
of any bank, beach, or flat; any lake, river, pond, stream, estuary, or the ocean; within 100 feet of any land subject to flooding or inundation by
groundwater, or surface water.
SECTION 3: Exceptions The permit and application required by this bylaw shall not be required for maintaining, repairing, or replacing, but not
substantially changing or enlarging, an existing and lawfully located structure or facility used in the service of the public to provide electric, gas,
water, telephone, telegraph or other telecommunication services, provided that the structure or facility is not substantially changed or enlarged,
provided that written notice has been given to the Commission prior to commencement of work, and provided that the work conforms to performance standards
and design specifications in regulations adopted by the Commission.
The permit and application required by this bylaw shall not apply to emergency projects necessary for the protection of the health or safety of the
public, provided that the work is to be performed by or has been ordered to be performed by an agency of the Commonwealth or a political subdivision
thereof, provided that advance notice, oral or written, has been given to the Commission prior to commencement of work or within 24 hours after
commencement, provided that the Conservation Commission or its agent certifies the work as an emergency project, provided that the work is performed only
for the time and place certified by the Conservation Commission for the limited purposes necessary to abate the emergency, and provided that within 21
days of commencement of an emergency project a permit application shall be filed with the Commission for review as provided in this bylaw. Upon failure
to meet these and other requirements of the Commission, the Commission may, after notice and a public hearing, revoke or modify an emergency project
approval and order restoration and mitigation measures.
Other than stated in this section the exceptions provided in the Wetlands Protection Act shall not apply.

by this bylaw affecting resource areas protected by this bylaw. The application shall include such information and plans as are deemed necessary by the

permit issued pursuant to this bylaw.
The Commission in an appropriate case may accept as the application and plans under this bylaw the Notice of Intent and plans filed under the Wetlands
Protection Act, G.L. C. 131, Sec. 40.

SECTION 5: Notice of hearings. Any person filing an application or request for determination with the Commission at the same time shall give written

The Commission shall commence the public hearing within 21 days from receipt of a completed application or request for determination.
The Commission shall issue its permit or determination in writing within 21 days of the close of the public hearing thereon.
The Commission in an appropriate case may combine its hearing under this bylaw with the hearing conducted under the Wetlands Protection Act, G.L. c.131,

The Commission shall have authority to continue the hearing to a date certain announced at the hearing, for reasons stated at the hearing, which may
include receipt of additional information offered by the applicant or others, information and plans required by the applicant, deemed necessary by the
Commission in its discretion, or comments and reconsendations of boards and officials listed in Section 6. In the event the applicant objects to a
continuance or postponement, the hearing shall be closed and the Commission shall take action on such information as is available.
SECTION 7: Permits, Determinations, and Conditions If the Commission after a public hearing determines that the activities which are the subject of the
application are likely to have a significant or cumulative effect upon the wetland values protected by this bylaw, the Commission, within 21 days of the
close of the hearing, shall issue or deny a permit for the activities requested. If it issues a permit, the Commission shall impose conditions which the
Commission deems necessary or desirable to protect those values, and all activities shall be done in accordance with those conditions.
The Commission is empowered to deny a permit for failure to meet the requirements of this bylaw; for failure to submit necessary information and plans
requested by the Commission; for failure to neet the design specifications, performance standards, and other requirements in regulations of the
Commission; for failure to avoid or prevent unacceptable significant or cumulative effects upon the wetland values protected by this bylaw; and were no
conditions shall be given to any demonstrated hardship on the applicant by reason of denial, as presented at the public hearing.
A permit shall expire three years from the date of issuance. Notwithstanding the above, the Commission in its discretion may issue a permit expiring five
years from the date of issuance for recurring or continuous maintenance work, provided that annual notification of time and location of work is given to
the Commission. Any permit may be renewed once for an additional one year period, provided that a request for a renewal is received in writing by the
Commission prior to expiration.
For good cause the Commission may revoke or modify a permit issued under this bylaw after public notice and public hearing, and notice to the holder of
the permit.
The Commission in an appropriate case may combine the permit or other action on an application issued under this bylaw with the Order of Conditions issued
under the Wetlands Protection Act.
SECTION 8: Regulations After public notice and public hearing the same as required to adopt zoning bylaws the Commission shall promulgate rules and
regulations to effectuate the purposes of this bylaw. Failure by the Commission to promulgate such rules and regulations or a legal declaration of their
invalidity by a court of law shall not act to suspend or invalidate the effect of this bylaw.
At a minimum these regulations shall define key terms in this bylaw not inconsistent with this bylaw.
SECTION 9: Definitions The following definitions shall apply in the interpretation and implementation of this bylaw.
The term "person" shall include any individual, group of individuals, association, partnership, corporation, company, business organization, trust,
estate, the Commonwealth or political subdivision thereof to the extent subject to town bylaws, administrative agency, public or quasi-public corporation
or body, this municipality, and any other legal entity, its legal representatives, agents or assigns.
The term "alter" shall include, without limitation, the following activities when undertaken to, upon, within or affecting resource areas protected by
this bylaw:
(a) Removal, excavation or dredging of soil, sand, gravel, or aggregate materials of any kind;
(b) Changing of preexisting drainage characteristics, flushing characteristics, salinity distribution, sedimentation patterns, flow patterns, or flood
retentioncharacteristics,
(c) Drainage or other disturbance of water level or water table;
(d) Dumping, discharging or filling with any material which may degrade water quality;
(e) Placing of fill, or removal of material, which would alter elevation;
(f) Driving of piles, erection or repair of buildings, or structures of any kind;
(g) Placing of obstructions or objects in water;
(b) Destruction of plant life including cutting of trees;
(i) Changing water temperature, biochemical oxygen demand, or other physical or chemical characteristics of water;
(j) Any activities, changes or work which may cause or tend to contribute to pollution of any body of water or groundwater.

4.6. Water Supply Protection District
4.6.1 Purpose
4.6.1.2. The purpose of the Water Supply Protection District is to promote the health, safety, and general welfare of the comunity; to protect, preserve,
and maintain present and potential sources of water supply and watershed areas for the public health and safety; to protect, preserve and maintain the
existing and potential ground water supply and ground water recharge areas within the Town for the public health and safety; to preserve and protect the
lakes, ponds, streams, brooks, rills, marshes, swamps, bogs and other water bodies and water courses in the Town; to protect the community from the
detrimental use and development of land and water within the District; to conserve the natural resources of the Town; to preserve and maintain the ground
water table and water recharge areas within the Town: and to prevent blight and the pollution of the environment.
4.6.2. District Delineation
4.6.2.1. The general boundaries of the Water Supply Protection District are as follows:
a. All lands lying with the Town of Lunenburg in the primary and secondary recharge ares of ground water and watershed ares designated as Zones 2 and 3
on Map 1 of the Aquifer Land Acquisition Application, by Dufresne - Henry Inc., dated 1983.
b. The Restricted Area of the District is the area designated as Zone 1 on the Aquifer Land Acquisition Application Map of the Lunenburg Water District,
on file with the Planning Board and Town Clerk and the area within a circle with a radius of four hundred (400) feet from the potential well sites
identified in the report titles Test Well Locations selected from Seismic Profile Analysis, Lunenburg, Massachusetts, dated Fall 1985, on file with the
Planning Board and the Town Clerk.
4.6.2.2. Where the bounds as delineated are in doubt or in dispute, the burden of proof that the use is outside the Water Supply Protection District or
does not impact the District shall be upon the owner(s) of the land in question. However, the Building Official retains its authority to determine final
Iocation with regard to said Water Supply Protection District.
4.6.2.3. At the written request of the owner(s) of the property in question, the Town may engage a professional geologist, a certified soil scientist, or
a registered professional engineer trained in hydrogeology to determine the location and extent of a protection area on the parcel in question, and charge
the owner(s) of said parcel for the cost of the investigation.

Although I have served as an administrator in this school system for six years, I
will attempt to present the state of the schools as viewed through the eyes of a
new, unbiased Superintendent.
Although the Lunenburg Schools have weathered a stormy fiscal period during the
past seven years, I feel that our academic programs are in fine shape and that our
extra and co-curricular programs are at least functioning (thanks to the extensive
financial support of athletic booster clubs, Music Aiders, etc.).

COORDINATION
This year coordination emphasis is being placed in the areas of social studies
and testing, grades pre-K through 12. Committees have been established to examine
the scope and sequence of the social studies curriculum and the testing practices
within the system. The social studies committee will carefully review that which
is being taught in social studies at each grade level. They will be watching for
excessive duplication of content matter and/or gaps or voids in the total social
studies program. In conjunction with the staff and administration the social
studies committee will determine what should and will be taught at each level, what
materials will be utilized to address instruction and what accountability devices
will be put in place to monitor student mastery of social studies content.
The testing committee will review current testing practices. As a group and in
conjunction with other staff and administration this committee's task will be to
determine which testing instruments will be utilized at each level, what types of
testing reports will be generated for the total school system, how those reports
will be utilized, and when all testing within the school system will take place.

STRATEGIC PLANNING
A very important component of the building and maintenance of a successful
school system is planning. The Lunenburg School System is in the process of
developing a five year, long range plan. Utilizing the Strategic Planning method,
staff and administration, parents, and other community members will be forming task
forces and subgroups to help develop a long range plan for the schools. Once this
monumental task is completed, there will be ongoing; yearly planning team meetings
at which "the plan" will be reexamined and revised.
Involvement of parents and other community members (business persons, political
figures, the elderly, etc.) in this process will insure a broad based perspective
upon which "the plan" will be built.
REGIONALIZATION
Another significant happening in the Lunenburg Schools is the study of
regionalization. During this year the Regionalization Study Committee will be
investigating the pros and cons of regionalizing with our neighboring community,
Shirley. The study committee will report to the Town Meeting, their findings; and
will recommend to either form a Regionalization Planning Committee or to pursue
regionalization no further. In the event that the Town Meeting votes to establish
a planning committee, this committee would design an implementation plan for
regionalization. A ballot vote would then be required to actually regionalize with
Shirley.
Simultaneously Shirley will be involved in the same procedures. If Lunenburg
and Shirley do vote to become a region, students from Shirley who are currently
attending Ayer High School would attend Lunenburg High School instead.
The immediate positive effects of such a union on the Lunenburg Schools are
threefold:
o
Increased enrollment at a high school which is suffering from a depressed
enrollment.
0
Lunenburg High School's ability to maintain its status as a comprehensive
high school offering programs for students of all abilities and interest
levels.
0
Additional funding from the state to the region which would diminish the
burden of the cost of maintaining a comprehensive school system.
The community of Lunenburg has always had a great deal of pride in the quality
of its' schools; justly so! The community has struggled to support its schools
financially in order to maintain that quality. I am proud to be a part of the
Lunenburg School System. I pledge my efforts to the maintenance and expansion of a
quality school system which will provide equal educational opportunity for all
students in order that they may leave us and assume responsible, productive posi-
tions in society.

A new program in operation for the 1987-88 school year is a self-contained
Special Needs Language Class. This program utilizes the Psycholinguistic Approach
to Language (PAL) as the basic teaching tool. All academic subject areas are
taught using the PAL method. This year there are 6 children in the class, ages 6

to other programs around the state. Not only would this be very, very costly to
the town; it would also be very disruptive to the children in their community, with
their peers.

Middle School Principal's Report
The middle school opened its doors this year to an additional group of students
- the fifth grade. They join our sixth, seventh and eighth grade population. Our
total enrollment is now 442, an increase of approximately 95 students.
Extensive preparations were made to accommodate this increased population of
younger students. Traffic patterns, lunch and recess schedules, room assignments
and student activities were all altered and/or reconfigured. The result of this
planning has been the successful assimilation of fifth graders into the middle
school environment.
CURRICULUM
Our overall curriculum has remained primarily the same. Our students are
required to take English, reading, mathematics, social studies and science. Eighth
grade students also have the opportunity to choose a foreign language. French,
Latin, Spanish and conversational Spanish are all being offered this year.
All curricular areas utilize our computer lab for both enrichment and word
processing. We also have two regular education aides who assist students with
remediation and enrichment in math, general study skills and language achievement.
An adjunct to the curriculum, a recreational reading program, Books and Beyond, has
also been introduced to the student body this year.
SPECIAL AREAS
The middle school offers a variety of special area course selections for all
grade levels. Students, in grade five through eight, receive instruction in
physical education, art, general music and health. Students in grades five and six
are scheduled, on a weekly basis, into a library orientation and instruction
program. Grades seven and eight are exposed to Home Economics and Industrial
Arts. This is the first year in recent history that sixth grade students are not
enrolled in Home Economics and Industrial Arts. Budgetary considerations caused
the elimination of these offering at the sixth grade level.
Other areas affected by fiscal constraints, causing a major alteration in
program offerings, are instrumental and choral music. These courses are now
offered on an extended-day basis. The cooperation of our students and their
parents is essential to insure the success of these programs. These courses begin
after the close of the regular school day.
The middle school has a fine guidance program, utilizing one full time and one
part time counselor. These counselors work both individually and in groups with
students. They deal with academic and behavioral problems, career exploration and
environmental or peer pressure situations. Two guidance assemblies on drug and
alcohol abuse have been presented to all our eighth grade students.
ACTIVITIES
The Student Council, the middle school student governing body, spearheads most
of the student activities. In addition to dance, roller skating and ice cream
parties and the like, they organized a successful Christmas Fair. This fair's
purpose is humanitarian in scope. Through the profits of this year's fair, dona-
tions to the Lunenburg Community Council and Nature's Classroom Scholarships were
made.
Other activities, not directly under the auspices of the Student Council
include Nature's Classroom and the annual Magazine Drive. The magazine drive,
whose profits go directly to student life; through field trips and assembly
programs, was again a successful venture this year.

College acceptance for the Class of 1987 was also impressive. One hundred and

STUDENT ACTIVITIES/INVOLVEMENT
The student body of Lunenburg High School participated in numerous activities
during the 1986-87 school year. In January a semi-formal dance was held for the
first time in many years - The White Ball - as part of the Winter Carnival. There
was a Valentine's Dance, as well as, the annual L.H.S. all-school Talent Show. A
musical, "Grease", was performed in March under the direction of Mr. Don Adams. It
was a great success. Competitive class plays were held in May. The Senior Class
won for its play "Baby", directed by Mr. Peter Lincoln. The Senior Class
delightfully provided "Hide and Seek".
The Junior/Senior Prom was held in May at the Italian Cultural Club. The
annual Senior Class trip to Washington occurred in the Spring. The Foreign
Language Department sponsored a trip to Spain during the April vacation.
The Garrett Conrad Fair and Craft Show was held on March 7. The level of both
school, Lunenburg Education Association, and community involvement was tremendous.
It showed because the event was a huge success.
All in all, academic and school spirit are alive and well at L.H.S. And we're
looking for bigger and better things in the 1988-89 school year.
