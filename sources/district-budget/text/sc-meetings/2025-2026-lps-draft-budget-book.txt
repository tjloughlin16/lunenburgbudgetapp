 FY 27 Budget Strategy 
 To the Community of Lunenburg, 
 The Fiscal Year 2027 budget proposal represents a critical strategic pivot for the Lunenburg 
 Public Schools. As we present this ﬁnancial plan, it is imperative to establish the data-based 
 rationale for our request. While the total allocation is higher than the previous ﬁscal year, the 
 core theme of this proposal is clear:  Restoration,  Not Expansion. 
 The personnel requisitions and ﬁnancial drivers included in this document are identiﬁed strictly 
 as operational requirements rather than discretionary enhancements. This proposal serves as 
 the foundational year of a strategic three-year process aimed at systematically restoring critical 
 roles and functions that were eliminated during the FY26 budget cycle. 
 Our objective is to return the district to operational stability. By prioritizing these restorations, 
 we aim to stabilize class sizes, reinstate critical support services, and ensure our educational 
 environment meets the requisite standards for student success. We are moving beyond 
 "keeping the lights on" to ensuring that the education provided is robust, compliant, and 
 eﬀective. 
 Strategic Priority I: Academic Excellence & Active Citizenship  Our budget prioritizes 
 foundational skills—speciﬁcally literacy—because literacy is the prerequisite for active 
 citizenship and informed decision-making. 
 The Data Challenge:  Consistent with post-pandemic trends observed across the 
 Commonwealth, longitudinal data indicates that a signiﬁcant portion of our students are not 
 currently meeting expected grade-level standards in reading and writing. Without immediate 
 intervention, these students are prevented from accessing the full curriculum. 
 To reverse this trend, we are requesting the addition of  Academic Interventionists.  Educational 
 research proves that early proﬁciency creates momentum, while early gaps compound over 
 time. These roles will deliver high-frequency, small-group instruction to accelerate progress 
 before Grade 3 and backﬁll gaps in upper grades. This is the primary mechanism to secure 
 long-term academic success. 
===PAGE 2===
 Strategic Priority II: Operational Redesign & Eﬃciency  In reviewing our internal data, the 
 administration has made the strategic decision to reallocate resources away from indirect 
 support models and toward direct student services. 
 ●  Eliminating the Coaching Model:  We are eliminating  the Literacy Coach position and 
 converting the Math Coach role into an Instructional Specialist. This restructured role 
 will dedicate 60% of its time to direct student instruction. 
 ●  Strengthening the Business Oﬃce:  To address increased  regulatory complexity, we 
 propose establishing a dedicated Assistant Business Manager. This ensures stricter 
 ﬁnancial controls, risk management, and grant compliance, while transitioning data 
 reporting responsibilities from a 1.0 position to a 0.5 FTE Data Reporting Manager. 
 Strategic Priority III: Critical Restorations  The  following personnel requests are not 
 "nice-to-haves"; they are the necessary infrastructure for a functioning school district. 
 ●  LMHS Leadership:  With a Principal currently stretched  across two distinct school 
 cultures and over 250 athletes, our leadership capacity has reached its limit. Restoring 
 the Full-Time Athletic Director and Assistant Principal is essential to close supervision 
 gaps and ensure student safety. 
 ●  In-District Special Education (Cost Avoidance):  Restoring  support positions for students 
 requiring signiﬁcant social-emotional or transitional support is a ﬁnancial necessity.  If we 
 do not serve these students in their home community, we are legally required to fund 
 their education out-of-district.  Tuition and transportation  for placements far exceed the 
 cost of hiring internal staﬀ. 
 ●  World Language & Scheduling:  Restoring World Language  staﬃng is required to 
 maintain program oﬀerings. Failure to restore this position would jeopardize the Latin 
 program—displacing 85 students—or force a delay in World Language instruction until 
 9th grade. 
 ●  ELL Compliance:  Our current staﬃng is insuﬃcient  for a diverse population of 71 
 multilingual learners. With students spread across three campuses, our staﬀ is stretched 
 too thin to provide optimal support. An additional position is necessary to align with 
 DESE recommended service levels. 
 This budget outlook is built on realistic assumptions. Supply requests were generated via 3-year 
 projections, factoring in estimated shipping costs and anticipated market volatility to ensure 
 accurate budgeting. 
 This budget is a transparent, data-backed request to restore the essential personnel required to 
 bridge the achievement gap. By funding these positions, the town is not just hiring staﬀ; it is 
===PAGE 3===
 building a system capable of handling the reality of our students’ needs across the PK-12

 Early Childhood Literacy Data 
 Literacy is more than a school subject; it is the single most critical competency we teach. It is 
 the gateway to all other learning, and without it, students face compounded challenges that 
 follow them for the rest of their lives. 
 Right now, our district is facing a "quiet crisis" of capacity that requires immediate attention. 
 When we look at our youngest learners, the data tells a stark story: 
 ●  Kindergarten:  68 children entered our district already identifying as "at-risk" for literacy 
 acquisition. Of those, 44 require substantial, intensive support immediately. 
 ●  First Grade:  Without suﬃcient intervention capacity,  gaps persist. We currently have 50 
 students struggling, with nearly half requiring intensive intervention. 
 ●  Second Grade:  We have 45 students falling behind,  with 29 needing the highest tier of 
 support. 

===PAGE 4===
 ●  Turkey Hill (3rd Grade):  The trend compounds, with 48 third graders currently reading 
 below grade level. 
 The Cost of Waiting: The "Matthew Eﬀect"  In education,  we observe the "Matthew Eﬀect"—a 
 harsh reality where the literacy gap widens over time. Students who master reading early 
 accelerate, while those who fall behind early tend to stay behind, requiring increasingly 
 expensive interventions in later years. 
 The Solution: Closing the Capacity Gap  We are committed  to ensuring that a student's starting 
 line does not dictate their ﬁnish line. However, the sheer volume of students requiring 
 moderate-to-intensive support currently exceeds our staﬃng capacity  . 
 To catch these children before the gap becomes unbridgeable, we urgently need to add 
 Academic Interventionist support. This is not a "nice-to-have"—it is a necessity to provide the 
 targeted, high-frequency instruction these 200+ students deserve. We have the data, we know 
 the methodology, and we have the students. Now, we simply need the personnel to do the 
 work.

 Upper Elementary & Middle School: The Caseload Crisis 
 As we look at our student performance data for the upcoming ﬁscal year, the critical story is one 
 of unbalanced equations. Successful education depends on viable ratios—student-to-teacher 
 contacts and support availability. However, the data reveals that as students advance in grade 
 level, our current staﬃng model has not kept pace with the volume of need. 
 We are requesting targeted investments to ﬁx these ratios, allowing us to move from reactive 
 crisis management to proactive student growth. 
 Turkey Hill Elementary: Overwhelming Capacity  At the  elementary level, the data indicates a 
 caseload that physically exceeds our current human capacity. 

===PAGE 9===
 ●  The Literacy Reality:  In our current 4th Grade class, 73 students (54%) require 
 intervention in English Language Arts. Currently, we have the staﬃng equivalent of just 
 one position to manage this entire caseload. It is functionally impossible for one 
 educator to design and deliver 73 unique support plans eﬀectively. 
 ●  The Math Reality:  The challenge is greater in mathematics.  99 students (56%) in 4th 
 Grade are currently below grade-level standards. Yet, to support them, we rely on a 
 single staﬀ member who is already split between two roles (.5 interventionist/.5 special 
 educator). 
 ●  The 5th Grade Trend:  This pattern persists into 5th  grade, where 64% of students 
 require literacy support. While we celebrate recent gains in math proﬁciency, we risk 
 stalling that progress without dedicated support for the 53 students still needing help. 
 The Solution (Grades 3–5):  We are requesting an additional  Interventionist  . This position allows 
 us to triage the caseload: separating "intensive" remediation cases (teacher-led) from 
 "moderate" support cases (technology-supported). It transforms an unmanageable list of names 
 into a structured system of support. 
 The Middle School: The "Missing Link"  As students move to the Middle School, the focus shifts 
 to High School readiness. Currently, the Middle School operates with zero dedicated 
 interventionists, despite data showing a critical need to close gaps before 9th grade. 
 ●  Grade 6:  68 students need math intervention. Our classroom  teachers cannot provide 
 specialized small-group instruction for half the class while simultaneously delivering the 
 core curriculum. 
 ●  Grade 7 (The Gatekeeper):  81 students require reading  intervention. In Math, half the 
 class is navigating the transition to pre-algebra on unsteady footing. Since 7th-grade 
 math is the "gatekeeper" for High School STEM courses, leaving these gaps unaddressed 
 limits future career choices for our students. 
 ●  Grade 8 (The Launchpad):  Nearly half the class (49%)  is at risk of leaving middle school 
 with a "literacy debt," and 62 students are not fully meeting math expectations. 
 The Solution (Middle School):  To ensure our students  enter High School ready to succeed, we 
 are requesting: 
 1.  A Specialized Middle School Interventionist:  To bridge  the gap for the hundreds of 
 students currently identiﬁed in our data. 
 2.  Implementation of IXL Technology:  While the Interventionist  provides the human 
 instruction for the most critical needs, IXL acts as a "force multiplier." It uses real-time 
 algorithms to generate personalized practice plans that ﬁll speciﬁc skill holes—whether 
===PAGE 10===
 it’s fractions from 5th grade or pre-algebra from 7th—allowing us to support the wider 
 population eﬃciently. 
 The Bottom Line  Current staﬃng ratios limit our ability to coordinate eﬀectively with families 
 and ensure student growth. By funding these positions, the town is not just adding staﬀ; it is 
 building a system capable of handling the reality of our student numbers. We are ensuring that 
 whether a student is in 4th grade or 8th grade, they have a structured pathway to proﬁciency. 
 Executive Summary: A Blueprint for Restoration and Stability 
 The Fiscal Year 2027 Budget is not a standard operational request; it is a corrective action plan. 
 Over the previous pages, we have laid out a transparent, data-driven case that highlights a 
 singular reality:  The Lunenburg Public Schools are  facing a crisis of capacity, not capability. 
 Our educators are working harder than ever, but the sheer volume of student need—from the 
 68 Kindergarteners arriving at-risk to the 50%+ of Middle School students carrying a "literacy 
 debt"—has outpaced our current staﬃng model. This budget represents Year One of a 
 three-year strategic pivot designed to restore the necessary infrastructure to meet these needs. 
 This proposal addresses the district’s challenges through three targeted pillars of investment: 
 1. Closing the Achievement Gap (Personnel + Technology)  We have identiﬁed a clear "Matthew 
 Eﬀect" in our data: gaps left unaddressed in primary grades compound into expensive, complex 
 challenges in middle school. 
 ●  The Solution:  We are requesting a tiered intervention  model. By adding  Academic 
 Interventionists  at both the elementary and middle  levels, we can provide 
 high-frequency human instruction to the students with the most critical needs. 
 ●  The Force Multiplier:  Simultaneously, the implementation  of  IXL technology  in the 
 upper grades allows us to eﬃciently support students with moderate gaps, ensuring that 
 no child falls through the cracks due to caseload constraints. 
 2. Operational Integrity & Leadership  A district of our size and complexity cannot function 
 eﬀectively on a skeleton crew of administration. 
 ●  The Solution:  The restoration of the  Assistant Business  Manager  is a move toward ﬁscal 
 safety, tighter grant compliance, and risk management. 
 ●  The Safety Net:  The restoration of the  Athletic Director  and  Assistant Principal  roles at 
 LMHS is a direct response to the span-of-control issues that currently threaten student 
 safety and supervision. 
===PAGE 11===
 3. Fiscal Prudence & Cost Avoidance  This budget is built on the understanding that prevention 
 is more cost-eﬀective than remediation. 
 ●  The ROI:  Hiring internal staﬀ to support special education and high-needs students is 
 signiﬁcantly more ﬁscally responsible than paying for Out-of-District tuitions and 
 transportation. Every dollar spent on an interventionist today is a hedge against far 
 higher costs tomorrow. 
 The theme of this budget is  "Restoration, Not Expansion."  We are not asking for luxury items or 
 experimental programs. We are asking for the tools to do the job the community expects of us. 
 By approving this budget, the Town of Lunenburg is not just funding line items; it is making a 
 decision to secure the academic foundation of the next generation. We have the data to identify 
 the problems, we have the strategic plan to solve them, and with your support, we will have the 
 personnel and materials to execute that plan.

 Executive Summary: Federal Title I Grant Funding Analysis (FY24–FY26) 
 The funding landscape for the Federal Title I Grant over the three-year period from FY24 to FY26 exhibits 
 a pattern of  extreme volatility  , characterized by a signiﬁcant "boom and bust" cycle. The total allocation 
 for the three-year cycle is  $677,073  . 
 While FY25 saw a substantial infusion of capital (+22%), the subsequent year (FY26) experienced a sharp 
 correction that erased those gains entirely. This analysis breaks down the speciﬁc movements and 
 discusses the operational risks of this high-variance environment. 
 1. Year-over-Year Trend Analysis 
 The following breakdown illustrates the speciﬁc movements between ﬁscal years: 
 Period 
 Transition

 FY24 to FY25  $210,432  $257,119  +$46,687  +22.19% 
 FY25 to FY26  $257,119  $209,522  -$47,597  -18.51% 
 Key Observations: 
 ●  The FY25 Peak:  The grant experienced a massive surge in FY25, reaching a high of $257,119. This 
 represented a nearly $47k increase over the baseline. 
 ●  The FY26 Correction:  The funding for FY26 did not sustain the growth. In a sharp retraction, funding 
 dropped by over 18%, returning to $209,522. 
 ●  Baseline Erosion:  Crucially, the FY26 allocation is  $910 lower  than the starting FY24 allocation 
 ($209,522 vs. $210,432). We have eﬀectively ended the cycle with less nominal funding than we 
 started with. 
 2. Strategic Implications 
 The "Real Value" Decline 
 The situation here is more severe than the "ﬂat-funding" seen in other grants. Because the ﬁnal year 
===PAGE 25===
 (FY26) amount is lower than the starting year (FY24), the district faces a "double hit" on purchasing 
 power. 
 ●  Nominal Loss:  There is a literal reduction in dollars available (-0.4% from FY24 baseline). 
 ●  Inﬂationary Pressure:  When combined with two years of cumulative inﬂation (estimated 4-6%), the 
 real purchasing power  of the Title I grant in FY26 is signiﬁcantly lower than in FY24. 
 Operational Impact & Staﬃng Risks 
 The volatility between FY25 and FY26 poses speciﬁc dangers for personnel planning. 
 ●  Sustainability:  This pattern suggests that "spikes" in Title I funding should be treated as one-time 
 windfalls rather than reliable revenue for permanent Full-Time Equivalents (FTEs). 
 3. Conclusion & Recommendations 
 The Federal Title I Grant stream is currently in a  correction phase  . 
 ●  Budget Forecasting:  For FY27 planning, it is imperative to use the conservative baseline of 
 ~$210,000  . Do not average the three years, as the FY25 outlier skews the data upwards. 
 ●  Spending Strategy:  Avoid committing Title I funds to permanent salary increases or new permanent 
 positions based on temporary spikes. Windfall years (like FY25) are best suited for one-time 
 purchases (curriculum, technology, equipment) that do not create a recurring "ﬁscal cliﬀ." 
 ●  Gap Analysis:  With funding dropping below FY24 levels, an immediate review is needed to 
 determine if General Fund money must now subsidize programs previously fully covered by Title I.

 Executive Summary: Federal Title II Grant Analysis (FY24–FY26) 
 Grant Focus:  Professional Development (PD) & Eﬀective Instruction 
 Overview 
 The funding landscape for the Federal Title II Grant—which constitutes the majority of the district’s 
 Professional Development (PD) budget—exhibits a pattern of  volatility and net decline  over the 
 three-year period. The total allocation for the cycle is  $105,448  . 
 After a starting point of $36.5k in FY24, funding dipped sharply in FY25. While FY26 saw a partial 
 recovery, the grant failed to return to its initial baseline levels. This analysis details how this ﬂuctuation 
 impacts the district's ability to deliver high-quality training. 
 1. Year-over-Year Trend Analysis 
 The following breakdown illustrates the speciﬁc movements between ﬁscal years: 
 Period 
 Transition 
 Previous 
 Amount 
 New Amount  Net Change ($)  Net Change (%) 
 FY24 to FY25  $36,509  $33,663  -$2,846  -7.80% 
 FY25 to FY26  $33,663  $35,276  +$1,613  +4.79% 
 Key Observations 

===PAGE 27===
 ●  The FY25 Dip:  The district experienced a nearly 8% reduction in PD resources in FY25, losing $2,846 
 in funding year-over-year. 
 ●  Incomplete Recovery:  While FY26 saw growth (+4.79%), it was not enough to oﬀset the previous 
 year's loss. 
 ●  Overall Contraction:  The cycle ends with FY26 funding being  $1,233 lower  than the FY24 start point 
 ($35,276 vs. $36,509). 
 2. Strategic Implications 
 The "PD Purchasing Power" Decline 
 Since this grant is not used for personnel salaries, every dollar lost directly impacts the  quantity  of 
 training services available (consultants, conference fees, travel). 
 1.  Nominal Loss:  The direct reduction of ~$1,200 represents fewer workshop days or fewer staﬀ 
 members attending conferences. 
 2.  Cost Inﬂation:  The cost of Professional Development providers and travel has risen signiﬁcantly 
 since FY24. 
 3.  Result:  A reduced budget combined with higher service costs creates a "scissor eﬀect," signiﬁcantly 
 shrinking the actual volume of professional learning the district can aﬀord compared to three years 
 ago. 
 Operational Impact 
 ●  Reliance on Title II:  As this is the "largest share" of PD money, the General Fund lacks the capacity 
 to easily absorb these ﬂuctuations. 
 ●  Quality vs. Quantity:  The volatility forces a choice between oﬀering high-quality expensive training 
 to fewer staﬀ, or lower-quality widespread training. 
 3. Conclusion & Recommendations 
 The Federal Title II Grant stream is in a phase of  slow contraction  . 
 ●  Budget Forecasting:  Plan for FY27 using a conservative estimate of  ~$35,000  . Do not assume a 
 return to FY24 levels ($36.5k). 
 ●  Strategic Shift:  Move away from expensive "one-oﬀ" external consultants. Invest Title II funds into 
 "Train the Trainer"  models where select staﬀ are trained to bring expertise back to the district, 
 ensuring sustainability despite shrinking funds. 
 ●  Prioritization:  With lower purchasing power, PD requests must be strictly aligned with District 
 Improvement Plan goals; discretionary or "nice-to-have" conferences may need to be cut.

 2. Federal Restructuring Scenario: "Block Granting" 
 Funds may be moved to other agencies (e.g., dissolving the Department of Education and moving grants 
 to the Treasury or State block grants). This is a known proposal in current political discourse. 
 What is a "Block Grant"? 
 Currently, our grants are "Categorical." Title I  must  be used for poverty/intervention; Title II  must  be 
 used for PD. If the Department of Education is dissolved, these are often converted to "Block Grants" 
 given to the State. 
 The Realistic Forecast 
 If this restructuring occurs, the forecast changes from "Stable" to  "High Risk/Declining." 
 1.  Loss of "Maintenance of Eﬀort":  Categorical grants legally require the Federal government to 
 maintain funding levels. Block grants often do not have these protections, making them easier to 
 freeze or cut in future budget cycles. 
 2.  State-Level Dilution:  If the money goes to the State as a lump sum, the State Legislature may 
 rewrite the formula. We compete against other districts for a general pot of money rather than 
 receiving a guaranteed entitlement based on our Census poverty data. 
 Impact Assessment 
 What is the tangible impact of this shift on our district? 
 A. Personnel (High Impact) 
 Currently, ~$260,000 could support approximately  2.5 to 3.5 Full-Time Equivalents (FTEs) 
 (Interventionists, instructional coaches, or support staﬀ). 
 ●  The Risk:  If funding shifts to a Block Grant, the reliability of this revenue stream drops. 
 ●  Consequence:  We can no longer safely fund permanent positions with these dollars. 
 ●  Recommendation:  Begin moving essential intervention staﬀ to the General Fund (Chapter 70) 
 through attrition or budget transfers over the next 24 months. 
 B. Professional Development (Medium Impact) 
 Title II is our primary PD engine. 
 ●  The Risk:  In a block grant scenario, "general aid" is rarely prioritized for PD. States often prioritize 
===PAGE 35===
 "hard costs" (operations) over "soft costs" (training). 
 ●  Consequence:  The PD budget could evaporate entirely if not ring-fenced. 
 C. Compliance & Administration (Short-Term Chaos) 
 Dissolving the USDOE does not eliminate the bureaucracy; it shifts it. 
 ●  Consequence:  Expect 12-24 months of administrative confusion as the State (DESE) attempts to 
 interpret new guidance from the Treasury or DOJ. This leads to delayed approvals and cash ﬂow 
 interruptions. 
 4. Strategic Recommendation for FY27 
 "Insulate and Migrate" 
 1.  Forecast Assumption:  Assume  0% Growth  and a  5% Real Value Cut  for FY27. 
 2.  Personnel Migration:  Stop hiring  new  personnel on Title I/II/IV. Treat these grants as funding for 
 Supplies, Technology, and Stipends  —expenses that can be cut instantly if the grant 
 disappears—rather than Salaries, which involve people and contracts. 
 3.  Advocacy:  Monitor State-level discussions. If Federal funds move to the State, the battle for fair 
 distribution moves to Beacon Hill.
