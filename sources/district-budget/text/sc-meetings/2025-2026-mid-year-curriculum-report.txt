SUBJECT:  Literacy Instruction and Support Systems in Lunenburg Public Schools 
 
This memorandum provides a comprehensive explanation of the literacy instruction, assessment practices, and 
student support systems currently in place in Lunenburg Public Schools. It is intended to clearly articulate what 
we are doing, why we are doing it, and how research informs these decisions, particularly in response to family 
questions and concerns about literacy outcomes. 
It is also important to understand the context in which this work is occurring. Over multiple years, Lunenburg 
Public Schools has experienced repeated budget reductions, the loss of instructional and support positions, and a 
downward trend in grant funding that is projected to continue. These fiscal realities have required the district to 
make difficult decisions, prioritize core instructional systems, and maximize the impact of every resource 
available. The systems described in this memorandum reflect deliberate, research-informed choices made within 
these constraints, not an absence of effort or commitment. 
The district’s literacy framework is not based on trends, commercial preferences, or isolated initiatives. Instead, 
it reflects decades of peer-reviewed research, guidance from nationally recognized literacy scholars, and 
alignment with state and federal expectations for equitable access to high-quality instruction. Importantly, these 
systems are designed to work together—instruction, assessment, professional learning, and student supports are 
intentionally aligned to ensure strong daily teaching, early identification of need, and timely, appropriate 
responses for students who require additional support. 
This alignment is especially critical in a context of limited and shrinking resources. When staffing levels, 
funding streams, and external supports are reduced, coherence becomes essential. Fragmented initiatives are

Tier 1 Literacy Instruction: The Foundation of Student Success 
The cornerstone of Lunenburg’s literacy system is high-quality Tier 1 classroom instruction. A robust body of 
research demonstrates that the most effective way to improve literacy outcomes at scale is to strengthen daily 
core instruction so that it meets the needs of the majority of students (Foorman et al., 2016; Fletcher et al., 
2019). Schools cannot “intervene their way out” of weak core instruction; when Tier 1 instruction lacks clarity, 
explicitness, and coherence, intervention systems become overburdened and inequities widen (Vaughn et al., 
2008). 
In a district that has experienced position losses and reduced intervention capacity over time, the importance of 
strong Tier 1 instruction is even more pronounced. When fewer staff are available to provide supplemental 
services, the quality of daily classroom instruction becomes the single most powerful lever for student success. 
The National Reading Panel and subsequent large-scale studies consistently identify explicit instruction in 
foundational skills, paired with rich language, vocabulary, and knowledge-building, as essential for reading 
comprehension (National Reading Panel, 2000; Scarborough, 2001; Cervetti et al., 2016). Importantly, Tier 1 
instruction is where all students receive daily access to grade-level content, and it is the primary driver of 
equitable outcomes across classrooms and schools. 
Tier 1 instruction must also be developmentally responsive. What constitutes the most effective instructional 
focus evolves as students move from early reading development to advanced comprehension and disciplinary 
literacy. Below is a summary of the Tier 1 instructional emphasis supported by peer-reviewed research across 
Grades K–12. 
Tier 1 Focus by Grade Span 
Grades K–2: Building Accurate, Automatic Word Reading and Language Foundations 
In Grades K–2, research strongly supports a Tier 1 emphasis on explicit, systematic instruction in foundational 
skills, including phonological awareness, phonics/decoding, spelling/encoding, and early fluency (National 
Reading Panel, 2000; Foorman et al., 2016). These skills are highly predictive of later reading success and are 
most efficiently developed during the early elementary years. 
Tier 1 instruction at this level includes: 
 Carefully sequenced lessons that build skills cumulatively 
 Daily opportunities for guided and independent practice

 Immediate corrective feedback to prevent misconceptions 
 Integration of reading and writing to reinforce skill development 
 
At the same time, research is clear that comprehension does not wait. Oral language development, interactive 
read-alouds, explicit vocabulary instruction, and systematic knowledge-building are intentionally embedded 
from the earliest grades (Scarborough, 2001; Cervetti et al., 2016). Lunenburg’s K–2 Tier 1 instruction is 
therefore designed to prevent reading difficulty by addressing both the mechanics of reading and the language 
and knowledge base students need to understand text. 
These instructional priorities are especially critical given reductions in early intervention staffing over time. 
Preventing reading difficulty through strong Tier 1 instruction is both educationally sound and fiscally 
necessary. 
When additional instructional support is required beyond Tier 1, time is allocated strategically and based on 
student data. In Kindergarten, students may receive targeted literacy support in brief instructional blocks of 
approximately 15 minutes. In Grades 1 and 2, students may receive up to 30 minutes of additional literacy 
support, with frequency determined by assessment data. Some students receive support five days per week, 
while others receive fewer sessions depending on demonstrated need. These decisions are reviewed and 
adjusted regularly based on progress monitoring. 
Grades 3–5: Consolidating Fluency, Expanding Vocabulary and Knowledge, and Strengthening 
Comprehension 
In Grades 3–5, Tier 1 instruction shifts toward consolidating fluent, accurate reading while deepening 
comprehension through vocabulary, background knowledge, and exposure to increasingly complex texts. 
Fluency remains critical because it reduces cognitive load and allows students to focus on meaning-making 
(National Reading Panel, 2000). 
Research demonstrates that comprehension in this grade span is strongly influenced by: 
 Breadth and depth of vocabulary 
 Knowledge of the world and academic topics 
 Facility with sentence structure and text organization (Cervetti et al., 2016) 
Tier 1 instruction in Grades 3–5 therefore includes: 
 Explicit instruction in vocabulary and morphology 
 Purposeful use of coherent text sets to build knowledge 
 Structured opportunities for reading, writing, and discussion connected to content learning 
 Continued attention to accuracy and fluency for students who need reinforcement 
 
As grant-funded supports have declined, maintaining strong classroom-based literacy instruction in Grades 3–5 
has become increasingly important to ensure students continue to make progress without reliance on short-term 
or unstable external funding.

Grades 6–8: Reading Complex Texts, Disciplinary Literacy, and Evidence-Based Writing and Discussion 
In Grades 6–8, research emphasizes that students must regularly engage with complex, grade-level texts and 
learn how literacy functions differently across disciplines (Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008; Duke et al., 2021). 
Avoiding rigor or oversimplifying texts in response to struggle is associated with weaker long-term outcomes. 
Tier 1 instruction at this level includes: 
 Explicit routines for close reading and text analysis 
 Vocabulary and morphology instruction tied to academic language 
 Attention to sentence structure and cohesion 
 Strategic scaffolding that supports access without lowering expectations 
Peer-reviewed research also supports integrating evidence-based writing and structured academic discussion, 
requiring students to cite textual evidence and explain reasoning (Graham & Perin, 2007). Tier 1 instruction in 
Grades 6–8 prepares students for high school by strengthening comprehension, analytical thinking, and literacy 
practices across content areas. 
At the middle school level, literacy screening and instructional response continue through structured systems, 
including the use of oral reading measures that prioritize accuracy before rate. Students requiring additional 
support are scheduled into a literacy-focused FLEX block, with caseloads determined by assessment data. Some 
students also receive targeted support through digital literacy tools aligned to their primary areas of need. 
With fewer supplemental supports available at the middle school level due to staffing and funding constraints, 
consistent access to rigorous Tier 1 instruction is essential to prevent gaps from widening as academic demands 
increase. 
Grades 9–12: Advanced Comprehension, Argumentation, Academic Language, and Postsecondary 
Readiness 
In Grades 9–12, Tier 1 literacy instruction emphasizes advanced comprehension, argumentation, synthesis of 
multiple sources, and discipline-specific literacy practices. Research on disciplinary literacy shows that students 
require explicit instruction in how experts read, write, and reason within different fields (Shanahan & Shanahan, 
2008). 
Writing research further indicates that explicit instruction in planning, revising, and producing evidence-based 
writing improves both writing quality and reading comprehension (Graham & Perin, 2007). 
Tier 1 instruction at the secondary level therefore focuses on: 
 Text-based analysis across disciplines 
 Structured argument and synthesis 
 Academic discussion grounded in evidence 
 Writing tasks that mirror college and career expectations

At the elementary level (Grades K–5), Lunenburg uses HMH Into Reading as the core literacy program. This 
program aligns with research demonstrating that reading comprehension depends on both accurate word reading

and strong language comprehension (Scarborough, 2001). Into Reading provides a coherent structure that 
integrates foundational skills, vocabulary, knowledge-building, and comprehension within daily instruction. 
At the middle school level (Grades 6–8), Lunenburg uses Amplify ELA, which reflects research on adolescent

The use of different programs across grade spans reflects developmental research and ensures that instruction 
evolves as students move from learning to read to reading to learn. These programmatic decisions were made 
with sustainability in mind, recognizing the district’s limited ability to absorb frequent program changes due to 
budget constraints. 
Assessment, Benchmarking, and Progress Monitoring 
Strong instruction must be paired with intentional monitoring of student progress. In the elementary grades, 
Lunenburg conducts universal literacy benchmarking in Grades K–5 using mCLASS, a research-based 
assessment system used for screening, benchmark assessments, and progress monitoring. 
All families receive assessment results following benchmark periods. Every parent receives a DIBELS report 
explaining their child’s performance, and accompanying letters are sent home to provide context and next steps. 
When assessment results indicate characteristics associated with dyslexia risk, families receive additional 
information as required. 
Decades of research confirm that: 
 Early identification of reading difficulty is critical 
 Frequent progress monitoring supports instructional adjustment 
 Data-informed decisions reduce long-term reading failure (Fuchs & Fuchs, 2006; Gersten et al., 2009) 
In Lunenburg, assessment data is used as an instructional tool, not as a label. Educators analyze data 
collaboratively to adjust instruction, target support, and ensure students receive what they need in a timely 
manner. 
To ensure consistency and equity, assessment data is reviewed by the Teaching & Learning team, 
interventionists, literacy coaches, and the Director of Teaching & Learning. Using established cut scores, teams 
determine which students require additional support, the type of support needed, the frequency of instruction, 
and how students are scheduled. This coordinated planning ensures that instructional decisions are intentional, 
transparent, and responsive to student need. 
Additional diagnostic screeners, including untimed phonics measures, are used as needed to further understand 
student skill profiles and refine instructional planning. 
Partnership with the HILL for Literacy (One-Week Partnership Beginning February 2026) 
Beginning in February 2026, Lunenburg Primary School will engage in a one-week partnership with the HILL 
for Literacy to strengthen elementary classroom instruction. Through her participation in a summer conference 
focused on instructional leadership in literacy, Principal Squier identified and secured this targeted opportunity 
as part of a broader effort to enhance teaching and learning.

Research consistently demonstrates that job-embedded coaching—support that occurs within classrooms and is 
directly connected to daily instruction—is among the most effective forms of professional learning, even when 
delivered in short, intensive cycles (Joyce & Showers, 2002; Kraft et al., 2018). 
During this one-week partnership: 
 Literacy specialists will model evidence-based instructional practices in classrooms 
 Teachers will receive feedback grounded in real-time instruction 
 Instructional decision-making will be strengthened at the classroom level 
Critically, this partnership is intentionally designed as a short-term, high-impact experience focused on building 
internal capacity, ensuring that improvements are sustained after the week concludes. This capacity-building 
approach is especially important given projected declines in grant funding and the district’s need to rely 
increasingly on internal expertise to support high-quality literacy instruction. 
Professional Learning Communities (PLCs) 
At the elementary level, teachers participate in regular Professional Learning Communities (PLCs). These PLCs 
are structured to focus on instruction and student learning rather than compliance or administrative tasks. 
Research shows that PLCs are most effective when educators: 
 Analyze student work and data 
 Plan instruction collaboratively 
 Reflect on and refine teaching practices (DuFour et al., 2016) 
In Lunenburg, PLCs serve as the primary engine for continuous improvement. Literacy coaches support this 
work by preparing instructional materials aligned to student data and maintaining an inventory of resources to 
ensure instruction is responsive to identified needs. 
Student Support Team (SST) 
When students require support beyond core instruction, teachers may bring concerns to the Student Support 
Team (SST). The SST reviews instructional data, student strengths, and response to instruction to determine 
appropriate next steps. 
This process aligns with research cautioning against “wait-to-fail” models, which delay intervention until 
difficulties are severe (Torgesen, 2002). Lunenburg’s SST process emphasizes early, targeted, and instructional 
responses aligned to student needs, even as staffing limitations require careful prioritization. 
Students with Disabilities and Specially Designed Instruction 
Students with disabilities who have reading-related goals in an Individualized Education Program (IEP) receive 
specially designed instruction (SDI) in accordance with IDEA. SDI is aligned to a student’s present levels of 
performance and measurable annual goals.

Multilingual Learners 
Students identified as Multilingual Learners receive literacy instruction that integrates language development 
and reading instruction. Research indicates that outcomes are strongest when students receive explicit reading 
instruction while simultaneously developing academic language and maintaining access to grade-level content 
(August & Shanahan, 2006; Goldenberg, 2008). 
Lunenburg’s approach emphasizes: 
 Reading, writing, listening, and speaking together 
 Research-based materials and strategies 
 Coherent integration of language and literacy development 
Family and Community Partnerships 
Lunenburg Public Schools recognizes that literacy development extends beyond the classroom. The district 
partners with the public library to promote access to books and literacy experiences and has engaged the PTO in 
literacy-related initiatives, including family learning opportunities and book studies. Lunenburg Primary School 
also hosts a Literacy Night as part of Title I requirements, providing families with resources, information, and 
strategies to support reading at home. 
Additional efforts include the development of topic-based text sets aligned to instructional priorities, with 
support from community volunteers, including high school students earning community service hours. 
Increasing access to high-quality texts and placing more books in students’ hands remains a core priority. 
Summary and Ongoing Considerations 
Lunenburg Public Schools has developed a coherent, research-aligned literacy system that integrates instruction, 
assessment, professional learning, intervention, and specialized services. These components function as an 
interconnected system, reinforcing one another to support sustained literacy growth rather than isolated or short-
term initiatives. 
This work reflects significant professional expertise, careful planning, and a sustained commitment to students, 
carried out over multiple years despite ongoing fiscal constraints, repeated budget reductions, position losses, 
and declining grant funding. The district remains committed to transparency, continuous improvement, and 
partnership with families to ensure all students have access to high-quality literacy instruction and the 
opportunity to become proficient readers. 
At the same time, these systems are operating at maximum efficiency. With limited remaining flexibility, 
maintaining instructional quality and student supports depends on stability in staffing and funding. Additional 
reductions would compromise the coherence and effectiveness of the literacy system and place current and 
future gains at significant risk.
