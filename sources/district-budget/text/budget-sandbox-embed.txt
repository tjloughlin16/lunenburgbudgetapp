 Key Observations & Trends
 • Significant Increases: Across almost all grades (1 through 5), class sizes are projected to increase notably from the 2025-2026 school year to the 2026-2027 school year. 
 • Largest Class Sizes: Grade 2 is projected to see the most dramatic jump, moving from 22-23 students per class up to 30 students per class in 26-27. 
 • Consistent Mid-to-Upper 20s: For the 2026-2027 school year, grades 1, 3, 4, and 5 are all hovering right around 28 students per classroom. 
 • Teacher Allocation: There are 26 total classroom teachers allocated across K-5 for the 2026-2027 school year. Kindergarten and 5th Grade are receiving the highest allocation with 5 teachers each, which helps keep the 5th Grade numbers slightly lower than they might have been otherwise. 
 Frequently Asked Questions
 Addressing community questions regarding the budget scenarios and FY25 surplus.
 Why was there a large budget surplus at the end of Fiscal Year 2025, and what is being done to prevent this in the future?
 At the end of FY25, the District experienced a larger-than-normal budget surplus (more than double our historical average of $200,000). We want to assure the community that these funds were not lost or misplaced; rather, the surplus was primarily caused by two factors:
 Facilities & Maintenance Vacancies: Significant turnover and unfilled positions in our facilities department resulted in unspent salaries and stalled maintenance projects.
 Unadjusted Grant Funding: Several paraprofessional salaries were ultimately covered by newly identified grants. However, the previous administration did not adjust the general operating budget to reflect these savings.
 It is important to note that these unspent funds become part of the town's certified "free cash."
 We understand the community’s expectations for transparency. Under the new leadership of Superintendent Dr. Jodi Fortuna and Finance Director Christopher McNamara, we have implemented strict accountability measures. These include a planned restructuring of the Finance Department, conducting monthly public reviews of expenditure reports, and establishing a Special Education Recovery Fund to appropriately manage any future surpluses.
 Financial Overview
 Hasn't student enrollment decreased? If so, why has the budget increased? 
 Student enrollment has remained steady. What we have seen since FY21 is a significant increase in the number of students with complex needs identified as high needs by the state.
 The state definition of a high needs student is an unduplicated count of students belonging to one or more of the following subgroups: low income (economically disadvantaged), students with disabilities, or English Learners (EL) / former EL students. This accountability designation, used by the Massachusetts Department of Elementary and Secondary Education, is an umbrella term for identifying students who may need additional supports to succeed.
 In order to provide a Free and Appropriate Public Education (FAPE) we need additional staff to provide the additional supports these students may need. This has become a budget driver for us. In FY26 we saw an increase of 32 students who needed this support. Our indications lead us to believe we will see another increase in FY27.
 Is the Assistant Business Manager an additional central office employee? 
 No, this role does not increase the overall central office headcount. Instead, an existing position is being reclassified to Assistant Business Manager, and the previous role will be eliminated to ensure greater operational efficiency without adding a net-new employee to the budget.
 Staffing & Classroom Impact
 Why are we reducing four elementary teaching positions but keeping the assistant principal? 
 Arriving at a balanced budget for the upcoming school year was an arduous and painful process. We exhausted all other avenues for operational reductions before making the difficult decision to reduce our elementary teaching staff by four full-time equivalent (FTE) positions.
 We understand the community's concerns regarding the assistant principal role and want to provide transparency on why this position is being retained:
 Financial Reality: From a financial standpoint, the salary of the assistant principal is roughly equivalent to the salary of a single teacher. Eliminating the assistant principal role would not generate the necessary savings to preserve the four teaching positions we are losing.
 Critical School-Wide Support: As we navigate the impact of fewer teachers and the reality of larger class sizes, our remaining educators will need more support, not less. While sacrificing the assistant principal role might save a teaching position for one specific classroom, retaining the assistant principal ensures that every classroom, teacher, and student in the building continues to receive essential, building-wide support for student behavior, instructional coaching, and daily operations during a challenging transition.
 How will the proposed reduction of four elementary school teachers impact students? 
 The reduction of these teaching positions will directly result in larger class sizes, with an estimated 28 to 31 students per classroom. Furthermore, the accompanying reduction in interventionist positions means there will be less support staff available to provide targeted, small-group instruction and assist classroom teachers.
 What is the final decision on the Assistant Principal position, given the previous discussions about sharing the role between buildings? 
 We understand that the discussions regarding the Assistant Principal position have been confusing at times. At one point, we did recommend sharing a single Assistant Principal across two buildings to save money. However, after thorough consultation with our leadership team, we realized that managing two buildings with one Assistant Principal would be extremely difficult, particularly if we are faced with overfilled classrooms. Because of this, an Assistant Principal is now fully funded and included across all levels of the budget (restoration, balanced, and core).
 Why does the new Primary Special Education Teacher only appear as a change in the balanced budget? 
 The new Primary Special Education Teacher is a required addition due to a high number of incoming K-2 students with Individualized Education Programs (IEPs). In both the restoration and balanced budget scenarios, this much-needed position is fully funded and added to our staffing plan.
 The core budget forces a structural swap rather than a net-new addition. Instead of hiring a new staff member, we would have to convert an existing Interventionist position into this Special Education role.
 We didn't list this simply as a "cut" in the core budget because the Special Education role is a brand-new requirement. However, achieving it under the core budget relies on sacrificing an Interventionist to fulfill that legal need, which is why the position only shows up as a true addition or "change" in the balanced budget.
 Programs & Extracurriculars
 Where does the 5th grade band stand in the current budget proposals? 
 We know the 5th grade band has been an important topic of discussion for our community throughout the budget process. Currently, the 5th grade band program is included in both the restoration and core budgets. However, it is not included in the balanced budget scenario.
 What is the status of middle school sports in the proposed budgets? 
 We recognize that middle school sports are highly valued and have been a major point of discussion recently. Currently, funding for middle school sports is included in both the restoration and core budgets. However, due to funding constraints, middle school sports are not included in the balanced budget scenario.
 Is it true that there will be changes to the 8th-grade World Language program at LMHS if the budget override does not pass? 
 Yes. If the budget override does not pass and we are unable to fund an additional World Language teacher, we will need to make significant structural adjustments to the middle school language program to manage the staffing shortfall. The anticipated changes include:
 Phasing out Latin: We will restrict the Latin option for 8th graders as we begin the formal process of phasing the language out of our curriculum. Our current Latin teacher will be reallocated to teach some Spanish sections.
 Increased Class Sizes: Even with reallocating staff, we will have to combine sections and levels more frequently, which will push World Language class sizes up to 31 or 32 students per section.
 Enrollment Caps for Spanish: Depending on capacity, we may be forced to limit the total number of 8th-grade students who are permitted to take Spanish.
 Schedule Adjustments: To accommodate these constraints, 8th graders may need to adopt the high school schedule for one additional period during the school day.
 Currently, the middle and high school leadership teams are working collaboratively to navigate these logistical challenges. These reductions are contingent upon our final budget reality, but we must be prepared. We will provide a comprehensive update to the community once a final plan is established.
 Reading Interventionists
 What exactly does the district mean by an "Interventionist"? 
 When Lunenburg Public Schools talks about an interventionist, they are specifically talking about a Reading Interventionist . These are highly specialized educators trained to help students who are struggling to read. They step in to provide targeted, expert help so that every child in our district has the opportunity to become a confident, capable reader.
 How is a Reading Interventionist different from a regular classroom teacher? 
 Our classroom teachers do an incredible job teaching a broad range of subjects to a classroom full of diverse learners. However, a Reading Interventionist has a very different job. They are highly specialized experts in the "science of reading." They use precise assessments to figure out exactly why a child is struggling and then apply specific teaching methods to fix those exact gaps.
 Does a Reading Interventionist just focus on sounding out words? 
 No, their impact goes far beyond the mechanics of reading. Struggling to read often causes children to experience deep anxiety, frustration, and a loss of confidence. If a student believes they are just "bad at reading," they often disengage or act out. Reading Interventionists are trained to encourage these students, ease their frustration, and rebuild their confidence so they believe in themselves again.
 Why is catching reading issues early so critical? 
 Third grade is a massive turning point in a child's life. From kindergarten to third grade, students are "learning to read." But starting in fourth grade, the shift flips: students must now "read to learn." If a student cannot read well by the end of third grade, up to half of their fourth-grade science, history, and math curriculums will be completely incomprehensible to them. Children who aren't reading proficiently by the end of fourth grade are four times more likely to drop out of high school.
 What does a Reading Interventionist do in Elementary School vs. Middle/High School? 
 In Early Childhood & Elementary School: The goal is prevention. Interventionists focus heavily on the building blocks of literacy—learning the alphabet, sounding out words, and reading smoothly out loud. They want to make these skills automatic before reading gets too complicated.
 In Middle & High School: The job gets much more complex. Middle and High schoolers are dealing with tough subjects like biology and history. The interventionist has to do two things at once: help the student catch up on the basic reading skills they missed, while also teaching them how to understand complex, high-school-level textbooks.
 Can't we just use computer programs or reading apps to help struggling students? 
 While technology is helpful, it cannot replace a human interventionist. A computer cannot read the micro-expressions of a frustrated child, adjust a lesson on the fly to make it easier, or provide the complex emotional encouragement a struggling student desperately needs to keep trying.
 Does investing in Reading Interventionists make financial sense for Lunenburg taxpayers? 
 Yes. When schools do not have Reading Interventionists, they are forced to use a "wait to fail" approach. Students fall further and further behind until they require an Individualized Education Program (IEP) and enter Special Education.
 Special Education is legally mandated and incredibly important, but it is also exponentially more expensive for the district. It often requires highly restrictive environments, one-on-one aides, and sometimes even out-of-district placements. By investing in Reading Interventionists early, we fix reading problems before they turn into the need for costly Special Education services.
 What is the long-term benefit for our community? 
 Teaching a child to read alters the trajectory of their entire life. Students who read at grade level by third grade have much higher high school graduation rates and go on to college or post-secondary education at significantly higher numbers. The early grade reading interventionist is our town's primary safeguard against the massive societal costs of school dropouts, ensuring our children grow up to be successful, economically stable adults.
