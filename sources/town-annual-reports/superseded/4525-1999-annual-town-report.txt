BOARD OF SELECTMEN/SEWER COMMISSIONERS
1999 began with a number of changes within this office, of note is the change in office
hours. The new hours are 8:00 A.M. to 4:00 P.M. Monday through Friday. With the
recent passage of the Home Rule Charter, 1999 has brought about some major changes in
the Town's form of government. Adoption of the new charter resulted in an increased
membership in the Board of Selectmen, the Board of Health and the creation of a new
position, Chief Administrative Financial Officer (CAFO). Richard R. Runtagh and Ronald
Lamothe were elected to the two newly created positions on the Board of Selectmen at
the special election held in September.
The Board of Selectmen acting in their capacity as Sewer Commissioners negotiated an
Intermunicipal Agreement (IMA) with the City of Leominster which was paramount in
meeting the Town's future sewering needs. The Annual Town Meeting Articles for the
construction phase brought out an overwhelming show of residents in favor of Phase 1 of
the Sewer Feasibility Plan. Members of this board canvassed the Whalom area in an effort
to secure a State grant from the Department of Housing Community Development (DHCD)
which the Town will be reapplying for in the Spring of 2000. It is anticipated that the
construction phase will commence in 2000.
This year the Gazebo and brick walkway on the Lower Common were completed by the
Gazebo Committee and the Board extends a "thank you" to them for their hard work.
Band concerts during the summer turned out to be a hit and the band members now have
ample room. The Board would also like to recognize the generosity of Mr. Raymond
Powell for his donation of the water fountain, sprinkler system, water bubbler, and the re-
seeding/re-loaming of the Lower Common. Many compliments have been received by this
office on this generous gift to the Town.
In conclusion, this Board would like to thank the Lunenburg/Townsend Rotary Club for
Christmas decorations in the Gazebo, the Lunenburg Grange for the Town Hall summer
window box and the Turkey Hill Garden Club for the Town Hall Christmas decorations.
The Selectmen would also like to acknowledge all of the individuals who work so hard for
the Town and those who volunteer their time and commitment by serving on the various
Boards, Commissions and Committees within the Town.

BUILDING DEPARTMENT/ZONING-OFFICIAL
The Building Inspectors office is located on the second floor of the Town Hall with regular
hours Monday through Thursday 8:00 AM until 3:30 PM and Fridays by appointment.
Roger Williams is the Building Inspector and Zoning Official for Lunenburg and is
available daily for calls and questions. Lisa Normandin is the Building Department
Secretary. Inspections are by appointment.
Building activity in Lunenburg has rapidly increased in 1999. New home construction
has risen especially in residential districts. A total of 363 permits were issued in 1999.
Fifty-eight single-family homes were built; reflecting an increase of 12 new homes since
the prior year. A new shopping center is underway on Massachusetts Avenue called
Crossroads Plaza. This new plaza (formerly The Coach House Inn) will feature a

and corporations who bid or perform residential contracting (reconstruction, alterations,

CAPITAL PLANNING
The Capital Planning Committee has been working since 1992 when Town Meeting
mandated the newly formed Committee to present Capital Plans to the Town of
Lunenburg at the annual Town Meeting. The Capital Plan for a Community is twofold.
The Capital Budget which this Committee presents as the FY plans and the Capital
Program which is the long-range plan that includes facilities. Facility Planning is also
mandated as an element under Master Planning, which is called "Community Facilities
and Service Element". The Charter has further mandated that Capital Planning be
extended into a five- year plan so the Community can see what is ahead.
This Committee has used an analogy called the Big Man - Little Man, which has been
before you for several years. To date, a percentile allocation has not been accomplished,
that is a specific amount of money set aside for Capital Expenditures, Improvements and
major Maintenance, nor has the percentile for long range (Facility) planning been
accomplished. It has been a hit and miss approach to the Town available revenues. These
revenues are allocated first to the necessary operating budgets. The CPC realizes the
importance of proper funding of operating budgets however, the longer the Town puts off
long range capital spending the least cost effective the construction of these needed
facilities will be. Different financial committees have sought the help of the Assessor's
office to take a look at the cost factors concerning major facility construction projects
and the actual effect this construction will have on the tax rate. This information is
available to the public. It needs to be digested with the thought that if the Town
continues to ignore the Capital Program as part of Capital Planning, this Community will
be in a major crisis.
CPC is looking forward to the arrival of the new CAFO, Mr. William Gustus, as our Chief
Financial Officer, who we are sure, will bring insight to our Capital Program problems.
To assure the public that we have made progress with our Capital Budget portion of
Capital Planning we note the following:
Capital spending in FY98 was $389,585.00, FY97, $509,450.00, FY96, $287,140.00 and
FY95 $633,200.00. These monies were spent on needed vehicles, equipment, building
renovations; computer needs and open space land purchases and etc.
The recap of last years FY plan is funding of a Library grant match/capital expenditure
study, diesel powered lawnmower, Wallis Park (fencing, repaving, striping), Police Dept.
cruiser, Fire Dept. pickup truck, all departments, computers and printer needs, Town Hall
boiler, and exterior improvements, renovation of Town Beach building, (year 1 of 3 year
project), addition of parking area and driveway (Turkey Hill Middle School), sidewalk
replacement (Elementary School to Mass Avenue), replacement windows- (Elementary
8
===PAGE 12===
School), replacement roof (Passio School), signage, benches and picnic tables for
conservation land, 4 foot aerator and topdresser.
This year's plan, as noted in its entirety in the Finance Committee's report, includes
items such as vehicles (Police and Highway), small multi-department equipment, Fire
Department radios, all departments computer equipment, renovations to Town and
School buildings, including needed asbestos removal at the schools.
It is important to note examples of where the Committee has been successful and where
we have to be continually aware of the needs. The Vehicle Rotation plan has ensured that
our police cruisers are replaced every three years. The Town has purchased needed
highway vehicles with a built-in pass down rotation to departments with lesser needs and
other vehicles necessary for fire protection and public safety. However, the above hasn't
done it all. Highway vehicles for example, still need updating as the department is still
running several pieces of old and worn equipment.
Capital Planning has been successful in outfitting our government offices with technology
needs. The Community must stay aware that the pencil technique is gone and
technology cost will be with us on a yearly basis. The technology changes rapidly and a
computer life is only three years. We have not been successful to initiate a Geographic
Information System program for land use efficiency.
The Town has been unsuccessful in moving forward on fulfilling facility needs. It is the
opinion of this Committee that we have to move forward with well-developed studies and
plans. We must also keep "economy of scale" in the foreground of our community
thinking while we are satisfying these important needs. We need to continue to plan and
implement plans for our Capital Program. Capital Planning is good planning.

CENTRAL MASS MOSQUITO CONTROL
Telephone (508) 393-3055 and Fax (508) 393-8492
The Central Massachusetts Mosquito Control Project (CMMCP) currently provides its
services to 29 cities and towns throughout Middlesex and Worcester Counties. The
Project's headquarters is located at 111 Otis Street, Northboro MA. Tours of the
headquarters or visits to field work sites may be arranged by calling the office in advance.
The CMMCP practices Integrated Mosquito Management (IMM), blending state of the art
methods and techniques with expertise, experience, and scientific research to provide
our member communities with modern, environmentally sound, cost effective mosquito
control.
During 1999 the Project conducted four hundred and twenty six (426) adult mosquito
spraying operations in residential and recreational areas, and three hundred fifty-four
(354) larviciding operations. Nine thousand, nine hundred and eighty-one (9,981) catch
basins were treated with larvicidal briquettes to control the mosquitoes that seek out
these cool dark wet areas to breed. One thousand six hundred thirty-five (1,635) culverts
were cleaned in an attempt to eliminate unnecessary standing water. This work was
done in conjunction with cleaning, clearing and digging of one hundred and six thousand
two hundred ninety-eight (106,298) feet of streams, brooks and ditches. This represents
over twenty miles of waterways which were cleaned and improved.
9
===PAGE 13===
Five ponds were cleaned and redug during 1999 to make them once again suitable for
native fish, frogs and other wetlands wildlife and vegetation. Our pond cleaning projects
have been extremely successful at reducing the mosquito breeding in these areas, while
at the same time restoring the pond's value for fire fighting, agriculture, recreation
and/or aesthetics.
The Mosquito Awareness Program which we offer to elementary schools in our district has
become very popular. Project staff meet with students and teachers to discuss mosquito
biology, mosquito habitat, and control procedures. Much of the presentation is directed
towards what the children and their families can do to prevent mosquitoes from breeding
around their homes. Live samples of mosquito larvae are included with the presentation,
and are left in the classrooms so that students can watch them develop. Slides, videos,
handouts and coloring books help to make this an interesting program.
As part of our effort to reduce the need for pesticides we continue to expand our water
management program. By cleaning clogged and overgrown waterways, mosquito breeding
can be reduced, wetlands are restored and water quality is improved.
Bti mosquito larvicide is used to treat areas where mosquito larvae are found. We
routinely check known breeding sites, but also encourage the public to notify us of any
areas they suspect could breed mosquitoes. Our field crews will investigate all such sites
and treat if needed.
Our goal is to handle all mosquito problems with water management or larviciding but we
recognize that there are times when adult mosquito spraying is the only viable solution.
Insuch cases residential and recreational areas are treated with either hand held or pick-
up truck mounted sprayers.
The Project's surveillance program monitors adult mosquito and larval population density
and is the backbone for prescribing various control techniques. Rain gauges are set out
and data collected by our surveillance crews in an effort to predict when mosquito
breeding will occur.
The Project's video "Working for You" is available to anyone interested in learning about
mosquito control and the services provided by the Central Massachusetts Mosquito
Control Project.
We would like to thank you for the support you have rendered during 1999 and we look
forward to helping you and your community with its mosquito problems in the future.
CONSERVATION COMMISSION
The Lunenburg Conservation Commission has seven members appointed by the Town
Selectmen. The Commissioners are charged with administering the Massachusetts
Wetlands Protection Act, the Massachusetts River's Protection Act, Stormwater

HISTORICAL COMMISSION
The Lunenburg Historical Commission had a productive year. Goals were set for short and
long term projects together with time lines to accomplish each.
Research on the Internet, other Historic Commissions, and the Secretary of State's
office, enabled us to choose Microsoft's Professional version of Excel '97 as our database
for recording all local records located in the Town Hall vault. Forms were developed
covering all the pertinent categories and inserts on acid-free paper were made, enabling
us to recognize which items had been catalogued. Access to this database will be available
to the public on computer as well as on printouts. Some commission members
volunteered their services on Wednesday afternoons during the summer to continue the
task. Summer work began on June 16th. Since some items take most of the afternoon to
complete, it will be an ongoing project. Town Clerk, Kathy Herrick, has been most helpful
in giving us access to these records. We have limited archiving materials and will use
these to properly store the records when the project is complete. However, our
commission is running into the same problem others in town are facing - space.
The Historical Commission has subscribed to the free listserv mahistory@cs.umb.edu
which serves as a forum for sharing information and ideas about the acquisition,
preservation, and use of Massachusetts historical records. Membership includes
individuals and organizations who create, use, hold, or are concerned about
Massachusetts historical records. This is a mailing list service administered through the
Office of the Secretary of the Commonwealth. The list is generously hosted by the
University of Massachusetts, Boston Computer Science Department. Appropriate areas for
postings, generally speaking, are archives, record management, education, preservation
and conservation, reference and use, and related areas. Individual home computers of our
members are used to access this service.
An inventory was completed listing all the books on microfilm and microfiche and is
available to the public. Microfiche #21 was missing but we contacted Holbrook Research
Institute and it has been replaced. Additionally, a listing of all Lunenburg Historical
Assets listed with the State and Federal government, and the oldest houses outside the
Historic District the commission hopes to document, has been compiled and copies given
to the Selectmen, Planning Board, Conservation Commission, Building Inspector, Board
of Appeals and Town Clerk. This listing is also available to the public through any of these
departments as well as through the Historical Commission.
Members of the Commission attended a Forum at the Centrum in Worcester, a
Conference at Clark University and a workshop in Gardner, all of which were sponsored by
the Mass Historical Commission.
William Bingham has researched and compiled an accurate map of the Historical District
for our commission using the metes and bounds listed with the National Register. There
has always been some confusion as to the exact dimensions of the District in as much as
the original submitted was hand drawn. This map will be available to the public as soon as
it is framed. Our very special thanks to Mr. Bingham.
Cemetery Superintendent, Greg Bingham, tried to apply for a grant to repair and preserve
some of the older headstones in our cemeteries. To be eligible, the cemeteries need to be
listed with the National Register. Several years ago forms for both cemeteries were
submitted to the State but were not accepted. The present commission is doing
additional research and when completed, will apply once again.

FIRE DEPARTMENT
The Lunenburg Fire Department responded to seventy more calls in 1999 than it did in
1998. This trend of more and more calls is proportionate to the increase in building of
both commercial and residential structures. Both building surges bring more growth to
our community, which in turn places more and more demands on our services. It is of
the utmost importance that we recognize these demands for service so that our
manpower and facilities will keep up with them. We are currently working with the
Police, Selectmen, Capital Committee and appointed citizens to study the building of a

both our public safety operations are to function reliably in the future we need adequate
modernized buildings located in an accessible area. Within the next two years we hope to
be prepared to present a plan to answer our building needs for the immediate future.
Space needs, access to our building and parking during emergency operations, continue
to be the major problems that we face today.
Our firefighters and EMT's continue to provide you with the very best of service, even
under these cramped and crowded conditions. I give them credit for their efforts to make
the most of this building when it lacks so many of the necessary items. Hopefully, very
soon, we can work together to provide a station that will answer the needs of a modern
public safety operation.

TURKEY HILL MIDDLE SCHOOL
Keith Hochstein, Principal
Turkey Hill Middle School started the 1999-2000 school year with a change in administration. Mr.
Keith Hochstein was appointed as principal in August, and Mr. Philip Crouse was appointed as acting
assistant principal. A search committee was created to find a permanent assistant principal. Even
though a number of good candidates were interviewed, the committee felt that no one candidate
presented the right fit for Turkey Hill. Based on these circumstances, Mr. Crouse agreed to stay on
until Thanksgiving. During that time, he did an outstanding job, and everyone at Turkey Hill Middle
School is grateful to Phil for his willingness to help the school fill that important void. Mr. Harry Karis
assumed the role of assistant principal after Thanksgiving. Mr. Karis, a long time teacher at Turkey
Hill, assumed the job with a thorough knowledge of the students and the school. He did an excellent
job filling this position. He and Mr. Hochstein formed a cohesive team, and the building ran smoothly
especially considering there had been a major change in administration.
A plan was immediately created in the late summer in order to address a variety of needed
improvements. The theme of the year developed into an attitude of making a good school better. This
change took many forms. All faculty were divided into working committees who took on a number of
tasks. The Safety and Discipline committee wrote a code of conduct that created a consistent and fair
set of rules which would apply to all students for all situations. This code will be published in both the
parent and student handbooks for next year. The Academic Standards Committee re-wrote the criteria
for the Honor Roll. This new framework reflected the higher standards that Turkey Hill Middle School is
seeking to adopt. This group also began work on a new report card system reflecting the MCAS marking
system. The Student Life Committee examined ways to improve the activities for students at the
school.
Our Professional Development Committee worked closely with the administration to plan the
professional improvements for the faculty. In the first half of the school year, these efforts focused
almost entirely on the School Improvement Plan. The School Improvement Plan called for the school
to emphasize improvement in MCAS. The school launched a comprehensive effort in this area.
Teachers took the MCAS to understand what it was like for a student who has to take this difficult test.
They wrote rubrics in their teaching areas and researched and discussed "anchor papers". Each teacher
was responsible for assigning open response questions to all students. By the year's end each student
had numerous practice opportunities with this part of the test. Teachers attended workshops,
purchased MCAS testing material and reviewed the "Item Analysis" to determine if adjustments had to
be made to our curriculum offerings. They made formal recommendations regarding changes in
curriculum, teaching strategies, improvements in students' abilities to take the test and long range
recommendations. These recommendations and changes were presented to the School Committee and
implementation will begin in September of 2000.
The Futures Committee looked at ways to prepare the school for the 2000-2001 school year. Next year
the school will be totally organized as a middle school. Every student will be on a teaching team;
teachers will have common planning time to organize instruction, to effectively use the flexible
schedule and to meet with parents. The entire school from grades 5-8 will be organized to meet the
emotional, physical and intellectual needs of this age level student. Planning for this took place this
spring. Teachers attended workshops, made visitations to other middle schools and discussed
philosophies and programs with guest speakers.
The Gifted and Talented Committee met frequently in the fall to address meeting the needs of high
achieving students. The committee presented a plan to the School Committee, and a plan for the
2000-2001 school year was adopted. This year a number of new programs have been implemented. For
the first time any student in the school can take part in national academic contests in math, science,
social studies and language arts. These are challenging and fun activities. A math web site activity for
38
===PAGE 42===
our fifth graders was initiated. Our robotics team participated in a statewide competition and finished
second in the state. These are just a few of the opportunities offered to our high achieving students.
We look forward to expanding these activities next year.
The School Advisory Council met frequently and discussed a wide range of topics. Council members
Kris Devaney, Diane Jo, Sandra Sheehy, Mary Ann Weisman, Mariam Cronin, Jennifer Hood-Biker,
Sharon Kimball and Keith Hochstein engaged in productive and reflective meetings. These harmonious
sessions resulted in a productive year for the council. Dialogue and communication between parents
and school were increased. The council regularly monitored the school's efforts to fulfill the School
Improvement Plan for this year. Members of the council were pleased that the plan had been properly

reorganization of the school as a complete middle school was created. The council looks forward to
continued cooperative meetings.
Lastly, parent involvement in the school significantly improved. A volunteer list was generated by

OFFICE OF INSTRUCTION
Faculty, administrators, and staff in the Lunenburg Public Schools worked diligently this past year to
embody the mission of our school. In the Office of Instruction our efforts continued to be focused in
three areas: (a) curriculum development (standards), (b) assessment and evaluation (measures), and (c)
providing ALL students meaningful access to learning outcomes. Following is a summary of activities
and results in each of these areas.
Standards
As of this school year, we now have aligned our local curricula with the Massachusetts State
Curriculum Frameworks in Mathematics, Science & Technology, English/Language Arts, Integrated
Health, Foreign Language, Business and History/Social Science. Curricula task forces comprised of
educators from all levels produced the curricula documents with input from the faculty and
community. Revised curricula in three areas were brought on line this school year: History/Social
Science, Business, and Foreign Language.
The Lunenburg Public Schools and Office of Instruction is most appreciative of the additional time and
effort contributed by the educators serving on the task forces and would like to recognize them for
their work. Faculty members serving on the History/Social Science task force included: Candy
Brochu, Stephanie Beers, Jeff O'Neill, Kristine Bassett, B. J. Carbone, Judith Lahti, Fred Crellin, Peter
McCauliff, and Jane Whitney. Serving on the Foreign Language Task Force were: Sharon Kimball,
Jeanne Alberti, Kay Hillman, and Kathleen McGuigan. Dianna Homoleski and Margaret Zottoli wrote
the Business Curriculum. As a result of these talented educators work, rigorous learning outcomes that
embody the Lunenburg Public Schools' commitment to excellence are articulated in written documents
and accessible to the public.
During the past school year, curricula work was also focused upon advancing and evaluating earlier
adopted mathematics, science and technology standards, and English/Language Arts. Faculties in all
the schools were involved in reviewing MCAS results in each curricula area. As a result of our work
together at various levels of the organization (grade/department, school, and district) we were able to
identify strengths and weaknesses within the curriculum and instructional practices.
An additional aspect of our work on standards this year involved special educators beginning to link
students' Individual Education Plans (IEPs) with the general education curricula. Our commitment to a
unified educational system for ALL students and recent changes to federal regulations governing
services to students with disabilities required that changes be made. As a result, students with
disabilities will experience increased access to general educational outcomes and improved
performance.
Currently, two task forces are at work, developing or refining curriculum: (a) the Arts, and (b)
Mathematics, Science and Technology. Members of the Art Task Force are identifying learning
outcomes in music and art, K-12. The Mathematics, Science and Technology Task Force is completing
an integrated review of the current curricula. As part of this review, task force members are analyzing
test data, adjusting grade level and course outcomes as appropriate, and developing a local assessment
system to monitor student progress.

IOWA Results
In the spring of 1999 students in grades three, seven, and nine also were administered the Iowa Tests.
The Iowa Tests are standardized, norm-referenced tests. This means that the tests were given using
specified directions and under specific conditions to a representative group of students nationwide.
47
===PAGE 51===
Scores from this national sample become the "norms" that permit local schools to compare the
performance of their students with this national group. Scores from norm-referenced tests do not tell
what students know and what they don't know. They tell only how a student or group's performance
compares with that of others in the norm group.
The scores reported below are national percentile rank scores. Percentile ranks are reported in units
that range from 1 to 99. A percentile rank score of 67 means that the group earned a raw score that
was higher than 67 percent of the students in the norm group. It does not mean that the local group
scored as well or better than 67 percent of
districts nationwide.

An additional 2% of the population is identified with a disability and receives accommodations. The
district recently adopted policies and procedures for providing accommodations and services to
students with disabilities under Section 504. Section 504 is a broad civil rights law that protects
individuals with disabilities from discrimination in programs and activities that receive Federal
financial assistance. Eligible students include those who have or have had a physical or mental
impairment, which substantially limits a major life activity such as walking, seeing, hearing, speaking,
breathing, learning, working, caring for oneself, and performing manual tasks.
Summary
We have had another exciting and productive year in the Lunenburg Public Schools. As we focus on
outcomes, standards, and measurement, we continue to build our capacity to use data to improve the
instructional program. The data reported above revealed strengths and weaknesses in our
instructional program. We will continue to address the weaknesses through our school improvement
planning process. Some highlights from our analysis of the data:
Elementary Level - In English/Language Arts fewer students failed and more students scored
at the proficient level. In 1998 students obtained 44% of the points possible on the writing
prompt, in 1999 this increased to 66%. The majority of students scored at the needs
improvement level. Overall there was little to no change.
Middle School Level - More students scored at the advanced level in English/Language Arts
and Science. The majority (69%) of students scored at the proficient or advanced level.
However, more students are failing in all areas.
High School Level - In 1998 students obtained 64% of the points possible on the writing
prompt, in 1999 this increased to 70%. The IOWA indicate a positive upward trend in
mathematics. Overall there is little change in test scores.
The Office of Instruction takes pride in the accomplishments of the past year while recognizing that
many challenges and opportunities remain. We are focused on our mission -"To develop self-motivated
well educated students through a learning environment that is exciting, challenging, and
innovative." and invite all parents and the community to join with us to create positive futures for all
our children. For additional information on any of these topics, please contact Loxi Jo Calmes, Director
of Instruction at Brooks House 582-4114.
DIRECTOR OF FACILITIES & GROUNDS
TOWN FACILITIES
The following town facility projects were undertaken in FY 00. Repairs to the town hall exterior began
in October with adding the soffit and gutter on the awning on the side of the building. The town hall
attic was cleaned, railings and filing shelves installed in November 1999. Repairs are expected to
continue in the spring. A replacement HVAC system is being planned for installation in the summer of
2000. Replacement of a section of roof over the fire station was completed in July 1999. The 1st part of
a three-year renovation of the town beach building began with a replacement roof and porch project.
Repairs to Wallace Park include resurfacing the basketball court and repair of fencing are scheduled for
late spring 2000. Ritter Memorial Library heating system needed additional boiler controls and
replacement of a circulation pump. Repairs are expected to be completed by March 2000.
In Fiscal Year 01, a request for a Massachusetts Preservation Project Grant will be submitted in June
2000 for assistance in preserving the Town Hall.
SCHOOL FACILITIES
The focus of the School Maintenance Plan for FY'00 was improvement to common areas within the
schools. Areas that are used by all students such as restrooms, locker rooms, corridors were painted,
plumbing fixtures replaced, restrooms renovated with old and missing fixtures being replaced and
lighting upgraded. Repairs to the school heating systems consumed a substantial part of school
maintenance budget and reinforced concerns about the age of the heating plant for the High School and
the Elementary School. An additional $74,000 in state assistance for urgent school building repairs was
received to fund improvements to security at the High School, replacement of a failed gym folding
partition at the Middle School and repairs to all school exhaust systems.
50
===PAGE 54===
In Fiscal Year 01, the focus of capital maintenance will be the removal of asbestos pipe insulation from
classrooms and common areas at the Elementary School. This project will not remove all the asbestos
from the school however it will remove the asbestos most likely to be damaged thereby decreasing the
risk of accidental exposure to asbestos and reducing future maintenance costs. An engineering plan for
a replacement heating system for the Elementary school and the High School will be developed with
the view to begin replacement of the heating system in Fiscal Year 02.
HIGH SCHOOL A particular emphasis was placed on repairs at the High School in anticipation of the
school accreditation team visit. Hallway lockers were painted, lockers repaired, missing class bells were
replaced. All restrooms were painted, replacement mirrors, sink faucets and soap dispensers were
installed. As part of the capital improvement plan, a new laboratory for the High School Science
Department was started in July 1999 with the addition of six lab workstations. Asbestos repairs were
undertaken in August 1999 to clear valves for plumbing repairs. Funding for replacement of the
exterior doors for the high school was received in February 2000 from the state to improve security at
the school. Repairs to the school exhaust system will continue into the spring of 2000.
In Fiscal Year 01, the High School will have upgrade made to its communication systems with the
replacement of the public address system and installation of a digital phone system.
MIDDLE SCHOOL As part of the capital improvement plan a drop off and pick up sidewalk was added
along Oak Ave and 18 additional parking spaces were added by the creation of a new lower parking
apron. Repairs to the Boy's and Girls restroom included replacement toilet partitions for two restrooms
and replacement mirrors and shelves for four restrooms. Funding for replacement of the gymnasium
motorized folding partition was received in February 2000 from the state. Replacement of the folding
door is expected to be complete in late spring. Repairs to the school exhaust system will continue into
the spring of 2000 as well.
In Fiscal Year 01, the Middle School will have repairs made to locker rooms, replacement of worn floor
tiles in several areas around the school.
ELEMENTARY SCHOOL Replacement of the windows for the Elementary School gym, cafeteria, boiler
room and administrative areas was a capital maintenance project that began at the end of February and
is expected to continue in the summer of 2000. Repairs to the roofing system in August and September
were successful in stopping leaks in 6 classrooms. A replacement sidewalk from the school to Mass Ave
was completed in August 1999 as a capital maintenance repair. Boiler repairs were completed after
asbestos repairs to the boiler room were completed in October 1999. Repairs to the school exhaust
system will continue into the spring of 2000.
In Fiscal Year 01, the Passios Elementary School will have repairs made to skylights, restrooms, and
completion of the window replacement project
PRIMARY SCHOOL: Replacement of the flat roof section of the Primary School was completed in August
1999. The Primary School had the chimney re-pointed and capped in July 1999. A replacement heating
control system was installed in September but problems with the 60+-year-old distribution system
continued with steam leaks. Repairs are expected to be made in late February. Primary School also had
replacement doors installed for the three primary entrances. Repairs to the front fascia boards and
cupolas will begin in April 2000.
