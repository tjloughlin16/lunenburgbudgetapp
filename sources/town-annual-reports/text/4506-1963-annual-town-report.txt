Each year, about this time, as we prepare our annual report for the printer, wefind
ourselves in the unique position of being able at once to see where we have been and
where we are going, financially speaking. Our municipal cost of living during 1963has
by now been itemized, tabulated and spread out before US. The estimates of amounts
necessary to maintain the many town departments during the year 1964 have been assem-
bled. The money articles for the warrant for the annual meeting have been roughedout.
The Finance Committeehas studied, analyzed and made recommendations concerning these
matters. While all is not yet in complete readiness for final presentation to the voters,
we have seen enough to lay a foundation for an educated guess about 1964.
By the time you read this report, or shortly thereafter, you will know with certainty
what the Town's fiscal law will be for the ensuing year. Finance is at the heart of muni-
cipal administration. The many activities can be carriedon only by raising and spending
large sums of money. How large is left to the determination of the voters. We areaware
that the "pocketbook nerve" is the most sensitive part of the taxpayer's anatomy. indis-
charging the duties of our office, we try to be guided by this fact. People everywhere
complain of heavy taxes, but it is likewise true that people everywhere are demanding
more and better municipal services. Holdinga budget in line with our collective pocket-
book becomes indeed a challenging task. All things being considered, there is no golden
road to efficient, economical and completely satisfactory municipal government.
When we comment in this vein, it is not to say that the residents of this Town and in
particular, the taxpayers, are not entitled to expect from local government as good and
efficient administration and management of the Town's affairs as possible, consistent with
its financialability, size and population. We think a comparison with other municipalite
is a satisfactory device for measuring our progress toward meeting the expectations of our
people. have undertaken to ascertain the relative merits of Lunenburg's administrative
policies with those of other cities and towns in our area of influence. If the standards by
which cities and towns are usually judged be the criteria of merit, we submit that Lunenburg
has not fared too badly the years and has, in fact, morethan held its own. Wehave
selected, for comparison, what we believe to be the main standards by which a city or
town is usually judged, the comparison being based on the latest figures available. The
standards are: I. Rate of Growth (population) over the last 20 years; 2. Rate of Growth
over the last 10 years; 3. Valuation (full property values, based on the records of the
State Tax Commissioner); 4. Tax Rate (actual tax rate, but on the basis of full values of
property); 5. School Rate (that portion of the tax dollar allocated to school purposes);
6. Debt Ratio (ratio of bonded indebtedness to assessed valuation); 7. Free Cash (sur-
plus revenue less outstanding uncollected taxes of prior years); and 8. Tax Collections.
In comparing Lunenburg with Ashby, Ayer, Fitchburg, Groton, Harvard, Lancaster, Leominster, Pepperell,
Shirley and Townsend, this is what we find:
1. GROWTH - Lunenburg's growth has been considerably more rapid than that of anyother city or town in our
area of influence, with a population increase of 189% in the past two decades and 62% in the last ten years. The
average increase for the 11 cities and towns was 65% and 16% respectively.
2. VALUATION - Lunenburg's property valuation per capita of $5200 is second only to Harvard's $5300. The
average value per capita of the 11 cities and towns is $3611.
3. TAX RATE - On the basis of full value, Lunenburg's $28.20 rate is the fourth lowest, and the second lowest
in population range. Leominster - $24.70; Pepperell - $26.30; Harvard - $27.70; Lunenburg - $28.20. Theaverage
rate for the 11 communities is $33.66. For further comparison, Fitchburg's rate on the basis of full value is $31.30.

In recent years, our Community, along with all others, has undergone great changes. If we are to preserve respon-
sible local government, we must make realistic adjustments to meet the challenge presented by these changes. It is our
desire to keep Lunenburghi on the list of progressive communities. In line with this thinking, we feel that some speci-
fic steps should be taken for the promotion and development of the industrial resources of our Town. As to the approach to
be made, we feel the voters Idhave a voicehere. Should weaccept the enabling act which permits the establishment

our industrial potential before accepting the enabling act? There are two articles in the warrant for this annual meeting
designed to accommodate a choice. Your Selectmen recommend that something be done, as their own duties and respon-
sibilities are far too numerous to allow them to make any substantial investigation and appraisal in this area.

and, we are sure, yours.

Included on the opposite page is what is referred to as the "Assessors' Recap", the
work sheet on which the 1963 tax rate was computed. It is hoped that this "recap", and the
explanation which follows, will serve as a basis for a better understanding by the Towns-
people of the factors that enter into the determination of a tax rate.
Item A includes all amounts voted at the Annual Town Meeting to be raised by taxation or appro-
riated from available funds. To Item A was added Items Band C, amounts due the State and County,
and Item D (Overlay), amount included to offset abatement of taxes. Item E in the amount of
$1,572,964.98 is the total of Items A, B, C and D and represents the gross amount to be raised.
From this gross amount was subtracted estimates of receipts to be received during the year (Item F).
Included under Item F are estimates of amounts to be received from the State Income and Corporation
and Old Age Meal Taxes as certified by the State on theso-called "Cherry Sheet". All other esti-
mated receipts were based on and could not exceed amounts received for the same purposes during
the previous year. It should benoted that the Motor Vehicle Excise, the largest item under Item F,
reduced the amount to be raised by taxation by $142,661.61 and accounted for approximately one-
third of the $459,887.39 total. Item F-30 listed individually, amounts voted to be taken from a-
vailable funds. In order to arrive at Item G, the net amount to be raised by taxation on polls and
property, it was necessary to subtract Item F-32, the total of all estimated receipts and available
funds, from the gross amount to be raised, Item E. The tax rate of $82.00 per $1000 valuation was
then easily determined by dividing the total assessed value of real and personal property (Item J)
into the net amount to be raised (Item G) less poll tax (Item H), multiplied by $1000, as follows:
$921,071.25 - $3932 X $1000 ÷ 184,625 = $82.00.
Assessors are also required by law to differentiate between the General Tax Rate and
the School Rate. In 1963 the School Rate accounted for $57.23 of the $82.00 total.
Expressed in percentage, the School Rate was 70% of the Total Tax Rate. This means that
70¢ of every tax dollar went toward school maintenance and operation. Of the 351 cities
and towns in Massachusetts there were, in 1963, but 24 municipalities in which theratio
of theschool rate to the total tax rate equaled or exceeded that of Lunenburg. Themedian
figure (for school rates) in considering all municipalities is only 54%.
In 1963, for the first time, we joined with the Assessors from 12 area towns to meet
and discuss mutual problems and methods of assessing. Another such meeting or conference
has been planned for April 1964. During 1963, there was comparatively little new home
and commercial construction in Lunenburg, which fact accounts for the relatively small
percentage increase in total valuation. It should be pointed out that it takes approxi-
mately $130,000 in increased valuation to offset each additional $11,000 voted to be raised by taxation.
In November 1963 the Governor signed into law an act providing a real estate tax abatement for certain persons 70
years of age or over. Chapter 808 of the Acts of 1963 spellsout the various requirements which must be met by qualifying
elderly persons. By the time 1964 real estate bills are mailed, State-approved forms for filing for such exemption will
be available at the Assessors' Office. Applications for abatement must be filed with the Board of Assessors on or before
October 1, 1964.
A foreword given at the opening of the Assessors' School in 1962, by William G. O'Hare, Jr., Director of the
Bureau of Government Research at the University of Massachusetts, included the following thoughts: "The cost of lo-
cal government will, inall probability, continue to rise with a consequent burdening of property to meet community needs.
On this account, not neglecting the precepts of justice and equity, the valuation of property for tax purposes shall be
a continuing and arduous responsibility of assessors. The citizens of the Commonwealth are concerned with both the
pattern and quality of public spending, but even more particularly with the quality of public service received. The
Assessors, therefore, need to be certain that their sector of the governmental unit is managed in the most up-to-date
and honorable fashion."
Lunenburg Assessors are ever mindful of the responsibilities referred to above and will continue to strive to carry
out the duties of their office in "an up-to-date and honorable fashion."

The report of the Town Counsel, which is contained on the following page, makes reference to many municipal
laws enacted by the State Legislature. Included in the Acts of 1963 were some 93 enactments which affect and pertain
to the various functions carried out in the Town Office. Changes in the laws pertaining to absentee voting, the abolition
of the poll tax, the establishment of a new basis for the distribution of State and County taxes and an act providing
a tax abatement on the real property of certain elderly persons were among the major enactments affecting this office.
The duties of the Town Clerk are many and varied. The Clerk is the official recorder of the Town's events and
activities and issues all licenses and certificates. It is in this office that vital statistics (births, marriages and deaths)
are recorded as are the minutes of every town meeting; the acceptance of streets as public ways; mortgages on personal
property; and the installation of poles, wires and conduits. The Town Clerk also serves as agent of the Board of Health
and of the Licensing Authority (Selectmen). Statistics of the Town Clerk for the year 1963 are as follows:

The Town Clerk is by statute a member of the Board of Registrars of Voters and serves as its clerk. Among the
duties and responsibilities carried out by the Town Clerk and her staff are the processing of nomination papers and
absentee ballots, preparing and printing ballots and voting lists for town elections, the preparation of work and tally
sheets to be used in the counting and canvassing of votes and setting up and overseeing town meetings and elections.
This office also registers voters.
As the Town Clerk serves assecretary to the Board of Selectmenwhich is also the Board of Health, Board of Welfare
and Licensing and Zoning Authority, much time is spent by the Clerk and her staff in carrying out the duties of these
offices. Not least among these duties is the preparation of the Town Report and the administration of a coordinated
system of sanitary, driveway, plumbing, building and zoning permits, in cooperation with the Nashoba Associated
Boards of Health, Highway Superintendent, Plumbing Inspector, Planning Board and Board of Appeals. Of the 126
applications filed during 1963 inaccordance with the Protective (Zoning) By-law, 107 permits were issued, 13 denied
and 6 referred to the Board of Appeals for Special Authorization. One hundred seven sanitary, 83 plumbing, and 58
driveway permits were also processed through this office.
A detailed report of the Tax Collector appearson page 25 of the Financial Section of this book. As of December
31, 1963, all prior year taxes were 100% collectedas were the current year poll taxes and betterment assessments. The
motor vehicle excise remains the most difficult tax to collect as any person not connected with the Tax Office would
have to be a Philadelphia lawyer to completely understand the procedures. The Town must now pay over to the State
$. 15 per bill to offset the cost of an even more complicated system.
The Treasurer, who is custodian of Town funds, is responsible for the receipt and disbursement of all Town monies
and handles investments and bond issues. A town, by statute, is allowed to borrow 5% of the average of the last 3
years' assessed valuation of taxable property (including motor vehicles) less abatements. An additional 5% may be
borrowed with permission of a State board called the Emergency Finance Board. This type of debt is referred to as
"Inside the Debt Limit". Special acts of the Legislature enable towns to borrow outside of the debt limit. Much of
our school borrowing was under one or more of these special acts. Of the $749,000 funded debt. outstanding on Dec-
ember 31, 1963, $68,000 was borrowed inside the debt limit and $681,000 outside.

LEGAL
Robert J. Larkin, Town Counsel
In our report of a year ago, we pointed to the growing importance of municipal legislation in
the so-called, small towns. We think the passing year has demonstrated the soundness of this obser-
vation. The effect of many laws enacted are not always perceptible to most of US. This is particularly
true of legislation which is state-wide, rather than local, in its application. Added to this is the
seeming lack of "news appeal" which would otherwise serve to publicize the new laws, and amend-
ment of existing laws. Nevertheless, there is a great body of the law pertaining to municipalities
which reaches directly into the local community, and is solely dependent upon the 'ayes' or the
'nays' of the voters. This type of legislation is commonly known as permissive legislation. If you
will scan the warrant for this meeting, you will doubtlessly find samplings of this vestige of almost
pure democracy.
It is safe to say that, with very few exceptions, the various and sundry matters which are presented
for voter consideration in any town meeting warrant, have been the subject of much research and
discussion by one or more responsible persons. This is doubly true where duly appointed committees
have been delegated to investigate and report back with findings and recommendations as a prelude
to voter action. In many such instances, the work of the committee has been carried on for a full
year. Your Town Counsel has met with some such committees, from time to time, during the year.
Speaking of legislation which is state-wide rather than local in its application, and which
escapes attention because of its lack of "news appeal", we have had a good example of the other
extreme. The Conflict of Interest Law became effective in May, 1963. Because of its dramatic
implications, scarcely a person escaped its impact. Prior to its effective date, and after hours of
discussion, the Board of Selectmen, the Town Clerk and Town Counsel met and effected a classifica-
tion of our municipal employees. Occasionally, during the year, your Town Counsel was called
upon to render rulings and opinions concerning the application of this very confusing law.
There was the usual complement of paperwork during the year which ranged from easements and
other legal documents, to general correspondence. Some claims and complaints of one kind or
another were made against the Town, but all were disposed of without resort to litigation or the
payment of money damages. Currently, there are two suits pending in the Superior Court, in which
the Town is the principal party. In one of these cases, the Planning Board claiming to be aggrieved
by the action of the Zoning Appeal Board in granting a variance to an applicant therefor, has
appealed the decision to the Superior Court. In the other case, the Appeal Board granted special
authorization to a petitioner to make a particular use of his land. An abutter claiming to be aggrieved
by this decision has appealed it to the Superior Court. As of this writing, the cases have not been
reached for a hearing.

PERSONNEL STUDY COMMITTEE
Calvin K. Sholl, Chairman
Howard A. Hamel
Stanley H. Howard
Walter B. Keeler
Phillips F. Lewis
"Lunenburg, like many other communities in the Commonwealth, is still in that state of transition of municipal
duties and responsibilities ranging from avocational stewardship to vocational employment." This statement, appearing in
the report of Sumner D. Charm, Consultant, describes the composition of our work force in the Town and some of the
problems incident to arriving at a practical classification and compensation plan. Development of sound and ac-
cepted practices and procedures, while at the same time recognizing the taxpayer and the unique nature of employ-
ment problems in a town the size of Lunenburg, represents a real challenge.
One of the assignments of the Study Committee, by virtue of articles 10 and 11 voted at the Town Meeting last
year, was to make a recommendation on Chapter 32B, General Laws. This legislation permits towns to provide group
life insurance and medical benefits with the Town paying half of the cost. After considerable discussion and frequent
meetings with insurance representatives, the Committee recommends that the provisions of Chapter 32B not be adopted
at this time. Our feeling is that first consideration should begiven to adoption of a classificationand compensation plan.
Early in May of 1963, the Personnel Study Committee engaged Sumner D. Charm and Associates of Boston to for-
mulate a compensation and classification plan. The study was essentially complete and a report made in early De-
cember of 1963. During this period the Study Committee met frequently to review parts of the study which include
detailed descriptions of each position, an evaluation of the duties of each job, a comparison by comparable jobs to
goingrates in other similar communities, wage and salary ranges for each position, and an analysis of "fringe benefits".
A total of 81 jobs, including the non-teaching staff in the School Department, were included in the survey.
The Personnel Study Committee is strongly in favor of a compensation and classification plan that will contribute
system and logic to the administration of wages and salaries and at the same time fairly compensateour employees. We
endorse the basic recommendations submitted by Sumner D. Charm & Associates and view the study as sound and
practical. We have recommended that the Finance Committee and Board of Selectmengive consideration to the salary
ranges established by the study in reviewing 1964 departmental budgets and requests for increases. Wedo feel, however,
that more attention on the part of the Committee should be directed to details of the study and analysis of the possible
cost to the Town in adopting its various provisions. For these reasons, the Committee recommends continuation of its
activity during 1964 with a final report and recommendation to be made at the next regular or special Town Meeting.

POLICE
Sherwood G. Winchester, Chief
Frederick B. Hobbs, Sergeant
Elwood C. Erickson, Patrolman
Each year the Police Department is faced with new and more numerous demands.
In addition there are certain phases of the police work that remain constant but nonethe-
less require a considerable amount of attention. Each year there appears to bea particu-
lar phase of crime that is more prominent than other types. In 1961 teen-age drinking
was in the forefront, in 1962 larcenies placed a heavy burden on the Department and in
1963 we were involved with a serious loitering situation in the Whalom Area.
During 1963 there were 5 fatal accidents in our Town and our accident rate was one
of the highest in the area. It is evident that additional manpower is required for adequate
traffic control, which continues to be our number one problem. A prohibitive amount of
time is also required to execute the large quantities of records required by such agencies
as State and Federal police, insurance companies, attorneys and military services.
In view of the ever increasing work load being placed on the Police Department,
Chief Winchester has included in his 1964 budget request an allowance for a fourth full-
time man whose duties will include the handling of routine office work including day logs,
accident reports and routine complaints, traffic duty, and patrol work at Whalom Park
in connection with the loitering problem.
Additional office space has also been requested by the Chief who has emphasized
a need for space which would accommodate permanent files and confidential data to be stored under lock andkey and
would allow interviews and interrogations to be conducted with more privacy.
During the year the Department made 207 arrests. Motor vehicle violations totalled 119 and continued to top the
list. The most common violations recorded were speeding (42), operating under the influence (10), passing a school
bus (9), using a motor vehicle without authority (8), and stop sign violations (8). Eighty-eight persons were arrested
for crimes against persons and public order. The three most common crimes investigated were intoxication (39), break-
ing, entering and larceny (9), and disturbing the peace (7).
AUXILIARY POLICE

CIVIL DEFENSE
Robert J. Okerman, Director
Civil Defense does not function merely for nuclear war but for any type of emergency. Most
Americans who worried and fretted two years ago about being on the receiving end of a nuclear
bombhave long ago abandoned the agonizing soul-searching of the Berlin crisis of 1961 or the Cuban
Crisis of 1962. We have no magic formula, short of an actual emergency, to arouse the general
public. That public apathy has been and will continue to be a threat to the security of our Country
is all the more reason why Civil Defense must be prepared when everyone else is unprepared for
the eventuality of a natural or unnatural emergency.
1963 saw the commencement of a complete overhaul of our local Civil Defense program. A School
Evacuation program was established which, in the case of an emergency, will enable all school
children to reach theirhomes as quicklyas possible and with the least amount of confusion. A prac-
tice run will be held in the schools sometime in 1964. An emergency control center which was con-
structed in the Junior-Senior High School Basement will serve as an auxiliary communications center
in case communications are lost at the Fire Station. All fire, police, town, civil defense and mutual
aid radio and telephone equipment will be duplicated. The remaining part of the basement has now
been accepted as a fallout shelter and will be used as a first aid station which will be completely
equipped with all necessary first aid equipment.
Radiological classes were conducted at the Junior-Senior High School. Aneight-hour course
taught by Dr. Roettger of Worcester Polytechnical Institute was attended by twelve persons. The
course made Lunenburg eligible to receive five radiological kits. These kits, which are used to track
fallout, will be located in all sectors of the Town.
A question which is most often asked is, "Should we havea community fallout shelter program?"
It has been written thata realistic evaluation of any defensive measure requires an examination of the
threat. Most of US would agree that the most ominous threat confronting US today is anattack initiated
by the Soviet Union. There are many strategies which the Soviet Union or any other aggressor may
choose to follow. Consequently, thereare many types of nuclear war. It is more or less agreed that
in most cases radiation would be the biggest threat faced by the majority of the population.
What protection does Civil Defense, centered about a community fallout shelter program, offer?
The greatest protection is that offered against radiation. If a person takes shelter intime, he will be
protected against the effects of radiation from fallout. Fallout shelters also offer some protection from
blast, as people who might otherwise be injured by flying glass or debris are likely to remain unhurt
if they are ina shelter at the time of burst. It is ascientific fact that firestorms are unlikely to occur
with the exception of three or four locations in the entire United States. Most community fallout
shelters could survive ordinary fires since they are relatively brief in duration and not as hot as a
- 44 -
===PAGE 47===
firestorm. Last, but far from least, the community shelter offers help from one's friends and neighbors.
An examination of civil defense preparations in European countries during World War II proves
that - I. it is difficult to anticipate the consequences of war, but it is far better to attempt to do so
in a realistic manner and plan accordingly, than to fail to face this difficult problem as some people
would advocate we should, 2. the value of an effective shelter system and warning system were dra-
matically demonstrated time and again, 3. careful planning and thorough carry through of post attack
recovery measures were especially helpful for morale and did much to speed recovery, 4. it is clearly
necessary to build shelters and plan for recovery before the attack occurs.
The 1964 Annual Meeting Warrant contains an article relative to the establishment of another
school building committee. It is the recommendation of your Civil Defense Director that any plans,
whether they be for an addition to an existing plant or for a completely new and separate facility
contain provisions for fallout protection. Even those persons who argue that shelters are a waste of
time and money because war will almost certainly never occur, should not object to shelters which
can also be used for cafeterias, gymnasiums or other school purposes.

TREE WARDEN
Richard W. Brown, Tree Warden
Moth Superintendent
Trees are a valuable asset to any community. Unfortunately, too many of US think of trees as being permanent
and indestructible. This is not the case. Trees need an occasional assist from man particularly in times of droughtor
when they lack nourishment. Because of the dry weather, 1963 saw some of the Maples lose their leaves prematurely,
the Oaks make slower growth than usual, and the Elms become defoliated. These factors tend to weaken trees and
make them prey for more serious menaces.
Shade tree planting, the cutting and removal of dead trees, and insect control were the major activities of the
Tree Department during 1963. A total of sixty-eight trees were cut and cleared away. During 1963, ninety-two
diseased Elms were cut, cleared and burned. Thirty-four samples were taken from Elm trees and sent to the University
of Massachusetts to determine whether or not the trees were infected with Dutch Elm Disease. The testing disclosed
that all trees tested were diseased. Twenty-three of these trees were cut by the Department of Natural Resources ata
cost to the State of $560.00. In addition, seventy-three trees which were obviously diseased, and therefore, werenot
tested, were cut and removed. Control of public nuisances during 1963 cost the Town a total of $2900.00, $126.35 of
which was reimbursed by the State.
Sealer of Weights and Measures
Raymond F. Butterfield, Sealer
While the large dollar values of seemingly small errors are understood by weights and measures officials, the
great importance of accurate weighing devices shouldalso bestressed toall who buy orsell. Every scale in commercial
use is a money machine, and, if inaccurate, it is either taking money away from the customer or giving away profits
of the businessman. It has been pointedout that an ounce is worth $1875. That is a lot of money for an ounce of any-
thing, but a one ounce error on a scale making 100 weighings a day, 300 days a year at $1.00 per pound will result
in an error of $1875 in one year's time.
Scales in Lunenburg are regularly tested for accuracy once a year, and unannounced spot checks are frequently
made. This is true also of gasoline pump meters and fuel oil truck meters and all other commercially used weighing
and measuring devices. Of the 160 weighing and measuring devices inspected during 1963, 155 were adjusted and
sealed and 5 were condemned by our Sealer of Weights and Measures.
All inhabitants or persons having usual places of business within the Town using weighing or measuring devices for
the purpose of buying or selling goods, wares or merchandise, for public weighing or for hire or reward, are hereby
notified to bring them in to be tested, adjusted and sealed.

The Conservation Commission and the Town Forest Committee hold joint meetings and carry out
a joint program. It is possible that in the not too distant future legislation will be enacted which
will legally combine the duties of these two committees whose functionsare similar and at times in-
tertwine. However, as each group has its own sources from which to receive help and advice, it
seems advisable for the present to retain separate identities.
Conservation has arrived. Wise men and women have pleaded for it for years. Wild life advo-
cates have worked and worried. Conservationists have championed its cause. Experts have watched
with apprehension the water resources dwindle and shrink. Supporters of natural resourceshave noted
fearfully the destruction of forests, the filling in and draining of swamps and wet lands, and the e-
limination of areas that support and maintain wild life. Now at last there is hope that those com-
munities which have not already lost their natural resources have gained a new awareness of conser-
vation and will fight to further its cause. People in all walks of life who have previously taken
natural resources for granted are now apprehensive. They have seen communities lose practically
all of their resources because of complacency and lack of leadership.
Included in the local organizations which have fought for years to preserve Lunenburg's natural
resources are the Grange, the Woman's Club, Girl Scouts, Boy Scouts, 4-H Clubs, and the Sports-
men's Clubs. Sportsmen's Clubs have been front line fighters in the battle for conservation. These
men deserve a great deal of credit and praise. It is the fees paid by the sportsmen for fishing and
hunting licenses that have paid for much of the conservation development in our State.
The Conservation Law allows a town to set aside each year in a Conservation Fund a sum equal
to one-twentieth of one per cent of the total assessed valuation. The State Department of Natural
Resources, undera Self-Help Program, will reimburse up to fifty percent of the cost of land purchased
for conservation. Fifty percent of the $9, 110 expended in 1963 for the purchase of the "Cowdrey
Lot" willbe reimbursed by the State early in 1964. This lot will pay for itself many times over through
the sale of lumber under a forest improvement plan.
It has been written that the basic principles and laws governing the entire living community
must be generally understood if wehuman beings are to esuccessful in maintaining the productivity
of the earth. Nature will not accept ignorance of her laws as an alibi. In his book entitled "Web
of Life", ecologist John H. Storer wrote - "Scientists have proved that it is possible to use the life-
supporting natural resources of the world without destroying them. These resources can be renewed
and made more productive with use. This is a triumph of the human intellect. The great and de-
ciding test, however, still remains -whether man can coordinate knowledge into understanding and
build within his heart the incentives and the wisdom to use these new-found powers wisely, and with
responsibility, for the common good."

Population growth in Lunenburg is inevitable. Detailed land capacity studies indicate that the
total number of dwelling units at ultimate development of the Town will approach 8,000 with an
equivalent ultimate population of 28,000 persons.
This ultimate is, of course, an appreciable number of years off and the number can only be
estimated. If the growth of the Town continues at its previous 15 year average, this figure will be
reached in about 75 years. On the other hand, if the growth rate isaccelerated somewhat as studies
of regional trends suggest, it could be reached in 50 or 60 years. Simultaneously, with this popula-
tion growth will arise the need for expansion of municipal facilities. Planning studies, not only to
produce predictionand estimates of future needs ofour Town but also to produce a scheduleand plans
for ways and means for taking care of these needs, is a responsibility of the Planning Board and one
which consumes an appreciable portion of the Board's effort. While the period to reach the ultimate
development, or even one-half of it, appears long in number of years, it is short in terms of munici-
palhistory. If neededsites are not planned for nowand purchased at opportune timesand if facilities
are not planned, sited and built to permit the required expansion, costly site acquisitions, facility
rearrangements and facility relocation, and their excessive burden on the taxpayer will result.
During the past year, the Planning Board studied and prepared a preliminary resume' on sites
for new and expanded Highway Department facilities and a preliminary study and resume' on sites

VETERANS' BENEFITS
Edward H. Strickland, Veterans' Agent
In addition to a personal interview, every application for Veterans' aid involves verification of discharge papers,
birth certificates, dependency statements, and wage and bank reports. Records of marriage, divorce and death, and
court records are also frequently required. Needless to say, securing these records takes time and sometimes involves
a considerable amount of correspondence. A home investigation must also be made to verify and supplement informa-
tion given at the time of application and to give the agent a better understanding of the overall problem. After thor-
oughly reviewing all of the facts, a report which includes the Veterans' Agent's recommendations is submitted to the State.
Only aftera State Investigatorhas given his stamp of approval does the Town become eligible for reimbursement by the
State of 50% of amounts paid to Town Settled Cases and 100% of amounts paid on account of veterans with State Settlements.
During 1963, eighteen veterans and/or their families were interviewed. Nine families were aided by the Town at
a total cost of $6,673.36 which was $4,517.68 less than the amount paid the previous year. Fifty percent of the total
amount expended during 1963 will be reimbursed by the State. Reimbursements received from the State during 1963
totalled $5,666.48 and represented amounts billed the State for the calendar year 1962.
Among the services made available by the local Veterans' Agent is information regarding medical and hospital
benefits, disability compensation, pensions and bonuses, Government Life Insurance, burial benefits, education,
training and loans under the G.I. Bill.
Although the total number of cases aided during 1963 was small, it is expected that this number will increase
during the next several years as World War I and II Veterans grow older.

CHILDREN'S LIBRARIAN
Eleanor B. Whitcomb
Circulation for the year again reached a new high, the total being 50,471. A breakdown of this total circulation
figure is as follows: Adult - 17,055; Children - 30,753; Periodicals - 2,663. The library now has about eighteen
thousand volumes on its shelves and subscribes to about sixty periodicals. During 1963, 457 adult books and 602 junior
books were added by purchase. In line with the policy of keeping the collection up-dated, 382 adult books and 61
children's books were removed. Gifts for the year were 173 volumes received from the following persons: Mrs. Charles H.
Hillman, Mr. and Mrs. Earl B. Pickering, Jr. - in memory of Nicholas Johnson, Mrs. Bigelow Crocker, Jr., Mrs.
Radcliffe Cheesman, Mrs. Irving Kimball, Mrs. Monica LaBonte, Mrs. Henry W. Pope, Mrs. E. J. McCormic,
Mrs. David Richards, Miss Jean Parker, Mrs. William B. Hurd, Mrs. Laurence Ebersole, and the Four Corners Club -
in memory of Willa Harley.
At the Town Meeting in March the article for the erection of an addition to the library was approved and the
trustees worked during the Spring with Dr. Arland Dirlam, the architect, to prepare plans and specifications. These
were advertised for bids during the Summer but when the bids were opened they were found to be unsatisfactory. The
trustees felt that these bids should be rejected and that new bids should be asked for because a sufficient number of
competitive bids were not received. After some minor revisions of the plans and specifications, the job was again
advertised for bids and, this time, a satisfactory bid in the amount of $68,979 was received from the Crowley Realty
Corporation and accepted. Accordingly, a contract was signed and construction started early in October. Work is
progressing satisfactorily as of the date of this report and the contractor feels that the new addition should be ready for
use in March and the complete job, including renovations in the existing library and outside work, should be completed
during the Spring. The trustees also believe that the $75,000 voted at the Town Meeting will be sufficient to
cover the contract costs and architect fees as well as certain necessary extras which have been incurred due to the
condition of the existing building. The major unexpected expense has been to replace the basement floor of the
existing library, which was rotted out underneath, with a concrete slab. The trustees have been pleased with the
cooperation received from the contractor in endeavoring to keep the library operating during construction and renova-
tion, although some inconvenience is to be expected. To date, the only time the library has been shut down was when
the new boilerwas being installed and thebuilding was without heat. This shutdown was scheduled over the holidays,
when demands on the library have always been light.
With respect to the furnishings, many people in Town have indicated a desire to participate directly in this
program of expanding the facilities of the library. Accordingly, a citizens committee is being established to receive
contributions to be used for the purchase of additional furnishings which the Town will be asked to accept as a gift.
The 1964 budget reflects an increase of $2,237 over last year's figure. The new addition and improved facilities
will further increase demands upon the library and the staff. Therefore, the trustees are contemplating increasing the
time during which the library is open from forty to fifty hours. These additional hours should also prove beneficial to
the students who do a great deal of research and study with the encyclopedias and other references which are now
available at the library. With a new up-to-date heating plant, it is felt that there will be no substantial rise inbasic
maintenance costs. The increase reflected in the 1964 library budget does not, therefore, include additional mainten-
ance costs but will, instead, expand the various library services.
Fines in the amount of $858.87 were collected and turned over to the Town Treasurer.

Thereare at present 77 miles of public roads within the Town, exclusive of the State Highway (Route 2A). General

CHAPTER 81
Work under this allotment included bituminous and gravel patching, scraping of gravel roads, treatment by calcium
chloride of various gravel roads, repairing of fences and guard rails, cleaning of drainage structures and ditches,
cutting back shoulders of roads, sweeping of roads in preparation for surfacing, treatment with asphalt of a portion of the
bituminous surface mileage. Approximately 12 miles of bituminous surface were treated, for which 32,725 gallons of

Round Street, Townsend Harbor Road, Leominster Road, Holman Street, Northfield Road, Howard Street, West Street

ROAD MACHINERY MAINTENANCE
The money for this account is transferred from what is known as the "machinery rental" account. This means
simply that whenever a piece of Town-owned equipment is used, the rental designated for each piece of equipment
is charged against the account under which the work is being performed and the amount charged is credited to the
"machinery rental" account. At the Annual Town Meeting a portion or all of the rental accumulated during the
previous year is transferred to the Road Machinery Maintenance Account to be used for repairsand maintenanceas well
as the purchase of new equipment. The Town employs a part-time maintenance man to repair Highway Department
equipment, and keep it in good condition. A portion of this man's wages is paid out of the Road Machinery Account.
In addition to maintenance and repair of all highway equipment, gasoline, diesel fuel, oil, grease, tire chains, tires,
plow blades, oxygen, acetylene and small tools are purchased from this account. During the year, the 1946 Galion
Grader underwent extensive repairs. This piece of equipment is of vital importance in the functioning of the Depart-
ment. The 1958 G.M.C. Dump Truck and Sander were painted.

The Park Department has under its jurisdiction and is responsible for the maintenance of six parks and playground
areas, three baseball and softball diamonds, three tennis courts, one ice skating pond, one outdoor basketball court

usual playground maintenance this figure included the cost of preliminary development of a new softball field at Mar-
shall Park, reforestation of the Marshall Park and Marshall pine groves, straightening the shoreline and cleaning brush
at Marshall Pond and construction of a new parking lot at Marshall Pond.
Special projects carried out with monies appropriated under special articles included the resurfacing of the tennis
courts at Wallis Park which was accomplished for the sum of $3, 175 and installation of a backstop and fence at the
Little League field at Marshall Park, the total cost of which was $1,440.
This year's adult program included softball and horseshoe leagues and tennis instruction and tournaments. Plans for
an adult volleyball program which will commence early in 1964 were formulated by the Park Commissioners.
Attendance at the summer program was greater than ever before and all phases of the program were met with en-
thusiasm. Mr. Emory Merrill served as Director of the program. Miss Sue Billings supervised the center playground
and Miss Susan Drumm the one at Wallis Park. Mrs. Irene Maxim headed the arts and crafts programs with Mrs. Patricia
Kalagher serving as her assistant. In addition to general playground activities the summer schedule included arts and
crafts, basketball leagues for boys and girls, boys baseball, girls softball, tennis instruction, track, and several spe-
cial events such as the Soap Box Derby, a trip to Benson's Wild Animal Farmand swimmingand picnicking at Willard
Brook. The Winter program which included skating and ice hockeywas highlighted by the innovation of a Biddy Bas-
ketball League.
Considerable displeasure has been expressed by Townspeople regarding the crowded conditions at Aqualand Beach
brought about by the fact that the owners and operators now contract with the City of Leominster as well as the Town
of Lunenburg. A survey of potential swimming areas within the Town has been commenced and an article will be in-
cluded in the 1964 annual meeting warrant which calls for the appropriation of $2,000 for preliminary development of
a section of Mulpus Brook which is located on the so-called "Cowdrey Lot" purchased last year for conservation and
recreational purposes.
Other articles included in the 1964 warrant call for an appropriation of $1,875 for repairs of the Tennis Courts
at Marshall Park, $625 for the purchase and installation of a fence to be installed across the front of Marshall Park and
$600 for playground equipment to be placed at the Wallis Park and Center Playgrounds. The 1964 Park Department
budget totals $8,405.18.
Other plans and recommendations for 1964 include further improvements at Marshall Pond, development of a soft-
ball field at Marshall Park, repairs to and maintenance of the Wallis Park quonset hut which is used as anarts and crafts
center, installation of toilet facilities at Wallis Park and the reseeding of the outfield at Marshall Park. In addition,
a survey of existing recreational facilities and programs will be conducted for the State Recreation Commission.
In 1963 the total cost of carrying out the various Park Department programs and maintaining the several park and
playground areas was $7,870.25.

Superintendent's Annual Report
We live in a world where Education is the foundation which will largely deter-
mine our ability to survive as a leading nation of the world. The author of a 20th
Century Fund Study has recently said: "To be willing to do anything does not help
because there are vacancies only for those who can do something." There is no doubt
in my mind that this thought pinpoints the emphasis which must be placed on all
phases of education in a world where through automation, machines are doing more and
more of our work and unskilled labor is therefore an increasing drug on the market.
Providing the kinds of education to meet the needs of all of our young people
must be the objective of all of America's school systems if we are to minimize the
burden and great cost to American society of untrained people.
Such a challenge presents to all of us the need to think bigger than ever
before, the need to possibly combine our efforts with other communities SO that the
educational facilities and opportunities for all of our children can truly be met.
The problems the local community faces in trying to meet these needs often seem
extremely costly and insurmountable, for Lunenburg continues to grow in school popu-
lation along with SO many other communities throughout our country. Last year I men-
tioned that the School Committee and Administration found themselves so often pre-
occupied with the "three B's" of school business: "Babies, Budgets and Buses". To
that I must add a fourth B this year which would stand for "Buildings". There seems
to be a geometrical progression as we relate ourselves from the first of these to
the others. Increased births do contribute to larger enrollments, therefore the
need for more buildings, the need for more buses and the inevitable effect of in-
creasing budgets to pay for all of these obligations. The needs for the so-called
four "B's" are always with us and School Committees must of necessity be occupied
with their business. There is constant need, however, to see that concentration on
these does not overshadow our more important need for emphasis on the "Three R's".
In Lunenburg, your school committee and administration are trying to do both well;
mindful of the fact that our school enrollment is constantly increasing as is visibly
indicated when we say that in September 1964 there will be approximately 370 more
pupils in the Lunenburg Schools than there were in September 1961 when the last
addition to the High School was opened.
It is obvious that such an increase in pupils is enough to fill twelve class-
rooms. The end of this growth is not in sight at the moment. Such increases in
enrollment do require more of everything from space, supplies, books and buses to
teachers, all of which inevitably contribute to larger budgets, The School Committee
of necessity must concern itself with these problems because schools today have in-
deed become big business. It is fortunate that School Committees are often helped
by the hard work of other groups with some of these problems. The Long-Range School
Housing Study Committee is one of these. Over a several year period they have been
carefully studying school facility needs and it is expected that this year they will
submit a recommendation to the Town for more school space by September 1966 if we are
to continue to adequately house and to properly educate our students. We commend
this Committee headed by Mr. William Steele, for their unselfish willingness to
give of themselves and for the fine work they have accomplished. Careful value

judgments and right decisions are of extreme importance in this area if our educa-
tional effectiveness is to continue to improve.
Last year it was reported to you that the School Committee had adopted a new
Budget and Accounting format based upon the recommendation of the Federal Government.
Although this was initiated not without some problems, it has nonetheless proven to

formed with the details of school costs. Such a change could not have been accom-

of the Finance Committee and the cooperation of Town Officials.

- BACKGROUND EXPLANATION OF 1964 INCREASE -
It is obvious to everyone, I am sure, that as school enrollments increase, the
cost of operation will of necessity continue to increase. The task of your School
Committee and Administration is to keep these costs within the limits of the Town's
ability to pay and yet insure the best of educational opportunities. Such a task is
difficult and is the subject of much soul-searching on the part of all who are
associated with schools.
A glance at comparative figures below will readily show that the budget increase
this year is $45,773. Later on in the financial section of this report, you will
find that salaries alone more than account for this total increase since they amount
to over $48,000. above the 1963 request. This amount is built-in in the salary
schedules which must remain competitive If we are to obtain the type of personnel
who will do the outstanding job required in the "front lines". It also represents
the need for additional teachers as enrollments reach the stage that more classes
must be established.
It would be logical to ask how it is possible to reflect a total budget increase

===PAGE 90===
which is approximately $3,000. less than the salary increase alone. The answer to
this was not simple but lay principally in the fact that the school transportation
was changed from single routes to double routes thereby reducing the number of re-
quired buses from 26 to 16.Had single routes continued, the cost would have increased
this budget request an additional $13,000. per year. At the time of implementing
this change, age and size restrictions were placed on the buses.
Savings such as this along with purchase of a school truck through Government
surplus for less than $50. have allowed more funds to be directed toward necessary
improvement in our educational needs and yet substantially decrease the amount of
the overall budget increase considerably below that of the previous two years as the
table of comparative figures below will show.

- ELEMENTARY LEVEL -
Basic to the educational effectiveness of any school system has been and will
continue to be contingent upon how effectively we teach the "three R's". Last year's

primary level this year by (1) beginning formal reading for pupils early in their
first year after giving a series of diagnostic tests to all pupils thereby eliminating
the need for a readiness program for those who show no need for it. (2) Remedial
work by the personnel in the reading department, in addition to help being received
in the classroom, has provided more valid diagnosis and assistance in individual
problems in reading. The earlier remedial problems are diagnosed and acted upon ,
the more rapid is the reading progress of the child.

- CHALLENGES AHEAD -- REGIONAL TRADE SCHOOL -
At the outset of this report mention was made that there was little place in
the world today for the untrained. These facts become more emphatic as the years
go by. In Lunenburg, we are somewhat exceptional in that we have about 57% of our
graduates going on to some type of advanced schooling. Even then, we are left with
almost five out of every ten graduates going out on the employment market, some of
whom are poorly equipped to compete for employment. Those who have pursued the
Business Course are the exception to this rule in a comprehensive academic high
school.
We have a greater obligation both to the individual and to society to better
meet the needs of these young people than we are now able to do in a comprehensive
school. We are not, nor can we hope to be a vocational school although we are en-
deavoring, as has been previously stated, to improve our Business Department and
Electronics facilities in an effort to improve our services to young peoples needs.
Among the forty plus % who terminate their education with High School, there
can be those who either by ability disinterest or whatever, simply cannot succeed in
the academic high school. Some of these become disciplinary problems as the result
of their disinterest and thereby inhibit the progress of those who can benefit from
such a program.
Meeting the needs of these numbers of boys and girls will make them the trained
people needed for today's society and will enable the others to move more rapidly.
In America we talk of universal education for all our youth. The way to achieve
this goal more fully is through encouraging the development of the Regional Trade
School movement.

During the year, Mrs. Lahti and Miss Schultz are on duty at the opening of school daily to
check on children who have been absent due to illness for a period of three days or longer and

Sabin Oral Polio Vaccine was administered in the schools as follows: TYPE I was offered in
January to students beyond Grade 2; 743 doses were given. TYPE II was offered in March; this was
the first time this type had been offered so all students were included; 1565 doses were given.
TYPE 111 was offered in May, again to students beyond Grade 2; 1265 doses were given. This pro-
gram is completed and in the future children who enter school should have received this protection
during infancy either at clinics conducted by the Board of Health or at their physicians.
Booster shots for diphtheria and tetanus were offered to students in Grades 6 and 11, and
smallpox vaccinations were offered to students in Grade 11. 193 diphtheria tetanus boosters were
given and 65 smallpox vaccinations were done.
The dental fluorine program was started in November and is'still in progress. Miss June
Duteau, Dental Hygienist for the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health is in charge again this year.
Students in Grades 2, 5, and 8 participate in this program.
The P-TA sponsored a dental education program during Dental Health Week in February. Talks
and demonstrations were given in Grades I, 3, and 4, by Mrs. Clarence May, Dental Hygienist.
The annual vision and hearing tests have been completed and notices of defects sent home
along with a form to be completed by the doctor at the time he makes the examination. Many of
these forms have already come back indicating that the children are receiving care. Financial
assistance has been obtained in several instances to help defray the cost of glasses.
Physical examinations are made routinely involving Grades I, 4, 8 and 10 by Dr. DeLeo, School
Physician. All students who participate in competitive sports have annual physical examinations.
In closing, I wish to express my appreciation to the volunteer workers, especially the mem-
bers of the Future Nurses Club and the P-TA groups who assisted at the polio clinics and also to
the administration, the teaching staff and the school personnel for their continuing cooperation.

School Lunch Program
PHYLLIS W. PROUTY, Supervisor
This is my First Annual Report of the Lurenburg School Lunch Program and is
for the school year, September 1962 to September 1963.
There were 173 lunch days for the Elementary School and 174 for the Junior-
Senior High School. During this period we fed 175,107 "Type A" lunches to students,
5,348 of which were "free" lunches to the needy; 10,998 meals were served to adults,
including the cafeteria personnel. Apart from these lunches, 141,223 half-pints of
milk were sold to students under the United States Department of Agriculture Special
Milk Program; 4,739 half-pints were sold to adults.
We were fortunate in receiving $13,537.75 worth of donated commodities during
the year. In addition to regular monthly items (butter, cheese, dried egg powder,
dried milk powder, lard, flour, dried beans, corn meal and rice), we received canned
pumpkin, sauerkraut, cherries, peanut butter, peas, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, peaches,
plums, pork, tomato paste, applesauce, pineapple and corn, plus fresh pears and
cranberries, as well as frozen chicken pieces, ground beef, turkeys and dried raisins.
These are available different months and the amount we receive is based upon the
number of students fed during the month.
Our school lunch participation for the year was 78% according to attendance and
76% according to membership in the Junior-Senior High School. In the Elementary
School, it was 65% according to attendance and 60% according to membership.
Mrs. Rita Seguin is now the new Manager in the Elementary School, with Mrs.
Hazel Whitney as Head Cook, Mrs. Helen Hebert as Assistant Cook, and Mrs. Bertha
Geigner as Baker. The General Workers are Mrs. Genevieve Nass, Mrs. Annette Thibo-
deau and Mrs. Claire Connolly.
Personnel in the Junior-Senior High School consists of Mrs. Lucy Chartrand as
Head Cook and Mrs. Emelian Perry as the Baker. The General Workers are Mrs. Edna
Huff, Mrs. Mary Thibault, Mrs. Ethel Nolan, Mrs. Gertrude Solovei, Mrs. Dorothy
Schlichter and Mrs. Gloria Beaulac
We have had a fine staff in the past and even though some of these ladies are
new I am certain we will all work well together.
Financially, the program opened with a balance of $4,051.16 in September, 1962.
After all bills were paid for the year there was a balance of $4,046.31 for the
opening of school in September 1963.
At the Thanksgiving dinner this past November 26th, 1963, we served 1,069
lunches to students and personnel in the Junior-Senior High School and 564 lunches
to students and personnel at the Elementary School. At our Christmas dinner on
December 19th, 1963, we served 1,100 students and personnel at the Junior-Senior High
School and 602 students and personnel at the Elementary School. On both occasions,
the students from the Elementary Annex came to the Junior-Senior High School for
their holiday meal.

The convention for all Worcester County School Lunch personnel was held at the
Narragansett Regional High School in Templeton on November 1st, 1963. This meeting
is in conjunction with the Teachers' Convention. Most of the school lunch personnel
from Lunenburg were in attendance.
In closing, I wish to express my thanks to Mr. Brown, who has been of so much
help to me, and to the School Committee, the principals and faculties, the town
personnel, and to Mr. Archambault and his custodians who help us in so many emer-
gencies, and to my personnel who work so hard and try in every way to make our lunch
program a success.
- LUNCHROOM SUPERVISOR RESIGNS -
At the close of school in June of 1963, Mrs. Ruth Correll, Supervisor of the
School Lunch Program in the Lunenburg Public Schools, resigned to accept a similar
position in the Public Schools of Amherst, Mass., which is located somewhat closer
to her home-town.
Mrs. Correll had grown with the School Lunch Program in Lunenburg and had
worked conscientiously, efficiently and tirelessly to bring it to its present peak
of efficiency. We are grateful for the years of faithful service Mrs. Correll has
given to the Town of Lunenburg--our best wishes for a happy future.
During the summer of 1963, she was succeeded by Mrs. Phyllis Prouty who as
head of the Elementary Lunch Program, had for a number of years worked closely with
Mrs. Correll. We feel especially grateful for having a person among our School
Lunch personnel, who was qualified and prepared to assume this position, thereby
making the transition in leadership extremely smooth and efficient.
