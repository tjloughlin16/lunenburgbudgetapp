SELECTMEN
Carlton T. Ellms, Chairman
David A. Eyles
Howard A. Hamel
The year 1959 proved to be a busy and difficult one. Due to the un-
precedented tie vote for the office of Selectman, the recount, and then a
special election to fill the vacancy, your Board was without its full mem-
bership until the middle of May. Even mother nature conspired against us,
bringing one of the rainiest years on record, thereby seriously hampering
our regular highway program and worsening our surface water drainage
problems. To the uninformed, the subject of water drainage and related
problems may appear relatively unimportant. As amatter of fact, we think
it is one of our more serious problems. The inadequacy of many of our
sewage disposal systems is but one of the serious related problems. As most
of US realize, Lunenburg does not have a public drainage or sewerage sys-
tem. For years we have relied on open side drainage in maintaining most
of our streets and highways. New developments have resulted in dumping
more surface water on our streets. There is a rising clamor from all sides
calling for the elimination of open ditches along highways and streets.
Everyone with a drainage ditch through his land demands that it be piped
and filled. In most such cases this means a highway project of major pro-
portions involving the expenditure of thousands of dollars. There is a nat-
ural reluctance on the part of the land owners to give drainage easements
to the Town without compensation therefor, and even with cooperation, it
is often times difficult to provide disposal areas. The problem becomes
progressively worse each year and therefore, we deem it prudent to call it
to your attention.
The Lakefront project was completed in 1959, thereby eliminating a
traffic hazard of long standing. The Main Street widening was also com-
pleted in 1959. We had hoped to be well into the Reservoir Road project
before the end of the year. In the interest of saving dollars, a concerted drive was made by the
Board to obtain necessary easements from abutters without cost to the Town. We were successful in
all but one case. We are in hopes that this individual will relent and agree to grant the easement.
If this can be accomplished the savings to the taxpayers will more than balance out any inconven-
ience occasioned by the delay. Improved street lighting, adoption of a plumbing code, and a
stepped up program of safety and health inspections were carried out during 1959 in an endeavor to
preserve and promote the health and welfare, of our citizens. Whalom and Leominster Road side-
walk projects will be completed in the spring.
Our remarks here have been general, as indeed they must. However, there is a wealth of in-
formation between the covers of this Town Report, which was painstakingly gathered and put to-
gether. If you are interested in specific information on any particular department, may we respect-
fully refer you to the reports of the departments found elsewhere in this book. We extend our thanks
to all who have in any way aided US in carrying out the duties and responsibilities of our office.

LEGAL
Robert J. Larkin, Town Counsel
In our report for 1958, we commented on the fact that two law suits
were pending at the close of that year. Although both cases were disposed
of in 1959, four additional suits were commenced. Two of these involve
claims for land damages arising out of takings for highway purposes, one
other is for damages to a motor vehicle allegedly due to a street defect,
and the remaining suit is for personal injuries arising out of a school bus
accident. Two or three other matters currently in contention have boiled over from 1959 and may
ripen into law suits during 1960.
A "busier than usual" period followed on the heels of the tie vote for the office of Selectman in
the annual election of town officers. Due to the intensity of the campaign and the importance of
the office, the law pertaining to the conduct of elections and recounts, the marking and counting
of ballots and the duties and responsibilities of those in charge of elections and recounts was
thoroughly reviewed and explained for the benefit of all concerned.
The town counsel attended most of the meetings of the Board of Selectmen, and gave advice,
rulings and opinions to the various other departments when requested.

ASSESSORS
Paul E. Wickham, Chairman
Annie C. McNally
Chester J. Pliska
This year, as in the past, we call your attention to the increased num-
ber of persons assessed Real Estate and Poll taxes. Persons assessed Real
Estate total 2331, representing an increase of 29 over the previous year
and a total increase of 344 over a five year period. The 1758 Poll taxes
assessed represent an increase of 78 over 1958 and a total increase of 152
in five years. These facts, while mildly interesting as statistics, have a
greater significance in Lunenburg's overall picture. The increased number
of real estate assessments is, to a great extent, caused by the breaking up
of large parcels of land into lots for homes. During the past year subdivis-
ion has commenced on three of our large farms. The fact that farms are
fast disappearing must surely influence our thinking and planning for the
future.
A decrease in the revenue received from personal property assessed
has been mainly brought about by the following:
1. Several firms have become incorporated and are subject to State
tax rather than local. Although State Corporation taxes are
distributed to cities and towns on a formula basis, the Town's
share is far greater when paid directly.
2. Personal Property taxes have been replaced by farm animal ex-
cise on all animals and poultry belonging to persons who are
actually earning their living by farming. This excise rate is
established by the State and is considerably lower than the per-
sonal property rate.
Motor Vehicle Excise, which is a great source of revenue to the Town, has, in the past, in-
creased appreciably from year .) year. We had hoped that 1959 would again show an appreciable
increase but, unfortunately, this did not come to pass and whereas in 1958 $102,262.49 was com-
mitted for collection the comparable figure for 1959 was only $96,341.72. As excise cannot be
processed until information is received from the State, and because a change in clerical procedure
delayed the processing of cards by the State, many 1959 excise will not be received by the Assess-
ors or the taxpayer until 1960. This means that next year it will be necessary for the Assessors'
office to explain the confusion which will, in all probability, arise as a result of 1959 and 1960
bills being received in the same year. As you can see, the Assessors have no control over this sit-
uation except to assure you that when cards come from the State the excise warrant will be sent to
the Tax Collector as soon as possible. This delay in 1959 also has a bearing on the 1960 tax struc-
ture as the item "estimated receipts" used in computing the tax rate can include only the amount re-
ceived in 1959. In view of the fact that additional information was requested on 1960 registration
blanks to expedite the processing of cards at the State Level the Assessors feel that in 1960 excise
will be sent out earlier.

Real Estate transfers continue to increase. The maps which were started in 1954 have been kept
up to date and have proven to be a valuable source of information. We encourage use of these maps,
which are located in the Assessors' office, by property owners, prospective owners, public service
companies and others who need information regarding the location of property in Lunenburg.
A question frequently asked is, "How much will my valuation be increased if I landscape my lot,
or paint my house?" The answer is that no increase is ever made for these reasons. The Assessors
feel that a well-kept house and grounds is an asset to the Community and should be encouraged.

As many of the duties of a Town Clerk pertain to elections and registrations, there was consid-
erable activity in the "Town Office" during the first five months of the year brought about by the
tie vote for Selectman and the resulting recount and special election. Details of officers elected
and votes taken at a special election in January, the annual election and town meeting in March
and the special election on May 16, may be found on pages 55 through 61 of this report.
An unusual number of acts pertaining to the functions of this office were passed by the State
Legislature during 1959. Laws relative to the recording of vital statistics, filing in connection
with subdivisions, and various and sundry unrelated duties and responsibilities of Town Clerks were
adopted and amended and the following new provisions of the Election Laws were enacted and are
now in effect:
Chapter 70 - Provides that envelopes containing absent voting ballots shall be processed by
the Warden as soon as the ballots cast have been removed from the ballot box.
Chapter 74 - Authorizes a voter to establish change or cancel party enrollment by forward-
ing to the Registrars of Voters a notarized certificate requesting same.
Chapter 127 - Provides for bi-partisan Boards of Registrars of Voters in all cities and towns
in the Commonwealth.
Chapter 133 - Provides for bi-partisan count of ballots at State elections in towns not di-
vided into voting precincts.
Chapter 137 - Provides that street lists shall designate persons who are registered voters.
Chapter 155 - Provides for a complete count of all ballots used or unused in elections and
recounts.

POLICE
Sherwood G. Winchester, Chief
Frederick B. Hobbs, Sergeant
Raymond P. Whitney, Patrolman
William R. Sullivan, Patrolman
As our population expands, the demands upon the Police Department be-
come greater. Increases in the number of criminal cases, complaints of
malicious damage, larcenies, assaults, vandalism and juvenile problems dur-
ing 1959 give testimony to Lunenburg's continuing growth.
Our State Motor Vehicle Department reports an ever increasing number of vehicles on the high-
ways. In order to protect local and transient motorists against speeding, drunken driving and the
many other highway violations which threaten the public safety Chief Winchester strongly recommend
that additional funds be allocated in 1960 for increased motor vehicle patrol work. Included in 125
cases prosecuted in the Fitchburg District Court by this department were 221 arrests for motor vehi-
cle violations.
This year's bicycle safety program, conducted with the cooperation of the Parent Teachers' Asso-
ciation and school authorities, once more proved successful. The eagerness of the children to com-
ply with safety regulations and to use safety equipment was ample reward for those adults who
assisted with this program. Additional signs were painted this year to safeguard the children who
use the playground areas.
This report would not be complete if we failed to acknowledge the invaluable assistance rendered
by other Town, City and State Police Departments in solving crimes and apprehending offenders.
The success of any police department today is dependent on its ability to cooperate with other law
enforcement agencies.

FIRE
Roger G. Bigelow, Chief
During the year the department responded to 94 alarms: 20 house or
building, 44 grass or brush, 5 chimney, 3 electrical appliances, 2 oil
stoves, 5 dump, 5 automobile, 1 resuscitator, 6 automobile accidents and
3 false alarms. We responded to only one brush fire this fall which is quite
unusual. Six hundred and eighty-two "burning" permits were issued for the
year.
Eight of the building fires were of a serious nature. The "Levin Farm" barn, which burned on
the night of July 10th, was the most spectacular fire of the year. All property which was located
in buildings and destroyed by fire was covered by insurance.
On two occasions Lunenburg called on the Fitchburg Fire Department for assistance under our
Mutual Aid Program. Our department deeply appreciates all the aid given US during 1959.
- 37 -
===PAGE 40===
Twenty-three members of our department attended a fire school sponsored by the State Depart-
ment of Education, which was conducted in Fitchburg, with officers of the Fitchburg Department
serving as instructors. This twenty hour course was designed to acquaint the towns in our Mid-State
Fire Mutual Aid Association with modern procedures and should promote even greater cooperation
between the twenty-six Towns which make up that Association.
Early last spring the Selectmen called for a survey by the Insurance Rating Board in an effort to
obtain a lower insurance rate for Town property. Although the survey was completed on April 1st,
to date no report of the recommendations has been received.
To comply with the requirements of the Federal Communications Commission our present radio
frequency must be changed as Police and Fire Departments cannot be on the same frequency unless
a Local Government frequency is utilized. Such a frequency has been obtained and we are now
waiting for matching funds from Civil Defense to make the change over. As other town departments
may also use the new set up, it is expected that the Highway Department will install radios in some
of its trucks in the very near future.
Fire apparatus is in good repair. In 1961 Chief Roger Bigelow plans to ask for a new 750 gallon
pumper to replace the Mack which was purchased in 1935.

TREE WARDEN
Richard E. Brown, Tree Warden
During the past year, extensive tree work was carried on throughout
the Town, involving, for the most part, the elimination of dead trees and
the removal of dead and dangerous branches. A total of seventy-six trees
were cut and cleared away. Ninety-five trees were purchased and planted
along the roadsides to replace trees eliminated by highway construction
projects. Mr. Brown plans to continue this program in 1960 by planting a
similar number of trees.
An article has been submitted for insertion in the 1960 warrant which requests the purchase of a
two man chainsaw for use by this department. The estimated cost of the saw is $515.00.
Control of Public Nuisances
Richard E. Brown, Superintendent
During 1959 only nineteen samples were taken from
elm trees and sent to the University of Massachusetts to
determine whether or not the trees were infected with
Dutch Elm Disease. This compares with one hundred and
sixteen samples taken the previous year. This decrease
may be attributed to failure on the part of the Finance
Committee to recommend and of the voters to appropriate
an additional $400.00 which was requested by the Moth
Superintendent. Although in 1959 Lunenburg's legal
liability, as determined by the State, was $1,802.51, it
is on the basis of additional appropriations, above and be-
yond our legal liability, that any assistance can be given
by the Department of Natural Resources.
White pines were sprayed in an effort to control aphids.
Lack of funds prevented spraying for control of the gypsy
moth, the elm beetle, and the oak tree weevil. Mr. Brown still feels that it would pay the Town
to purchase its own sprayer and do its own spraying.
Although Lunenburg's financial liability for 1960 is still only $1,802.51, the District Super-
visor from the Department of Natural Resources has recommended that $3,000.00 be appropriated
for carrying out next year's program. The Moth Superintendent has submitted a request for this
amount.

DOG OFFICER
Elwood C. Erickson, Dog Officer
The owner or keeper of a dog three months old or older must license it
annually, on or before March 31, and shall cause it to wear a collar or
harness with dog tag attached. If the tag is lost a substitute tag must be
secured from the Town Clerk.
Any dogs acquired after April 1st must also be licensed as well as those
which become three months of age after that date.
The fee for every dog license is $2.00 for a male and $5.00 for a female. If a female dog has
been spayed it can be licensed for $2.00 providing a proper certificate from a registered veterinar-

TOWN FOREST
Richard C. Hayes, Chairman
During the past year a culvert was installed in the road leading through
the large town forest. This culvert should prevent washouts and consequent
expensive repairs to the road. Also several pieces of woodland were sur-
veyed to establish boundaries which had been rather obscure.
In conjunction with the Lunenburg Planning Board a survey of the prin-
cipal wetlands or swamps was made, and it is earnestly hoped and strongly recommended that the
Town obtain effective control over much of this area either by gift from present owners, purchase at
assessed valuation or by easement, whereby the Town may be protected against the possibility of these
swampy areas being abused or destroyed. A number of towns and cities have employed the latter
method wherein the land owners retain control of the land for all normal uses but surrender certain
rights to the town.
The wetlands problem has become urgent with the explosion of population and movement from
city to town or suburb and has been the subject of a great deal of recent publicity in newspapers,
and conservation, sporting and nature publications as well as the large national magazines.
There are several ways in which this may vitally effect our Town and its taxpayers. For instance
waterfront property owners have or should have concern about what happens to our swamps which
function as control valves helping to maintain an even flow of water throughout the year. Complete
drainage of all swamps would reduce most streams to mere drainage ditches which would flow only
after a storm with the possibility that the level of ponds and lakes could be so lowered as to render
them useless.
Another by-product of swamp drainage would be a lowered water table with consequent drying
up of wells. Most experts agree that in the years to come water will be a very critical commodity.
It already is in many sections of our country.
Sewerage is unfortunately another problem which arises when unthinking or unscrupulous real
estate promoters take over and "develop" swampy areas. A continued trend along this line could
necessitate the construction of a sewerage disposal system for our Town. The effect on the tax rate
would be staggering.
Swamps are the last stronghold of many forms of wildlife and are, of course, the only place where
many beautiful and rare species of birds, animals, trees and flowers can be found. Once a swamp
is drained the wildlife is gone forever. Swamps also act as a retreat or sanctuary for the surround-
ing areas to which wildlife may go for protection or escape. While the wildlife may be of more
immediate interest to sportsmen and nature lovers, we will all be poorer if we lose it.

WELFARE
Thelma LeBlanc, Director
The Board of Public Welfare administers programs of financial assistance
and services to meet basic welfare needs. It is the aim of the department
to make these programs positive and constructive. The program objectives
are focused on the individual and his family. Most people, whether they
are the aged, the disabled, or children, wish to remain in their own home
and in the community into which they have sunk roots. Public Assistance
contributes to the stability of the family by providing financial assistance, making it possible for
the needy, aged person, disabled person or dependent child to be maintained in his own home with-
out interruption of family life. The provision of financial aid was one of the first and has remained
one of the most important factors in keeping homes intact, rather than to place children in foster
homes. It helps keep parent and child together and provides the opportunity for needy children to
grow up in the setting of their own family relationships.
As illness is one of the greatest dangers to family security and stability and one of the principal
sources of unemployment, dependency and unemployability, the problem of maintaining health is of
particular importance in all of the programs. Although surveys have shown that over one-half of
the people over 65 regard themselves in fairly good health, it must not be assumed that they do not
need medical services to remain that way. Long term care for the aged continues to be à problem.
Hospital costs have increased more than any other medical expenditures and hospitals are used more
frequently. New medicines and wonder drugs have added to the costs. As the cost of living in-
creases and people require more money for day to day expenditures, they will be less able to save
money for hospitalization. One-half of the population has some kind of a health insurance though
most of it is so limited that only a fraction of family medical costs are included. Insurance plans
have only recently been made available to those over 65 years of age. Tax supported hospitaliza-
tion will probably be necessary for many people for long term illness. Per diem hospital costs now
run larger than the average daily income of more than half of our population. The Massachusetts
Department of Public Welfare has set up a Medical Plan whereby specified fees are paid according
to the type of service rendered.
Planning for a more permanent system of assistance to prevent destitution, culminated in the
Passage in 1935 of the Social Security Act which provides for federal grants-in-aid to states for
public assistance to the needy. Under the Social Security Act the Federal Government shares in the
expenses of four major types of assistance, Old Age, Aid to the Blind, Aid to Dependent Children
and Aid to the permanently and totally Disabled. Because public assistance is granted only to in-
dividuals who are in economic need, this must be established through a means test.
In general Old Age Assistance makes it possible for aged persons to go on living in their own
homes either alone or with their families and to receive regular monthly cash allowances for their
maintenance. Medical payments may also be made to the vendor in their behalf. The Old Age
Assistance case load for 1959 shows a slight decrease in the number of recipients and a decrease in
the amount of regular cash grants. However, these decreases are off-set by the increased number
of persons who need assistance to cover medical care.
- 46 -
===PAGE 49===
Needy children have always made up a large percentage of the poor. The Social Security Act's
definition of a dependent child is "A child under 18 years of age who has been deprived of parental
support or care by reason of death, continued absence from the home, or physical or mental incapac-
ity of the parent, and who is living with a relative." Aid to Dependent Children is not the only
social security program to help needy children. An increasing number of children receive support
under Old Age and Survivors' Insurance and some under Workmen's Compensation laws. However
the Aid to Dependent Children program supports many children in their own home or the home of a
relative.
The program for the Disabled, provides cash payments and medical care to adult, invalid per-
sons under sixty-five. Included in this category are persons who suffer from physical or mental
impairment, disability, or loss of limb that substantially prevents them from engaging in useful occu-
pations within their competence. The attention of federal and state agencies is being focused on
coordination of social, medical, vocational and rehabilitation services for the disabled.
General Relief is a program for the needy who are not eligible for one of the aforementioned
public assistance programs. 1959 has been a very unusual year as very few people found it necess-
ary to apply for emergency aid. Since general relief is an emergency program, most assistance is
"aid-in-kind" and medical payments made in a recipient's behalf.

North Cemetery, started in 1958, was completed and lots laid out with bound stones. There are now

with gold leaf lettering and black backgrounds were purchased and will be erected at the front of
each cemetery.
The Cemetery Building on Holman Street has taken on a "new look". A coat of paint was applied
to the outside and to the interior of the office and tool room. New shingles were placed on the en-
tire roof. The roof of the garage connected with this building was repaired and a coat of weather-
proofing was applied. A cement floor was laid in the garage. A neat sign reading "Lunenburg
Cemetery Department" has been placed on the front of the building. The grounds surrounding the
building and garage were cleaned up and the trees and shrubs were trimmed and thinned out. The
circular drive in front was relocated and improved.
In 1960 your Cemetery Commission plans to continue making improvements at both cemeteries.
Improvement of the roads will be one of the main projects as the Commission plans to grade, fill
and oil as many roads as possible. The long range plan is to improve all sections of the cemeteries
by levelling, grading and reseeding so that the areas in both cemeteries will follow a natural con-
tour. It is planned to improve as many sections as possible in 1960.
There are no permanent records of the burials in the Old Sections in either cemetery. The Cem-
etery Commission intends to make as complete records as possible of these burials from the names on
existing stones or markers and from any historical data that is available. There has been no map or
blueprint made of the cemeteries since 1927. With the completion of the Grave Survey early in
1960 the Cemetery Commission plans to make a drawing or map of both cemeteries which will re-
flect an up-to-date record of present lot owners and show the new lots available.
As it would seem fitting and proper to perpetuate the memory of Lunenburg's first settler, an
appropriate marker will be placed at the grave of Samuel Page in the South Cemetery.

Throughout 1959 the many 4-H'ers of Lunenburg busied themselves with various projects, including: clothing,
cooking, dairy, poultry and garden clubs. Important achievements were recognized at monthly rallies where
4-H'ers also had an opportunity to socialize with games, songs and refreshments. Three or four clubs set up window
displays during National 4-H week, for which the Stitch Wits received top award in the Clothing Division.
In May, Worcester played host to 4-H Clubs from towns throughout the County on Demonstration Day and for the
Speaking Contest. During June, older members attended the Junior Leaders Conference at the University of Massa-
chusetts, in fellowship with 500 boys and girls from other communities in the State. In September, 4-H club officers
representing all town clubs were elected as follows: President - Sheron Stillman; Vice-President - Elton Chartrand;
Secretary - Doris Geigner. In October Mr. Marshall and one member of his staff presented pin and seal awards for
project completions, and the annual banquet was held in November.
On Achievement Day, Home Economics projects were judged by County Agents and Junior girls receiving Ex-
cellent awards went on to compete at Junior Girls' Day in Spencer while Senior girls attended the County Dress Re-
view and Project Exhibit in Worcester. Lunenburg can be justly proud of the awards received by members who ex-
hibited at the Sturbridge Youth Fair, the 4-H Fair in Spencer, our own Community Fair, other local fairs, and
recognition received at the Medal Winners Banquet. Following are the awards received by Lunenburg 4-H members

HOME DEPARTMENT
A meeting of the Town committee was held at the home of Mrs. George Gordon and plans for the year were made.
A "How not to go broke" meeting was held with many homemakers in attendance. Mrs. Gordon and Mrs. Egide
Mathieu attended a leaders' meeting in Worcester on "Sparing Calories". Twelve women participated in a luncheon
meeting held at the Parish House.
In May the local committee was host to a group of Leominster women who gave a demonstration on "Mending
Techniques" at the Whalom Recreation Building. Officers attended an area meeting at Sterling in June and the
annual Field Day in July. For four weeks Mrs. Ralph Farr and Mrs. Russell Travis attended a leader meeting on
"Furniture Refinishing" in Clinton and, in turn, held meetings at Whalom with twelve interested homemakers. In
- 54 -
===PAGE 57===
September, several members went to Leominster and learned to make reed stools which could be used for holiday
gifts. A "Table Setting" workshop meeting was held at the Parish House, under the direction of Miss Alice Bair of
the Worcester County Extension Service, with five neighboring towns as guests. Mrs. Mathieu and Mrs. Travis
served as leaders and later held a meeting in Whalom with homemakers participating.
Early in November a large group of women met at the Whalom Recreation Building and formed an Extension
Group. This group takes the place of the committee which has functioned for so many years and will meet once a
month or more often. Officers elected were as follows: Mrs. Egide Mathieu, Chairman; Mrs. George Gordon, Co-
chairman; Mrs. Robert Garneau, Secretary-Treasurer; Mrs. Arthur Popp, Publicity Director; Mrs. Robert Logan,
Advisory Council Representative.
The Annual Christmas Open House was held in Worcester on December first, and Christmas cooking, wrappings,
and many demonstrations were shown at a program presented in the afternoon. Lunenburg and Leominster women en-
joyed planning and decorating a tree with over one hundred articles, no two being alike. This was enthusiastically
received. A "Santa Jack-in-the-box", made by Mrs. Edgar Letarte and her committee, was entered in the mantle
decoration competition. Several members were hostesses during the day and contributions to the "Mildred Thomas
Scholarship Fund" were made. A workshop on "Consumer Facts and Frauds" was held in Whalom with Miss Bair as
leader.
The group plans to participate in many interesting activities during the rest of the season and anyone interested
in homemaking is welcome to join. The time and place of the meetings will be noted in the local paper.

At a legal meeting of the Freeholders and other inhabitants of the Town of Lunenburg, assembled, May ye 21st A.D. 1759 voted
and chose Mr. John Heywood, Moderator.
Voted and accepted Mr. John Wyman to serve as Constable in the room of Capt. Samuel Hunt, he being hired by said Hunt and
May ye 21st was sworn as the law directs.
Voted and chose Benj. Goodridge Collector to gather the half penny acre rate, who has since been sworn as the law directs.
Voted that:the Town will make the fence on the South Side of the way, from the way near the meeting house, between the way
and Capt. Hutchen's land till it comes to Mr. Daniel Austin's land.
Voted and granted to Mr. Daniel Holt eight shillings old tenor pr rod, for building said fence, provided he makes it good, and
finishes it within four months.
Voted that the place for building a School-house, near Mr. Stickney's be moved near the Norwest Corner-of Mr. Isaac Redding-
ton's land.
Voted that the town will new build the bridge over Mulpus brook, near Mr. Bellow's mill.
Voted and granted six pound to rebuild said bridge withall, and that Mr. Thomas Peabody take care that so bridge be built.
Voted that Swine run at large the present year.

During the past year a Lunenburg landmark dis-
appeared. The old blacksmith shop built around 1819-
now replaced by a modern service station on Massa-
Emisson. Will Kilburn Tom archibald: Harrigan George Litchlin
chusetts Avenue - has had an eloquent history. The
workshop of many "smithies" for almost one hundred
years, it was the birthplace of farming implements
prior to the Civil War, and continued to cater to hor-
ses, wagons, and farm equipment right up to the first
World War in 1918.
One of the blacksmiths - from 1863 to 1873 - was
Moses Mclntyre, father of S. Ulmer McIntyre who for
many years was custodian of the center schools, the
Library and the Congregational Church.
Thomas A. Archibald operated the shop in 1884,
and in 1889 when the Boulder on Rollstone Hill in
Fitchburg began to show severe cracking, he was called
upon to make an iron band, which was fastened around
the boulder under the direction of Franklin S. Francis,
the great-grandfather of Edith K. Proctor, Margaret
Harley, and Kimball Francis.
The Blacksmith Shop finally closed its doors in
1918 when Eben Mead remodelled it into a garage to
service automobiles. Here he conducted a successful
business until his death in 1945. The Jenney Manu-

Report of Superintendent of Schools
for the Year 1959
I herewith submit my Tenth Annual Report (Sixth since with-
drawal from School Union, Ashby-Lunenburg-Townsend) as Superinten-
dent of Schools
For the past decade, most of our annual reports have indicated
that the school enrollment has substantially increased and many
have stated that we are cramped for space in all school buildings.
This year is no exception, except to state that if construction is
not begun very soon we will find it necessary to use substandard
space and/or revert to the two platoon system.
The orderly growth of a school system should never be in conflict with educa-
tional efficiency. Toward this end, reasonable planning should provide the necessary
administrative and supervisory personnel.
It will be a problem to accommodate our children next September in either the
Elementary or Secondary School areas. Many of our classrooms in the Elementary Annex
(not originally built for elementary use) are over-crowded at the present time. Cer-
tainly it will be a real problem to house the anticipated increase of approximately
one hundred pupils next September, but, by September 1961, there will be no housing
for many youngsters who thought they had a birthright for schooling. I feel that
many citizens do not know that presently we are using several areas not acceptable
for classroom uses,--for example, a teachers' room, a cloakroom, a locker room, a
custodial storage room. Even if these areas were satisfactory, they subtract from
services which in the recent past were thought to be minimum necessities. School
personnel should not be too strongly criticized for cooperating wholeheartedly under
adverse conditions with the administration and school committee until such time we
again have adequate facilities in which to do a better than average job in the educa-
tion of the whole child, -your child.
It is a long story to present the evaluated cost to your children because of
prolonged periods of platoon schooling and the long road back to better-than-average
(50 percentile) scholarship. These evaluations made under extreme and opposite con-
ditions will gladly be explained to you by appointment with the school principals or
guidance department. For many years, while on platoon shifts and for several years
thereafter, we could not get far from the 50th percentile but with adequate facilities
we now usually find group averages scoring from 80 to above the 90th percentile. May
I refer to recent years town reports which cautioned that immediate steps to provide
adequate school facilities should be considered by the Lunenburg citizens.
Consider the total school report for 1959. I would like you to carefully study
and analyse each individual report in its own right, but particularly I call your
attention to the reports of the Elementary and Secondary principals. There is much
in each that should be analysed for projection into the future--toward the better ed-
ucation for your child, or children.
May I here repeat from the 1958 report, "Construction is costly but education
with inadequate facilities is also costly, to your children especially. The tax-
payer and parent must do the deciding."

BUDGET
Our school population increase is reflected in our budget. There is little in-
crease otherwise that is not mainly reflected by the rising costs of supplies and
operation, and by normal salary increases. Please refer to the statistics of expen-
ditures and reimbursements, You will note that approximately one-third of the educa-
tional costs are reimbursed by the State and Federal governments. Our per pupil costs
are comparably very satisfactory when measured by communities of equal size and val-
uation.
REPAIRS
Most of the repairs during the past year were those of a continuing necessary
nature. Some major items that did cost more than anticipated were exterior painting,
classroom furniture repair, continued lighting corrections, installation of several
safety devices for doors and emergency roof repairs. With the addition to the staff
of a supervisor of custodians, repairs and maintenance plans will be made to have
this work coordinated and supervised in order that the building and grounds may be
kept in first-class condition at all times with a minimum of expense to the town.
Those concerned with Education are keenly aware of its cost and like every com-
modity, Lunenburg has felt the inflationary effects of this decade.
Appreciation is expressed to the principals, teachers, parents, and all citizens
for their cooperation during these difficult days for decisions relative to the many
problems. The Lunenburg School Committee, I'm sure, join with me in looking to a
bright future for the Lunenburg School System to help us bear with the difficulties
of today.
Respectfully submitted,
MAURICE P. BILLINGS
Superintendent of Schools

Report of Lunenburg Junior-Senior
High School for the Year 1959
It is with pleasure that I herewith submit my Third Annual
Report as Principal of the Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School. The
administration, faculty and student body take great pride in our
school and its accomplishments; because we firmly believe that ours
is a good school. However, we are not content to rest on the lau-
rels of past or present accomplishment; but we are attempting to
remain ever alert to modification which will enable us to make even
more effective use of our excellent educational facilities.
The citizens of Lunenburg can well be proud of the educational facilities that
long and dedicated planning has made possible. We look forward to the continued
growth of the Junior-Senior High School with confidence; because we know that the
citizens of Lunenburg want good schools which are equipped to furnish a sound educa-
tion for the youth of our town.
GROWTH
Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School continues its rapid growth. On October 1,
1959, 575 students were enrolled in Grades 7 through 12. It is estimated that about
630 students will register for school in September 1960. Thus, our school has grown
from an enrollment of 460 to the present 575 in a period of only three years.
Since there are only seven rooms in the Junior High School wing, long hours of
careful planning have been required to house the existing ten Junior High School
divisions. At present, it has been possible to borrow room space in the Senior High
School wing; but the "borrowing possibilities" will be nonexistent, as our enrollment
in the upper grades increases. In fact, the need for additional classrooms is most
evident, even now. Careful attention must be given to expansion of the Junior-Senior
High School. Any projected expansion will cost money; but the citizens of Lunenburg
have expressed their interest in a sound education for the youth of the community.
Thus, it is evident that the necessary additional space will be forthcoming.
There were 50 Seniors graduated from Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School in 1959
and of this number, 22 have gone on to higher education, 8 are in the armed forces,
10 are employed in various office jobs and 10 are engaged in general type employment.
The present Senior Class now numbers 80.
EVALUATION
Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School is a member of the New England Association
of Colleges and Secondary Schools. Membership in this accrediting organization is of
true educational value to our community. This membership gives us official recogni-
tion when our students seek admission to institutions of higher learning. This rec-
ognition enables the Principal to certify B average students for college acceptance.
In 1954, the New England Association initiated a program of evaluation of public
secondary schools. Members of the association believed that these evaluation activi-
would "up-grade and improve the entire program of public school education".

SCHOOL PROGRAM
Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School offers an excellent opportunity for the
Youth of Lunenburg to receive a good solid education. We are ever mindful of our
obligation to the community that we serve. We are sincerely dedicated to the task of
accepting each student as we find him and assisting him in making the most of his
talents and abilities.
We are all proud of our school; because we sincerely believe that ours is a
good school. Our curriculum offerings are under constant study; and the faculty is
ever mindful of the challenges facing educational efforts in the "age of space". We
who work with the youth of today face the future with vigor and confidence. The bus-
iness of education is certainly a serious business because our product is educated
youth. Lunenburg young people continue to make us proud of our product. We believe
that our curricular and extracurricular endeavors are making possible excellent ac-
complishments by those who study here.
Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School offers a wide variety of activities to meet
the interest areas of our students and to aid in the development of special talents.
A large percentage of our students take an active part in these activities.
The Driver-Training Program remains very active in our school. Under the direc-
tion of Mr. Leo Millea, our boys and girls have studied in the classroom and "behind
the wheel". Those completing the course have been divided into five student teams.
At the conclusion of this training, the students concerned will take a final road
test and receive a driver's license.
Once again, we offer our sincere thanks to the Leominster Motor Sales, and Mr.
David Goodell, for their cooperation in making a training car available to us. Mr.
Goodell's interest in our school and his desire to be of service to us have been most
sincerely appreciated.
GUIDANCE
We are proud of the consistent gains which we have made in our total guidance
efforts. Under the direction of Mrs. Margaret Harley, the Guidance Department has
provided successful career, academic and social guidance.
The guidance counseling system has been expanded and has been highly successful.
The Junior High School counseling activities have been coordinated by Mr. Allan
Williams of the Mathematics Department. Senior High counseling has been under the
direct supervision of the Guidance Director. Mr. J. William Leary is serving as the
College Counselor. In this capacity, Mr. Leary has been responsible for the counsel-
ing of all college-bound Seniors and the complete follow-up activities which must be
completed for college admissions. Mr. Leary is performing his duties in a most effec-
tive manner and his services have guaranteed excellent coverage of activities in-
volved in college admissions.
During this past year, we have made two modifications in our handling of school
records. These changes have been made with the cooperation of the Guidance Depart-
ment and enable a more efficient student accounting procedure. The purchase of a
photocopy machine has enabled us to save a great deal of time in our transcript work.
After having processed a master copy for each student, it is possible to photograph
this master and forward photocopies to those schools seeking student grades.

===PAGE 86===
In addition to this modification, we have translated our student grades into the
comparative Carnegie Units. This change is recommended by the National Secondary
Schools Principals' Association. Both of these changes have helped to streamline our
college admissions operation and should reflect well on our school, the community and
above all, on the young peoples' records that are forwarded for consideration.
Modern education demands a great deal from the Guidance Department; but the Lun-
enburg Junior-Senior High School has not been found deficient. Mrs. Harley has done
an excellent job in her capacity of Guidance Director; and her enthusiastic devotion
to duty has guaranteed excellent results.
PHYSICAL EDUCATION
Lunenburg Junior-Senior High School's Physical Education Program has greatly ex-
panded in the past few years. This expanded program has given us a well-rounded slate
of athletic activities, which has brought a great deal of recognition to our commun-
ity. However, we firmly believe that this program has been of even greater value;
because it develops attitudes and skills which will carry over into successful adult
life.
Both our boys' and girls' basketball teams were strong contenders for the Wachu-
sett League crowns for 1959. The boys' team lost to Ashby in a playoff game at Fitch-
burg Teachers' College and the girls were runners-up to Ashby in their race. The
baseball team won the League Championship for 1959. Both our boys' and girls' basket-
ball teams for 1959-1960 are strong contenders, once again. This year's girls' team
is coached by a new Head Coach, Mrs. Beverly Dunigan.
We believe that our teams are well schooled; and this schooling is reflected in
the quality of the individual and team play; our school and our community can well be
proud of the excellent manner in which these teams have represented us.
Our coaching staff is made up of Mrs. Beverly Dunigan, Girls' Varsity; Mr. Rich-
ard Mulligan, Boys' Varsity; Mr. Peter Grenier, Boys' Freshman and Junior Varsity and
Mr. Richard Garvey, Junior High School Boys'. The Junior High School girls are cur-
rently participating on an intramural basis. Mrs. Josephine Meloon is directing this
phase of the program. Mr. Floyd Barney is coaching Varsity Baseball and Mr. Grenier
is coaching the Junior High School Baseball. Mr. Mulligan is the track and Cross-
Country Coach and Mrs. Dunigan handles Field Hockey.
Not only have our teams reflected good training; but our cheerleaders have ex-
celled. The cheerleaders are under the direction of Mrs. Jean Killeen. These young
ladies do a great deal to enhance school spirit; and their neat appearance and well
rehearsed cheers make them true credits to our school.

ship. Mrs. Elizabeth Gould is the capable faculty sponsor.
The Student Council sponsors all class elections and supervises all balloting.
Each candidate is required to file nomination papers and make a campaign speech

Report of Lunenburg Elementary Schools
for the Year 1959
The world continues to move at an accellerated rate. The air
age of only a few years ago has been transformed into scientific
probing into space; research and invention that previously was ac-
complished in 50 to 100 years can now be culminated in 5-10 years;
coordinated research teams and digital computers have supplanted
the individual inventor and his long painstaking experiments. The
above revolutionary developments do not mean that teachers and
school staffs must suddenly upset the entire school curriculum to
keep pace with these strides. It does indicate, however, that our
school faculty, like school faculties throughout the nation, must
maintain constant vigilance over our curriculum offerings, making
sure they are updated, and especially being mindful of the quality
of education we are offering.
We in Lunenburg do not believe that we are going to prepare our youngsters for
this rapidly evolving life through panaceas of more and more time devoted to mathema-
tics and science, special programs for the gifted, having the television supplant the
teacher, or by some magic formula. Trends of this nature have merit and are being
initiated, even in Lunenburg. A school faculty, however, must determine the use and
extent of use, or extensions of its curricula, first on the basis of what is being
offered, and secondly, on the basis of how well an extension of the present curriculum
improves our offerings in preparation of our youngsters for our junior-senior high
school.
Every time we consider extensions or modifications of our program we keep one
thing in mind: we must determine without doubt that a modification or extension is
improving the quality of elementary school education in Lunenburg. If it does not
meet this criterion, then the consideration for a change or addition to the program
is either dropped or restudied. Lunenburg does not believe in adopting something
just because it is the "educational fashion" to do it. It must be necessary for
Lunenburg after considerable research, study and sometimes even experimental use.
The most important consideration in any revision in program is the major premise
that we must not lose sight of offering the best basic elementary school education for
each child. Every thing added or changed must have this essential objective in com-
plete focus.
IMPROVEMENT OF CURRICULUM OFFERINGS
Seeking to continually improve the quality of education, our faculty assumed a
preliminary study of another area of the curriculum during this year, that of social
studies, geography, and history. Since September, fifteen members of our facul t y
including one member of the high school social studies department, have been studying
the trends and content of social studies curricula. This study will be continued into
the coming year. As a result of this study, it is anticipated that offerings in the
social studies will be coordinated in Grades one through twelve. It is to be noted
that thisis the fourth curriculum area to be studied by the elementary school faculty.
Others already studied, which contributed to definite improvements in the quality of
the offerings in each subject area,were: arithmetic;language arts (English, spelling,
penmanship); and science.
91 -
===PAGE 94===
Along with the social studies course we have had completed a thorough survey of
our map and globe equipment in each classroom, Grades one through six.
During the last few weeks and continuing into January, all fourth grade teachers
have been working on a teaching technique called "team teaching", studying its basic
philosophy, examining the techniques that it includes, and preparing a unit of work
to be taught to all fourth graders using this plan. This technique makes maximum use
of every teacher in the teaching process and, at the same time, through temporary re-
grouping of pupils allows for maximum learning through the adaptation of assignments
to the abilities of pupils. This technique is being used in connection with social
studies and has great promise of improving the quality of teaching in other areas and
in other upper grades.
In one of our sixth grades we are giving some children of normal and low ability
additional assistance in reading through the use of a multi-level set of reading
materials. These reading materials are so organized that a student proceeds individu-
ally to more difficult materials as he masters each level of difficulty. The advantage
lies in the fact that this program is provided in addition to the regular reading
program. It has become evident that this program will improve reading skills at a
much more rapid rate. Plans are now in progress to expand this multi-level program
limitedly to some other rooms in the upper grades.
When we see an area needs additional attention, we immediately begin to work to
improve this area. Recent testing showed that reading for comprehension needed
attention on the primary level. As a result, definite action has been taken for
additional teaching in this area. It must be mentioned here that one of the best uses
of tests is that of locating those areas that need greatest attention, and concentra-
ting on improving these offerings.

At the same time that we use tests to identify strengths and weaknesses of
individual pupils, SO also do we employ the results to evaluate our own school's
strengths and weaknesses. As in the past, the standardized testing program "sounds
the alarm", getting us into action to improve the situation in any subject offering
where a need is shown for improvement. During this current year, for example, we
are fortunate that through the testing program we can concentrate our efforts on the
indications that greater attention is needed in reading comprehension, spelling,
punctuation and usage. Teachers are working on a detailed analysis of the children's
answers to questions in these areas in order to determine the specific types of items
in each subject that need reteaching or reinforcing. Detailed followup of this nature
is what makes a testing program valuable.

SCHOOL POPULATION
A recent enrollment compilation of pupils in the Elementary Schools indicated 902
students. At the present time we are housing our Elementary School students as
follows: 24 classrooms in the Elementary School; 8 classrooms in the Annex, and 1 class-
room at Brooks House. Additional classroom space will have to be provided beginning
in the school year 1960-61. The School Building Survey Committee is now investigating
the needs of the elementary school as well as that of the junior-senior high school.
It is to be noted that present enrollments in some elementary grades are high enough
so that even a slightly greater than normal move-in of additional population or addi-
tional housing could very rapidly change the classroom need situation. Under normal
circumstances the next peak in enrollment will be reached in the school year 1962-63.

Mr. Maurice .Billings, Superintendent, the members of the School Committee, and citizens
of the community.

Excerpts from Superintendent's Report to School Committee, September, 1958
ABOUT OUR NATION AND OUR GOVERNMENT:
We believe the goals of America as set forth in the Declaration of Independence, the Constitu-
tion, Bill of Rights, and subsequent amendments continue to be the foundations on which our free
society rests, Our faith and confidence in these values remain firm and our courage to support them
is unwavering
ABOUT OUR SCHOOLS:
The task ahead of the public schools in scope, program quality, and sheer added numbers calls
for a financial effort as magnificient as is our ideal
Although certain values, certain knowledge, certain skills are constant, education like our
society must change. The Educational Program of today should be different from that of yesterday,
and today's will prove inadequate tomorrow. Public schooling in the United States has had the
essential quality of flexibility in the past. This characteristic is imperative for the future.
Where the schools are headed is more important than where they have been.
Unless the unique differences of learners are recognized and educational programs designed
accordingly, education will prove a false hope. Diversity as to vocational purpose, values to be
sought in life, together with the significant range of abilities, suggest that school programs and
school standards must be designed with like diversity. Furthermore, our total society in the long
run will be best served when all diverse talents are fully developed. The more complex a society
becomes, the more it demands men of diverse competence.
All this suggests that both curriculum and instruction be determined only by those who know our
culture, know our values, know our changing world, and know the nature of the individual--how he
learns and how his learning is best measured.
Although there is ample evidence to show that the public schools have been successful in fitting
many pupils for college, for citizenship, and for vocational productiveness, the demands of the future
require much improvement. Both quality and quantity in learning should be stepped up. Not only more
speed but also more depth of learning can and must be achieved. New content and newer methods,
supported by research, are imperative lest the schools fall.
ABOUT OUR SCHOOL BOARDS:
We believe boards of education carry responsibilities for the local, state, and national wel-
fare unsurpassed by any other group of public servants, America's fundamental faith in the wisdom
of the people receives its greatest expression through our school board system of educational control,
As legal officials of the state and legally selected representatives of the general public,
school boards are obligated to take leadership to provide ever-improved educational programs and
better facilities for learning. Although sensitive to the wishes of the general public, they must
maintain independence of action and independence in policy-making.
School board members should be sensitive to the desires, aspirations, and judgments of the
people, recognizing that after all the conversations, comments, commendations, and complaints have
been heard, they themselves must ultimately serve as umpire and resolve the arguments because it is
their legal responsibility to do so

Report of the School Lunch Program
for the Year 1959
Dear Mr. Billings:
This is my Fourth Annual Report of the Lunenburg School Lunch Program and is for
the school year, September 1958 through June 1959.
During this period we fed 128,843 Type "A" lunches to students and 9,469 lunches
to adults for a period of 172 lunch days which is an average of 804 lunches a day in
both schools combined. Beyond these figures, we sold 125,497 half-pints of milk under
the special milk program; as well as 3,687 half-pints to adults. That is a total of
267, 496 half-pints of milk during the year.
Financially, this past year was a little better than the previous one. This Fall
(1959), many more meats and canned goods are available on the "commodity list". We
have received turkeys, ground pork, cheese, butter, rice, flour, dried milk and eggs,
peanut-butter, corn-meal, and canned peaches, cherries, purple plums, applesauce,
sliced apples, green peas, whole kernel corn, tomatoes and tomato paste.
Participation percentage-wise in the Junior-Senior High School was 80% according
to attendance, and 75% according to membership. In the Elementary School, participa-
tion according to attendance was 62%, and 57% according to membership.
The personnel in the Elementary School consists of Mrs. Phyllis Prouty as Manager;
Mrs. Hazel Whitney, Cook; Mrs. Helen Hebert, Assistant Cook; Mrs. Lucy Chartrand,
Mrs. Bertha Geigner, Mrs. Margaret Haueisen and Mrs. Rita Seguin (4 hours) as general
workers,
In the Jr.-Sr. High School, Mrs. Dorothy Maki is the Head Cook. The workers are
Mrs. Myrtle Eastman, Mrs. Edna Huff, Mrs. Ethel Malone, Mrs. Fanny Ovaska, Mrs. Eme-
lian Perry, and Mrs. EmmaRhude.
At the Thanksgiving dinner this November, we served 1400 "Type A" lunches. The
Elementary Annex pupils were transported to the high school to enjoy the meal with
us. At Christmas, we served 1356 lunches, The tables in the Elementary School were
decorated very appropriately by the students and teachers and the dining area in the
high school by the Art Department. The turkeys were available for both meals from
the Government Commodities.
Mrs. Prouty and I attended a meeting of the Massachusetts School Lunch Advisory
Committee on January 19, from 2:00 P. M. to 4:00 P.M. at the Boston Trade High School
for Boys, Parker Street, Boston. There was a very interesting all electric kitchen
in this school. In April, Mrs. Prouty and I spent Monday, April 20, at the Amherst-
Pelham Regional School, attending the Spring Workshop, sponsored by the Massachusetts
Department of Education and the Office of School Lunch Programs, We took in another
day of this workshop at the Boston Gas Company that same week. On Tuesday, September
22, 1959, Mrs. Prouty and I attended another meeting of the School Lunch Advisory
Committee at the Henry O. Peabody School in Norwood. We also attended the dinner of
the Massachusetts School Food Service Association in Framingham on the evening of May
8, 1959, and on Saturday, May 9, nine of our school lunch personnel attended the
annual meeting and program of the association at the Framingham High School.
- 107
===PAGE 110===
The annual Summer Workshop held for one week at Fitchburg Teachers' College
(July 13-17, 1959) was attended nearly 100% by our personnel. Lunenburg was the
hostess for these days to an average attendance of 200 supervisors, managers, and
workers daily from all over the State of Massachusetts. The state School Lunch Pro-
gram Office was very appreciative to the Town of Lunenburg, the School Committee and
the Administration for allowing them to conduct this Workshop in Lunenburg Jr. -Sr.
High School. Actual "Type A" lunch was prepared, figured and costed each day.
The annual meeting of the Massachusetts School Food Service Association which is
usually held in the Spring of the year was moved back to the Fall and was held at
Wrentham on October 31, 1959. Mrs. Prouty and I attended this all day meeting at the
beautiful King Philip Regional School in Wrentham, Massachusetts. The admittance of
Hawaii, our 50th State, was the theme and decor of the meeting, as well as other sub-
jects and demonstrations.
At the first regular meeting of the P-TA in October 1959, the members, parents,
and friends went through a shortened form of a student's day in school. We served a
typical "Type A" lunch to the parents, reaching nearly 300 in both schools. The
lunchroom workers donated their time for this public-relations story. We had trays
of food, samples of two weeks menus on display in the corridors with actual costs
shown. The Art Department did displays in the show cases and I wish to express my
thanks to Mrs. Drumm and Mrs. Powers for this attractive exhibit.
It has been my privilege to be invited by the State Office with about 24 Super-
visors of School Lunch Programs in the state to participate in an Equipment Seminar
this Fall. We are rewriting a bulletin which we hope will prove helpful to architects,
building committees, superintendents, and administration in the future new schools'
cafeterias and kitchens.
I am enclosing a complete report of 1958-59 accounts on the next page which I
hope will be of interest to all. I strongly recommend a salary increase for the
personnel of the lunchrooms, but feel the need of assistance as the students' money
cannot take a "cost-of-living raise" to employees. I also continue to hope that we
may serve the Annex children from a kitchen near there in the future.
In closing I wish to thank you, Mr. Billings, Mr. Alley, and Mr. Passios, the
School Committee, also the Finance Committee, all town personnel, the faculties and
custodians, and, of course, my own personnel, who are very wonderful and doing such a
fine job this Fall.
Respectfully submitted,
RUTH A. CORRELL, Supervisor

Physical examinations are being conducted on small groups of students each week
by our School Physician, Dr. John DeLeo. So far, students in grades one, seven, and
ten and all who participate in athletics have been examined. In the course of these
examinations, several were found to have heart murmurs. The parents of these students
were notified and all have been seen by their own physician. None were serious enough
to warrant restriction in any way.
Under the sponsorship of Nashoba Associated Boards of Health a Mantoux tubercu-
lin testing program was held for the pupils in grade one. One hundred twenty-nine chil-
dren had the test and all were negative. Seven children in other grades who were ab-
sent when the tests were made last year were tested at this time. All were negative.
Members of the teaching staff and other school personnel, including bus drivers, who
were due for a routine chest x-ray this year, had a choice of taking this test, since
if the Mantoux test proved negative, they would be certified. However, if the test
proved positive they would still have to have a chest x-ray in order to be certified.
Thirty-five adults took the test; of this number, thirteen showed positive reaction
and were x-rayed. One of these, a member of the Elementary School personnel, had a
positive chest x-ray. He was hospitalized immediately following the report.
The annual dental survey and fluorine program was offered again this year under
the auspices of the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health. This is available to chil-
dren in grades two, five, and eight. Three hundred forty-two pupils participated in
the program. Mrs. Clarence May served as dental hygienist.
Hearing tests have been completed and we are presently conducting vision tests.
Every pupil in the school system has these tests regularly every year. We are getting
cooperation from parents regarding correction of defects. Financial assistance has
been secured in several instances in order to obtain glasses. We are indebted to the
Red Cross and the Salvation Army for this assistance,
In closing, I wish to express my appreciation to you, Mr. Billings, to the
principals, the teaching staff, and the personnel for their courtesy and cooperation.
Respectfully submitted,
SIGNE E. SCHULTZ, R. N.
School Nurse
January 14, 1960.
