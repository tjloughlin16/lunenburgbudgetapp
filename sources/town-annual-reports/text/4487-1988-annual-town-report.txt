Memoriam
HERBERT E. HILL
1917 - 1988
Herb Hill was a very popular and much loved
resident of Lunenburg for many years. He was
active in Town sports and volunteered a great deal
of his time to the youngsters of our community.
He was involved with Little League, Pop Warner,
and the local Babe Ruth League was named after
him.
The Brazier/Hill Weight Room at Lunenburg High
School is named for Herb Hill and Newton Brazier.
Herb, along with Newt, was instrumental in raising
the funds for this facility and ran the program
there for several years.
Herb encouraged Lunenburg youth to better their
grades thus enabling them to meet college entrance
requirements. He shared his time, interest and
knowledge with the young people of Lunenburg. He
will be remembered always for the humor, kindness
and caring he gave to all he met.
RAYMOND G. CHAPDELAINE
1929 - 1988
Although not a "true Lunenburger" (Ray moved to
Town when he was just under two years old), his
love for Lunenburg was quite evident. Raised on a
dairy farm on Leominster Road, he was a 1947
graduate of Lunenburg High School.
Anyone who attended town meetings knew that Ray
was never hesitant to let his opinions be known.
Usually he kept his thoughts until the last
minute, then he would ask to be recognized by the
Moderator. Many a vote outcome was changed
because of this.
Ray never said "no" when his Town needed him. He
was always ready to donate his time, business
knowledge, resources, and even his antique trucks
and tractors for parades. He served on the
Conservation Commission for many years and served
as its Chairman. Ray will be sadly missed by this
Town. The Annual Town Meeting will not be the
same in his absence.

board. Our thanks to Lance for his hard work on behalf of the Town.

Town Counsel
It appears that the area-wide real estate boom has abated somewhat. Despite that
fact, the development of the former Maplewood Golf Course has consumed a great deal of
Town Counsel's time. In addition, extensive discussion and negotiation have taken place
regarding the acquisition of the Town's well sites.
Town Code enforcement has also taken a great deal of Town Counsel's time and,
unfortunately, has had mixed results. Efforts to have a number of dogs removed from
premises at Hickory Hills Lake in order to abate a noise nuisance have resulted in dual
proceedings in both the Superior and District Court with resulting appeals. Finally, the
Town was served with a civil complaint which alleges Town responsibility for the death of
a pedestrian due to inadequate street lights.

Board of Assessors
The year 1988 was a year of planned, controlled, dramatic, and deliberate change for
the Board of Assessors. These changes took place in the areas of Board membership, office
staff, office procedures, and preparation of the upcoming revaluation. Kathy Herrick
retired from the Board effective in May after many years of service, and the Board wishes
to extend its thanks to Kathy for her service and for her assistance in the transition
that has taken place in the last two years. All three Assessor positions were on the
ballot in the May 1988 Town Election. Jim Noonan and John Male were re-elected, and Ernie
Sund was elected to an initial term on the Board. Regarding the office staff, Judy Holden
served her first full year as Chief Clerk, and was promoted to the position of Assistant
Assessor in December 1988. Lisa Normandin has been a great help as a part-time clerk in
the office. During the course of the year, all members of the Board, as well as the
Assistant Assessor, successfully completed the certification process administered by the
Department of Revenue.
The office procedures have been automated during the past year. The computer
installed last year has been heavily utilized. The Tax Base information for the entire
Town has been loaded onto the computer. Tax bills were printed by the staff using the
computer, as opposed to using an outside service. Also, parcel lists for office and
public use are now prepared on the computer. The CAMA software has been successfully
installed, in preparation for the revaluation.
The 1990 revaluation will be done by the Assessors utilizing the CAMA software. The
Department of Revenue has approved the revaluation plan submitted by the Assessors, and is
working closely with the Assessors on use of the CAMA software. The Assessors' maps have

been consolidated as planned, and are being used by the data collection firm, Batchelder
Associates. This firm has been contracted with by the Assessors to visit and collect data
regarding every piece of real property in Lunenburg, as required in a revaluation. The
Assessors will use this data, together with the CAMA software, to arrive at updated
property valuations. As a separate part of the revaluation, the Assessors have contracted
with the firm Heaphy & Associates to do a valuation of personal property, as required by
the Department of Revenue revaluation process.
At Town Meeting, voters determine the amount of money to be raised by taxation. A
revaluation, required every three years by the State, is intended to apportion this tax
burden equitably among Town taxpayers based on the full and fair market value of real
property. Property values, and in particular land values, have increased substantially in
the recent past. Accordingly, assessed values will reflect this increased market value,
but this will be accompanied by a corresponding decrease in the tax rate since the total
dollar amount to be raised (as voted at Town Meeting) is subject to Proposition 2.5
limits. This will result, for the "average" taxpayer, in no significant change in the
actual tax due. However, since the land value will most likely comprise a greater
percentage of total value than in the past, there may be individual situations involving
large parcels of land, or particularly valuable land, such as waterfront, in which tax
amounts may increase. The proposed valuations prepared by the Assessors will be available
for public inspection. The Department of Revenue also must certify the newly proposed
values as an accurate representation of full and fair market value, prior to finalization
of the new values.
There are various tax exemptions available to qualifying taxpayers. Complete
information may be obtained at the Assessors' Office in Town Hall, or by phone at 582-6192
daily from 8:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. A summary of these exemptions is as follows:

Library Trustees
If ever there was an overworked phrase, it has to be the one about "good news/bad
news." Nevertheless, there is no more apt way to describe the Library's situation at the
end of 1988. Good news is that circulation of materials jumped almost 15% this year and
that the staff managed to cope with the increase. Bad news is that we are getting further
behind in our ability to purchase and process new materials. Good news is that we have a
new septic system and that work is finally underway on our new bathrooms and work space.
Bad news is that the current project will not yield any more space, and that so many other
urgent building needs still await attention. Good news is that we have offered some
innovative programs this year and they have been well received. Bad news is that the
staff persons responsible for planning and carrying out these creative projects are
woefully underpaid and we see no hope of improving their compensation. All of which means
that we've had a banner year in terms of service to the Town, yet feel increasingly
apprehensive about our ability to continue this level of service.
It has been gratifying to find that the reduction of open hours in the fall of 1987,
due to lack of funds, did not cause more than a temporary drop in Library use, but we
still hear patrons saying, "You're never open when I can get here!". The fact that there
is nothing left to cut from the budget except staff - and that would mean an additional
reduction in open hours - is cause for much anxiety. We have actually requested five
essential additional staff hours next year to help keep pace with the demand for services.
The renovations now in progress will provide new restrooms on the entry level of the
building where they will be handicapped accessible, and no longer prone to septic
overflow. This necessitates a rearrangement of the varied segments of Library service
located on the entry floor. When the new restrooms are ready, the old ones downstairs
will be dismantled and converted into staff work space. In addition to the funds voted
toward this project at last year's Town Meeting, all of this year's State aid to the
Library will be spent in completing the renovations.
Many of our services beyond the most basic would be impossible to provide without the
support of the Friends of the Library. Their volunteer work and sponsorship of programs
have enriched the Library's offerings immeasurably again this year. Their gifts have
included museum passes, children's programs, craft supplies, award books, concerts,
contest prizes, Christmas wreaths, rental books and refreshments.
Donations of materials were made in memory of Ralph Benjamin, Raymond Chapdelaine,
James Congdon and Irene White. Contributions from the Lunenburg Memorial Fund, the
Women's Club and numerous individuals have been of great help in augmenting the
collection.
The strongest demand for services continues to be in the children's area. The
school's "Books and Beyond" program has created a sharp rise in circulation. Preschool
story hours and story sessions for the Primary School are an integral part of the
Library's schedule, as is the summer reading program with two or three activities each
week. Some exciting programs for children have been offered for the first time. One was
a very successful "sleep-over" held in the United Parish Building with 62 third and fourth
graders and a total of 18 adults alternating periods of reading with group activities and
at least a few hours of sleep. "Open Doors to the Future" offered six weekly sessions for
middle school children to listen to a story followed by discussion, with a variety of
adults from the community also participating.
Ritter Bits, our monthly newsletter, was first issued in May, and has become an
established Library resource, with patrons looking forward to each new issue. Its
usefulness is extended by inclusion of a community calendar and brief announcements of
matters of interest to Townspeople.
The opportunity to borrow video cassettes from the Library has received an
enthusiastic response from borrowers. A collection of about 175 tapes has been supplied
through a combination of grant money and the Friends, and is supplemented by rotating
collections from the regional system and other libraries. Video circulation now adds up
to over 9% of total circulation. Libraries are definitely not "just books" any more.

Police Department
The Lunenburg Police Department for the past several years has needed additional
help. With all the growth in the community and the increased demand for service, we find
it increasingly difficult to keep up with the calls for service. Many times we are twenty
minutes or more delayed in our response time for calls. Frequently, we will leave a call
to respond to one of a more serious nature and return 30 - 45 minutes later to finish the
first call.
Presently, we run with two officers assigned per shift. In November of 1988, the
Selectmen and the Finance Committee approved an additional man on the second shift to act
as a traffic safety officer. By adding one additional man and making a safety officer on
the day and evening shifts, we would be able to target "traffic troubled areas" in the
community and apprehend some of these traffic violators who have a complete disregard for
public safety. It would also enable us to broaden our services to the community and spend
more time investigating serious crimes, as well as matters dealing with alcohol and
substance abuse. I ask that you support my request for additional help so that we can
continue to provide to you, the Townspeople, a safe community to live in and a healthy
environment to raise your families.

Fire Department
During calendar year 1988 the Fire Department responded to 616 calls for assistance.
This number represents an increase of 71 calls from the previous year. The calls are
classified as follows: Structural - 44; Motor Vehicle - 22; Motor Vehicle Accident - 18;
Investigations - 13; False - 2; Storm Standby - 3; Explosion - 1; Service - 13; Medical -
2; Hazardous Materials - 15; Smoke Detector Activation - 17; Box Alarms - 30; Mutual Aid -
15; Illegal Burning - 7; Rubbish - 8; Brush and Grass - 24; Telephonic - 7; Outside
Electrical - 12; Good Intent - 5; Water/Ice Rescue - 8; Ambulance - 350.
One of the largest increase in number of calls was in the number of ambulance calls.
We ran 86 more calls than in 1987. The last two years have shown an overall increase of
109 calls for both fire and ambulance. This can be directly attributed to new growth and
an increased population. This trend is not expected to diminish, thus it is very
important to make sure that our public service departments keep pace with this growth. We
must adequately fund budgets, replace apparatus in a timely manner, and supply adequate
buildings and training facilities for our firefighters to insure that our citizens will be
properly protected against the tragedy of fire or severe medical problems. Firefighting
and emergency medical work has become a very complex and dangerous job. We must commit
ourselves to be able to handle every emergency that arises. The only way to do this is to
have top notch equipment and highly trained firefighters to respond to your calls. To
this end, we will continue to ask your support so we can give you the calibre of fire and
medical coverage that you expect and deserve.
Last year I listed three areas of concern in the Fire Department for future years.
They are the high cost of replacement apparatus, the purchase of an aerial ladder, and the
construction of a new Fire Station to adequately house our apparatus. These problems
still exist and we must come to grips with them in the near future. With the advent of
townhouse construction and people building much larger homes, we find ourselves not being
able to reach roofs and chimneys with our ground ladders. This puts us at a severe
disadvantage and also imperils the lives of our firefighters and could lead to disastrous
consequences for the homeowner. However, until we build a larger station, we do not have
the room to house a ladder truck. We really must give this problem some serious
consideration at this point in time.
I want to take this opportunity to thank all the Townspeople and Town officials for
their continued support during the past year. Also, special thanks to our firefighters
and EMT's for their dedication and unending efforts to provide protection for the citizens
of Lunenburg.

Historical Commission
The Lunenburg Historical Commission meets on the second Wednesday of each month in
the Lunenburg Historical Society building. Our main concern during the past few years has
been the establishment of a National Historic District in Lunenburg, and we have been
working on the inventory of historic buildings, deciding on boundaries and applying to the
State for Historic District status. In the fall of 1988 we received notice of approval of
Lunenburg Center as a National Historic District. Although we had researched some
outlying properties, it was decided to limit our application to the area around the Center
of Town. This included Main Street; a short distance on Oak Avenue; Highland Street from
Main Street to the Town Pound; Massachusetts Avenue for about a half mile in each
direction from the intersection of Main Street and Lancaster Avenue; Lancaster Avenue from
Massachusetts Avenue to Rolling Acres Road; and Leominster Road from Massachusetts Avenue
to West Street. All the properties in this circle are not "conforming" properties; many
are not historically or architecturally significant. There are about forty-five
conforming properties, including many private homes, some public buildings such as the
Town Hall, Eagle House, Library, and Congregational Church, and commercial properties such
as the Credit Union, Honey Farms Market, and the former Methodist Church. Our Commission
is now engaged in gathering and disseminating information about plaques which individual
property owners may buy and install on their buildings, and about roadside markers to
designate the boundaries of the Historic District; the latter will need funding by the
Town.
The Historical Commission functions on a minimal budget, but the Town has been very
cooperative about voting funds for specific needs, which is greatly appreciated. We feel
that a milestone has been reached in the establishment of the Lunenburg Center Historic
District, which could be enlarged or extended at any time. Also, separate Historic
Districts could be established.

All communities in Massachusetts are faced with a
new reality of revenues which are not keeping up
with expenditures, and a citizenry which is not
willing to raise taxes through Proposition 2 1/2
override processes. However, as any financial
planner will point out, if one is faced with money
problems, one has to face that reality. Lunenburg
has been spending beyond its means for almost six

depleted, the stabilization fund has been used to fund current expenses such as police
cruisers and radios, we have begun to borrow for items for which we used to appropriate

as an individual who uses all his savings to pay for his mortgage realizes, the next month
the mortgage must be paid again, and there will be no reserve for an emergency.
What is the answer? The financial planner would recommend two solutions: increase
revenues and decrease expenses. We must continue to look to keeping fees for services in
line with related expenses, state and federal grants must be pursued, and consolidation of
purchasing on a town-wide basis will help. However, that alone will not solve the problem
of spending above available resources. Fiscal year 1990 will see a year of reduced
personnel forces, and departments' expectations will need to be lower for at least three
years, as we begin a savings program, and match expenditures to actual revenues. We have
to pay for the mistakes of the past, and it is in this atmosphere that we will see the
true management capacity of our appointed and elected officials. The sooner we "face the
music" and take decisive action, the healthier Lunenburg's financial position will become.
TREASURER
Perhaps one of the biggest changes in the town's government occurred at the annual town
meeting when it was voted to establish the position of Town Accountant effective July 1,
1989. Historically, the treasurer of each town also served as its accounting officer.
Over the years, most towns have voted to have a different person handle the bookkeeping
and accounts. While this may add an additional layer of government, the initial stages of
this transition have already begun through the separation of files, and a training program
for staff in the new functions. Under my administration, the town's accounting system was
converted to the Uniform Municipal Accounting System (UMAS) in 1986. As a result, the
town accountant will be able to take over an up-to-date accounting procedure. This new
position will have more authority and responsibility than that of the treasurer serving as
accounting officer, including the right to refuse payment for illegal, fraudulent or
excessive bills.

TAX COLLECTOR
Following in the footsteps of someone as committed as Senator Mary Padula, it has been the
goal of my administration to maintain her process of 100% tax collection. Through a
concerted effort by my staff and I we have been able to meet that goal. Through
advertising of properties in the Fitchburg Sentinel and constant communication with
property owners, we have collected well over 98% of the taxes committed to this office.
Those few people who do not pay by June 30, have a lien placed against their homes at the
Registry of Deeds. The town is thus protected against someone selling out from under the
taxes. Many thanks for the hard work of my office staff!!!
The purchase of a personal computer for town hall has proved its worth as a result of the
spreadsheet capacity, even without specially designed software. My Assistant Treasurer,
Becky Whitten developed a program for use in the daily cash book for real estate and motor
vehicle excise taxes. As a result, taxes which have been paid are being posted on a more
timely basis with fewer errors than the system of manual calculations in previous years.
Similar programs were developed for treasury and clerk functions.
Motor vehicle excise brings in over a half million dollars in local receipts to Lunenburg,
and it often seems as many complaints from car owners. It should be noted that the bills
are only as accurate as the information that people provide to their insurance companies,
for it is the Registry of Motor Vehicles which sends the bills to us to mail. If those in
the Whalom District fail to note that their cars are garaged in Lunenburg, their bills may
be sent to Fitchburg. If a car is totaled in an accident or fire, and the registration
plates are not returned to the Registry, bills will continue to be sent out. It is the
responsibility of each and every car owner to see that their information is kept current
at the Registry. People should expect to receive an excise bill approximately two to
three months after they register their automobile. As is true of the real estate taxes,
failure to receive a bill in the mail due to a wrong address or problems with mail
delivery, does not excuse the tax or late charges. If you have not receive a bill within
this time frame, call town hall.
Property taxes represent over 50% of the town's budget, and therefore represent a major
component of cash flow projections. If a revaluation is completed late and tax bills
don't go out until December or later, the town will be forced to borrow in anticipation of
those tax receipts. The revaluation that has been ongoing in 1988 appears to be on track
to allow for timely tax bills in fiscal year 1990. In a downturn of the economy, taxes do
not get paid as quickly and as a result the town does not earn as much on investing the
funds. Combining this will the loss of the non-recurring sources of money such as free
cash, and the state's threat of not paying state aid on a quarterly basis, Lunenburg is
facing a major increase in paying for interest on temporary loans. We must be
conservative in expenditures at the same time as aggressive in collecting what is due to
the town.

Towns or records which were forwarded to this Town from other municipalities in the State.
Lunenburg residents are urged to notify the Town Clerk if in-State records have not been
received and recorded. If a Birth, Marriage, or Death occurred outside the State, the
same may be recorded in the Lunenburg Town Clerk's office if certified records are
presented. In some cases the Town Clerk's records are supplemented by records of burials
which appear elsewhere in this report. In order for these records to also be included in
the official Vital Statistics records, the family of the deceased should be present a
certified copy of the death certificate to the Town Clerk. In accordance with the General
Laws, marriages which occur here but relate to licenses which were obtained elsewhere in
the State, are no longer included in our official Town Records.

MAY 14, 1988 SPECIAL TOWN MEETING AND ANNUAL TOWN MEETING
The Meeting was called to order at 9:00 A.M. by Town Moderator Richard J. Bishop, a quorum
having been declared present.
Fr. Daniel Mulcahy, Associate Pastor of St. Boniface church provided the invocation for
the meeting.
Selectmen Chairman, Joseph F. Bilotta requested that the meeting observe a moment of
silence in memory of Raymond Chapdelaine, an active Town Meeting participant who recently
died.
The meeting pledged allegiance to the United States Flag.
Town Clerk, Robert J. Ebersole, read the opening and closing of the Town Meeting Warrant.
David Rodgers, 82 Highland Street, was recognized and asked that the Town recognize the
years of service of the following person who have retired from employment with the Town:
Evelyn Colvin 35 years Highway Department; William Reed 15 years Board of Assessors;
Bernard Paton 40 years Fire Department, Board of Selectmen and Assessors; Kathryn Herrick
18 years Treasurer's office and Board of Assessors; and Josephine Meloon 30 years School
Department.
At 9:15 A.M. it was voted to recess the annual meeting and take up the Special Town
Meeting Warrant.

ARTICLE 3. To see if the Town will vote to amend Article XXI (GENERAL WETLANDS PROTECTION
BY-LAW) of the Town's By-laws by deleting SECTIONS 1 and 2, and inserting new SECTIONS 1
and 2 as follows: "SECTION 1: Purpose The purpose of this by-law is to protect the
wetlands, related water resources and adjoining land areas in the Town of Lunenburg by
prior review and control of activities deemed by the Conservation Commission likely to
have a significant or cumulative effect upon wetland values, including but not limited to
the following: public or private water supply, groundwater, flood control, erosion and
sedimentation control, storm damage, prevention of water pollution, fisheries, wildlife
habitat, recreation, and aesthetics, these values are to be known collectively as the
"wetland values protected by this by-law". SECTION 2: Jurisdiction Except as permitted
by the Conservation Commission or as provided in this by-law, no person shall remove,
fill, dredge, build upon, or alter the following resource areas and buffer zones: a. Any
freshwater wetland: 1. Bordering vegetated freshwater wetlands, 2. Isolated vegetated
freshwater wetlands, 3. Seasonal freshwater wetlands, and within one hundred 100 feet of
said areas; b. Any bank or beach, and within one hundred (100) feet of said areas; C.
Any lake, river, pond or stream, whether intermittent or continuous, natural or manmade,
and within one hundred (100) feet of said areas; d. Any land under aforesaid waters; e.
Any land subject to flooding or inundation by groundwater, surface water, storm flowage,
whether such land is bordering or isolated, and within one hundred (100) feet of said
areas."; and to add the following SECTION 6 between SECTIONS 5 and 7: "SECTION 6:
Coordination with Other Boards Any person filing a permit application or a request for
determination with the Commission shall provide written notice thereof at the same time,
by certified mail or hand delivery, to the Board of Selectmen, Planning Board, Zoning
Board of Appeals, Board of Health and Building Inspector. The Commission shall not take
final action until such boards and officials have had fourteen (14) days from receipt of
notice to file written comments and recommendations with the Commission, which the
Commission shall take into account, but which shall not be binding on the Commission. The
applicant shall have the right to receive any such comments and recommendations, and to
respond to them at a hearing of the Commission, prior to final action.", or act anything
thereon. (Submitted by Conservation Commission) VOTED UNANIMOUSLY.

4.5.
OFFICE PARK AND INDUSTRIAL DISTRICT
4.5.1.
PURPOSE
THE PURPOSE OF THE OFFICE PARK AND INDUSTRIAL DISTRICT IS TO PROVIDE SUITABLE
AREAS FOR OFFICES, RESEARCH, DEVELOPMENT, ASSEMBLY AND LIGHT MANUFACTURING
CONSISTENT WITH THE LEVEL OF SERVICES PROVIDED IN THE COMMUNITY AND NATURAL
RESOURCES AVAILABLE.

PERMITTED USES
4.5.2.1.
IN THE INDUSTRIAL DISTRICT THE FOLLOWING USES ARE PERMITTED:
A) OFFICE BUILDINGS
B) OFFICE PARKS

IN THE INDUSTRIAL DISTRICT THE FOLLOWING USES ARE PERMITTED SUBJECT TO A SPECIAL
PERMIT GRANTED BY THE PLANNING BOARD:
A) INDUSTRIAL PARKS, INCLUDING BUILDINGS FOR ASSEMBLING OF PREVIOUSLY
MANUFACTURED PARTS, PROCESSING AND PACKAGING PRODUCTS.
B) RESEARCH ESTABLISHMENTS, INCLUDING LABORATORIES AND INCIDENTAL ASSEMBLY.
C) MANUFACTURING.
D) ENCLOSED STORAGE.
E) DISTRIBUTION.
F) EARTH REMOVAL AND LANDFILL OPERATIONS SUBJECT TO THE LUNENBURG EARTH REMOVAL
BYLAW AND TO ALL OTHER PROVISIONS OF THIS BYLAW.
G) RETAIL USES AND SERVICES ACCESSORY TO A PERMITTED USE SUCH AS A CAFETERIA
FOR EMPLOYEES.
4.5.3.
CONDITIONS FOR USE
4.5.3.1.
A SITE PLAN SHALL BE SUBMITTED IN THE MANNER AS PROVIDED IN SECTION 4.4.4.1. AND
2. TO ASSIST IN ITS DELIBERATIONS, THE PLANNING BOARD MAY REQUEST AN OPINION
FROM THE INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT COMMISSION.
4.5.3.2.
THE OFF-STREET PARKING AND LOADING SHALL COMPLY WITH THE RULES AND REGULATIONS
OF THE PLANNING BOARD GOVERNING OFF-STREET PARKING AND LOADING DESIGN,
CONSTRUCTION AND MAINTENANCE OF.

===PAGE 57===
4.5.3.3. THE PLAN SHALL SHOW A BUFFER STRIP WHICH SHALL BE LANDSCAPED OR COVERED WITH
NATURAL GROWTH INDIGENOUS TO THE AREA OF
A) AT LEAST FIFTY (50) FEET WHERE THE DISTRICT JOINS A LOT USED OR AVAILABLE
FOR RESIDENTIAL PURPOSES.
B) AT LEAST TWENTY (20) FEET AT ALL SIDE AND REAR PROPERTY LINES, EXCEPT AS
PROVIDED ABOVE.
4.5.3.4.
UNLESS OTHERWISE APPROVED WITH THE SITE PLAN, ACCESSORY USES NOT CONTAINED IN
THE PRINCIPAL BUILDING SHALL BE PROVIDED WITH SAFE ACCESS AND SHALL BE SCREENED
FROM VIEW FROM A PUBLIC WAY OR SHALL BE IN A STRUCTURE OF AN ARCHITECTURAL STYLE
COMPATIBLE WITH THE PRINCIPAL STRUCTURE.
4.5.4.
LIMITATIONS UPON USES
4.5.4.1.
IN ADDITION TO THE LIMITATIONS SET FORTH IN SECTION 5.0. OF THE BYLAW, ALL USES
WITHIN AN OFFICE PARK AND INDUSTRIAL DISTRICT SHALL BE SUBJECT TO THE FOLLOWING
LIMITATIONS:
A) THE GROSS FLOOR AREA OF ALL BUILDINGS AND STRUCTURES ON A LOT SHALL NOT
EXCEED
1. FIFTY (50) PERCENT OF THE TOTAL LOT AREA OF A LOT USED FOR AN OFFICE
PARK OR OFFICE BUILDING.
2. SEVENTY (70) PERCENT OF THE TOTAL LOT AREA OF A LOT USED FOR STORAGE OR
DISTRIBUTION.
3. AN AMOUNT DETERMINED BY THE PLANNING BOARD NOT TO EXCEED SEVENTY (70)
PERCENT OF THE TOTAL LOT AREA FOR ANY OTHER USE.
B) THE TOTAL AREA OF ANY LOT DEVOTED TO BUILDING, PARKING, REQUIRED LEACHING
AREA, OUTDOOR STORAGE AND DISPLAY, AND OTHER PAVED OR HARD-SURFACED AREAS SHALL
NOT EXCEED
1. NINETY (90) PERCENT OF THE TOTAL LOT AREA OF A LOT USED FOR AN OFFICE
PARK OR AN OFFICE BUILDING.
2. SEVENTY (70) PERCENT OF THE TOTAL LOT AREA OF A LOT USED FOR STORAGE OR
DISTRIBUTION.
3. AN AMOUNT DETERMINED BY THE PLANNING BOARD NOT TO EXCEED NINETY (90)
PERCENT OF A LOT USED FOR ANY OTHER PURPOSE.
4.5.4.2.
NO BUILDING SHALL BE CONSTRUCTED NEARED TO THE EXTERIOR STREET LINE OF ANY
STREET THAN FORTY (40) FEET.
4.5.4.3.
ALL USES OF BUILDINGS, LOTS OR PREMISES SHALL CONFORM TO THE FOLLOWING
STANDARDS. PRIOR TO RECEIVING A BUILDING PERMIT, THE APPLICANT SHALL SUBMIT TO
THE BUILDING INSPECTOR ADEQUATE MATERIAL AS APPLICABLE TO 780 CMR AND ALL OTHER
MATERIALS TO INSURE THAT THE PROPOSED USE OF THE BUILDING OR PREMISES WILL NOT
PRODUCE ANY NUISANCES BEYOND THE LOT LINES AS MEASURED BY THE PERFORMANCE
STANDARDS LISTED BELOW OR AS EXISTING IN COMPARABLE OPERATIONS ALLOWED IN THE
DISTRICT.
A) AIR POLLUTANTS
EXCEPT AS IS HEREIN PROVIDED, ALL USE AND CONDITIONS OF LAND, BUILDINGS AND
STRUCTURES SHALL BE IN CONFORMANCE WITH THE REGULATIONS 310 CMR 6.00 - 8.00
OF THE DEPARTMENT OF ENVIRONMENTAL QUALITY ENGINEERING, COMMONWEALTH OF
MASSACHUSETTS, DECEMBER 31, 1981 AND AMENDMENTS THERETO.
B) NOISE
NO USE SHALL BE ALLOWED IF IT WILL CAUSE SOUND, NOISE, VIBRATION, ODOR OR
FLASHING PERCEPTIBLE WITHOUT INSTRUMENTS MORE THAN TWO HUNDRED (200) FEET
FROM THE BOUNDARIES OF THE ORIGINATING PREMISES, EXCEPT FROM WARNING DEVICES,
CONSTRUCTION WORK, MAINTENANCE OR OTHER SPECIAL CIRCUMSTANCE.
AT THE DISTRICT BOUNDARY LINE, NOISE SHALL NOT EXCEED SIXTY (60) DECIBELS
BETWEEN THE NIGHTS HOURS OF 8:00 P.M. AND 6:00 P.M. AND, AT ALL OTHER TIMES,
EXCEPT FOR TRUCKS USED IN CONNECTION WITH THE INDUSTRY, SHALL NOT EXCEED
SIXTY (60) DECIBELS FOR MORE THAN TWENTY (20) MINUTES IN EACH HOUR.
NOISE SHALL BE MUFFLED SO AS NOT TO BECOME OBJECTIONABLE DUE TO
INTERMITTENCE, BEAT FREQUENCY OR HIGH FREQUENCY.
C) ODOR
EMISSIONS FROM PLANT SITES OR OTHER SOURCES AS MEASURED AT THE USER'S
PROPERTY LINE SHALL NOT EXCEED THE ESTABLISHED THRESHOLD LIMIT VALUES FOR

Superintendent's Report (Concetta A. Verge)
Having completed my first year as Superintendent of the Lunenburg Public
positive. Schools, I reflect upon a busy, exciting year which was very productive and
Thanks to the efforts of a committed staff and the support of dedicated School
Committee, involved parents, and concerned community, we have weathered another
fiscally "difficult" year and seen to it that our students have received quality
educational experiences.
The Administrative Team in our schools have pulled together and shared limited
materials and supplies, staff and ideas. The School Committee and administrators
goals listed below are clear, to the point and basic. These goals are guiding and
directing the Lunenburg Public Schools.
LUNENBURG SCHOOL COMMITTEE GOALS
1988-89
1.
PURSUE ADEQUATE FUNDING FOR APPROPRIATE EDUCATION OF
LUNENBURG STUDENTS.
2.
MAINTAIN A COMPREHENSIVE HIGH SCHOOL.
(i.e. REGIONALIZATION)

IMPLEMENT PHASE II OF STRATEGIC PLANNING PROCESS.
4.

EXPAND TECHNOLOGY IN LUNENBURG SCHOOLS.

MAINTAIN PRODUCTIVE SCHOOL / PARENT/ COMMUNITY RELATIONS.

PROVIDE APPROPRIATE, WELL-MAINTAINED, ENERGY-EFFICIENT
FACILITIES.
At a time when education and the product of the schools is being SO severely
criticized across our nation, we can be thankful for what we have in Lunenburg.

The high standards and commitment of a dedicated staff and administration and
the accountability mechanisms which have been put in place are serving to insure
that students are getting the most out of their educational experience and that
staff are providing quality instruction within the confines of existing fiscal
constraints.
ACCOUNTABILITY
The Lunenburg Public Schools have long been known for graduating productive,
successful young people who either go on to further their education in an institu-
tion of higher learning or who leave L.H.S. and move into the workforce and become
productive, self-sufficient citizens.
The fact that this happens in our community is no accident. Lunenburg staff
and administration is held accountable for delivering quality instruction.
Lunenburg students are held accountable for capitalizing on that instruction.
During the past couple of years, particularly because the entire nation has
been casting such a critical eye upon the schools, Lunenburg has formalized and
increased its systems of accountability for administration, staff and students.
Formal evaluation of staff and administration has been "stepped up". The
Lunenburg administrators and high school department heads went through an intensive
three day evaluation training program, designed to sharpen their evaluation
skills. Formal observation and evaluation of all staff has been increased and
taken very seriously.
Through the efforts of Dr. Michelle Moran Zide and Fitchburg State College, in
conjunction with the Lunenburg Public Schools, staff and administrators have been
offered training in the areas of peer observation, peer coaching, alternative
education and critical thinking strategies (Circle Collaborative Project).
Achievement Monitoring Programs for students in math and mechanics usage have
spread up from the elementary school to the middle school. Students' written
expression is also being carefully monitored and evaluated at all levels in our
schools.
The K-12 Testing Committee has recommended specific, coordinated practices of
testing all students in grades K-12 which will further insure student and teacher
accountability. This same testing committee is currently studying the research on
teacher made tests and special education testing, and will get back to the admini-
stration with further recommendations in these areas.
COORDINATION
Coordination of curriculum and programs is an ongoing top priority of this
administration.
The K-12 Social Studies Committee is completing its second year of study of
that curriculum. This group is in the process of producing a scope and sequence
for social studies grades K-12 which will insure a well-articulated social studies
program. Upon completion of that scope and sequence the K-12 committee will
re-group to determine recommendations for texts, and materials and supplies to
address the delineated scope and sequence and/or objectives of the social studies
curriculum. This will prevent the chance of haphazard purchasing of nonessential,
non-recommended or non-relevent social studies materials. Acquisition of texts and
supplemental social studies materials will be fairly uniform and based on the
agreed upon curriculum, insuring consistency of instruction and exposure to the
same contents to all students.

Staff from all disciplines K-12 have had an opportunity to meet and discuss
programs at our Early Release Day meetings. Although it was the intent of the
Superintendent to concentrate on the completion of the K-12 Social Studies program,
finalize K-12 Testing recommendations, and to initiate the K-12 Reading Curriculum
coordinating effort this school year, several of our science teachers became very

from science experts and have been diligently working towards the coordination and
revision of science K-12. The type of "self-starting," genuine enthusiasm, and
commitment to excellence displayed by our science teachers is what exemplifies our
staff and distinguishes our teachers from the norm.
STRATEGIC PLANNING
In the meanwhile, the Strategic Planning Task Force, comprised of staff,
administrators, community members and parents has been meeting one or twice month-
1y. The Task Force's mission is to "hammer out" a grand design or five year plan
for the direction of the Lunenburg Public Schools. The recommendations of the Task
Force will be brought back to the original Planning Team (also comprised of edu-
cators, parents, and community members) for their input and approval. The Planning
Team will then pass their recommendations on to the Lunenburg School Committee for
final approval.
Sub-Groups
The Strategic Planning Task Force, coincidentally, independent of the school
administration, appears to be heading in the same general philosophical direction
the Administrative Team, School Committee, and staff are pursuing. The Task Force
is proposing increasing systems of accountability, coordinating all subject matter,
supporting and training of existing staff, and empowering teachers.
It appears that all efforts of the School Committee, staff, and administration
are "dovetailing" nicely with what is being proposed for the Lunenburg Schools of
the future.
Once the Strategic Plan is determined and acted upon by the School Committee,
it will become a "living document" which will be reviewed and possibly altered by
the Planning Team (based on perceived need) on a yearly basis.
REGIONALIZATION
After a careful study of the possibility of Lunenburg and Shirley joining to
form a region, the Lunenburg Regionalization Study Committee recommended to the
1988 Lunenburg Town Meeting to form a Regional Planning Committee. Since the
Shirley Regionalization Study Committee also recommended the formation of a
Regionalization Planning Committee at their Town Meeting, the two committees joined
to form a Regionalization Planning Board. This board made up of Jane DeNike
(Lunenburg) and Dot Wilbur (Shirley) co-chairpersons, members Richard Beardmore and
Martha Moore from Lunenburg and Roy Jeannotte and Michael Johnson from Shirley, was
charged with developing an implementation plan for a Lunenburg-Shirley Regional
School District. This plan will then be presented to the communities of Lunenburg
and Shirley at Special Town Meetings. The Towns will then be asked to vote to
either accept or reject the Implementation Plan. Should both towns vote to accept
the plan, Lunenburg and Shirley will join together to form a school region.
Lunenburg is grappling with a low enrollment at the high school level which,
coupled with the Town's inability to adequately fund the schools and all Town
Departments (under the existing tax structure), makes it almost impossible to main-
tain a comprehensive high school. The low enrollment also makes it difficult to
justify the maintenance of many of our essential high school programs.

Elementary School Principal's Report
(William F. McSheehy)
The definition of a comprehensive high school merely is a high school that
offers programs for students of all aptitudes and abilities; nothing fancy, only a
high school that would educate all future citizens of Lunenburg. These programs
would include basic skills courses, advanced placement or high level courses such
as calculus, or advanced biology, business courses, and/or courses in industrial
technology.
Because the per pupil cost of maintaining even basic programs is high when
enrollment is low we have had to cut in many areas and have become dangerously
close to losing our status as a comprehensive high school. Unless something is
done to bring in additional students to Lunenburg High School and to more ade-
quately fund the schools, we will not be able to maintain our comprehensiveness,
and, therefore, not be able to provide an educational program for all the young
people who enter Lunenburg High School.
Regionalization is one means of bringing in additional students and better
funding the schools without placing an additional burden on the townspeople. If
Lunenburg and Shirley decide to form a regional school district, the new region
will qualify for Chapter 71 funds or Incentive Regional Aid.
If after careful study a positive recommendation comes from the Regionalization
Planning Board regarding the formation of a Lunenburg-Shirley Regional School Dis-
trict and the towns so choose to implement, the problems of the maintenance of a
high school in Lunenburg will be solved and both towns will be relieved of a some-
what significant financial and moral burden and obligation.

COMPUTER
In the elementary school we have two very different computer systems available
to the children.
Apple IIe's are housed in the classrooms for use by all students grades pre-
school through four.
In addition to the Apples, we have the CAI system which assists instruction.
All children in grades two, three and four are scheduled daily. This system
builds, records and self-adjusts a child's individual program, according to his
success level.
T. C. Passios has been chosen as a demonstration site to other systems. This
will afford us the opportunity to upgrade our hardware and enlarge upon our educa-
tional opportunities.
THEME PROGRAM ADOPTED
A school-wide theme program was adopted at the Passios School this year with
each theme representing a focus area to be used in all classes and special areas
during a seven week cycle. Selected themes include self-esteem, friendship, school
as a community, caring and sharing, and recreation and leadership. Activities have
taken place within classrooms and throughout the school, and there have been some
joint efforts between the Elementary and Middle Schools. It is hoped that this
program will foster a better feeling of self within each child and, at the same
time, will support an atmosphere of sensitivity and caring towards other people.
ACHIEVEMENT MONITORING PROGRAM
The Achievement Monitoring Programs continue to provide valuable information
and feedback to children, parents, and teachers. Children in grades K-4 partici-
pate in the Math Achievement Monitoring Program, and their progress is checked six
times per year on grade level objectives. Computer printouts provide the students
and their parents with specific information stating their correct and incorrect
items. As the year progresses the child's mastery of certain objectives and those
areas needing reinforcement becomes evident. Teachers are able to individualize
and group children according to the results provided in their class printouts.
Children who master the objectives and skills at their level are able to progress
to the next level.
A similar A.M.P. in English is also established. Students in grade one through
four are tested on grade level objectives four times per year. Objectives tested
include mechanics and usage skills. Computerized feedback is also provided for
these results.
In addition, children's progress in written composition is examined four times
per year in grades one through four. A sample of each student's writing is rated
by his or her teacher based on specific grade level criteria. The skills rated
include compositional skills as well as mechanics and usage skills. An overall
rating is also assigned to each sample. Each of the samples and ratings remain in
the student's writing folder and accumulate from year to year. This provides a
clear profile of each student's progress in writing.

CURRICULUM
The regular curriculum areas have experienced little change this year. We,
however, introduced Alternative Education into our regular education program on a
pilot basis. The clientele in this program are children of better than average
ability who have not demonstrated their potential due to behavioral or emotional
considerations. It is our hope to continue and expand this program in the future.
Through a shift in personnel from the high school to the middle school, we have
acquired the services of a reading specialist. This has allowed us the opportunity
to offer remediation in reading to the students in grades six, seven and eight.
Expanded guidance services for eighth grade students continue this year.
Students have taken part in group counseling activities revolving around drug and
alcohol abuse. This is a continuing program under the supervision of Mr. Anthony
Falcone, grade eight guidance counselor.
Instrumental music is now taught during the school day. Lessons and small
group ensembles are offered in lieu of studies in grades six, seven and eight.
Large group instruction, such as the concert, immediate and beginning bands, as
well as lessons, continues to be offered after school in an extended day program.
ACTIVITIES
Student activities have taken on a blend of academic support and social enrich-
ment. Students have the opportunity to visit such places as the Eastern State's
Exposition, Boston's Freedom Trail, Museum of Fine Arts, Museum of Science, Lexing-
ton and Concord and the Lowell National Park. Trips to Quebec, Canada and Washing-
ton, D.C. are available for students in grades seven and eight.
Assembly programs include topics on child safety, substance abuse, story-
telling, geography bee, creative writing, musical programs, international themes
and career choices.
After school activities are well subscribed. Students participate in skiing
programs, computer club, International club, sporting activities, drama, newspaper
and choral/instrumental music.

High School Principal's Report (Robert A. Jarvis)
Testing Results
The results of the standardized tests, i.e., S.A.T.'s, state-mandated Basic
Skills and Assessment test, once again indicate that Lunenburg High offers a
quality education for all of its students.
In September, the College Board released the results of the Scholastic Aptitude
tests (S.A.T.'s); Lunenburg High School seniors outscored their peers nationally in
verbal scores by twenty-three points (451 to 428) and in math by thirteen points
(489 to 476); Lunenburg High School seniors outdid their peers in Massachusetts by
nineteen points in verbal and fifteen points in mathematics.
On the Massachusetts Basic Skills test Lunenburg High School's performance
reflects the quality of our academic program. The Commonwealth set a mythical
standard of sixty-five percent (65%). All communities are measured against that
standard. Ninety-seven percent (97%) of our students passed the reading component;
ninety-five percent (95%) passed the math component, and ninety-eight (98%) passed
the writing component of this exam. These are individual test performance results
and they show that L.H.S. students are mastering the basic skills of "reading,
writing and arithmetic."
Massachusetts also measures a school system's program quality against a mythi-
cal standard this set of exams are known as the State Assessment Tests. Each
community is measured against other "like" communities throughout the state. Stu-
dents in grade twelve were tested in reading, math, science, and social studies.
The assessment tests differ from the Basic Skills Tests in that the Basic Skills
tests are individual measures of achievement and the Assessment tests are program
evaluation measures. The average score for Massachusetts communities is 1300 in
each category; for Lunenburg the Reading score was 1410 (110 points higher); in
Math the score for Lunenburg was 1390 (90 points higher); in Science our score was
1420 (120 points higher) and for Social Studies our score was 1420 (120 points
higher). In sum, our results indicate a strong academic program.
Extra and Co-Curricular Programs
L.H.S. prides itself on offering a full range of extra-curricular activities
for our students. The other half of education is the enrichment opportunities a
school can provide for its students. At the Certamen (Massachusetts Latin competi-
tion) Lunenburg High School did very well garnering twenty-eight (28) awards and
one of our students, Matthew Williams, earned a summa cum laude certificate and a
gold medal.
Of the seventy thousand (70,000) students who take the exam at the Certamen
only seven hundred (700) or 1% earn gold medals.
During the school year, high school students were provided with many enrich-
ment/educational opportunities through field trips. Because of budget constraints,
students themselves paid for these trips or outside funding such as the School
Improvement Council paid for the transportation. Some of the places our students
visited included: The Kennedy Library, the Worcester Art Museum, the North Shore
Music Circus, the Boston Museum of Science, the Federal Reserve Bank in Boston,
Sage-Allen facility in Leominster, the Gardner State Prison, the Leominster
District Boston. Court; the National Seashore Park in Wellfleet and the Computer Museum in
Assembly programs at the high school included a performance by Jay DiPrima as
Gandhi (funded by the Massachusetts Arts Lottery); Careers for Women in Science;

Reconciliation and the bringing people together instead of building borders.
