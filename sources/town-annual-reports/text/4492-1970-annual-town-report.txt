This book is respectfully of dedicated to the Memory
Carl W. Proctor
Born in Lunenburg and a Selectman since 1967, Carl retired in 1964, after forty-one years service,
from the New England Telephone & Telegraph Company in Boston where he was Plant Extension
Manager. He actively operated a dairy farm in this community. Although retired, Carl devoted
his time and talents not only to matters of municipal government of which he had a most progressive
attitude and knowledge, but to the many civic and professional organizations of which he was a
member. He was a gentleman in the finest sense of the word and, therefore, not only possessed
integrity among other virtues, but used it as his personal guideline in his many activities.

Board of Selectmen
Many Areas of "Responsibility"
THEMPSON
F. CLIFTON MILLER
RUDOLPH MASELLA
Board of Selectmen: left to right - Rudolph Masella, Chairman Normand J. Thompson and F. Clifton Miller.
Lunenburg, Worcester County, Massachusetts, population approximately 7500, 29 square mile area, 82.79
miles of road, a typical middle-class New England suburban community. The average visitor, reporter or resident
would think this about our Town. Lunenburg Selectmen have different thoughts of our Town. They are more apt to
think of thirty-five departments, appointed offices and committees which are their direct responsibility.
They think in terms of Monday night regular meetings lasting on an average of four to four and one-halfhours,
approximately seventy-five to eighty meetings of all types per year--budgets, dog complaints, building and Board
of Health permits, licenses (all kinds), street light requests, taxes, drugs, expansion of all departments, new industry,
snow removal, new highway construction, drainage problems, housebreaks, new townbuilding needs, bombing threats,
town sewerage, sanitary landfill and many more items too numerous to mention--any and all of which might come up
in forty to fifty items on the agenda during their Monday night meetings.
They see the Town as big business and little business, borrowing $900,000.00 for various purposes during the
year, approving payment of $3,908,299.12 in fifty-seven warrants, everything from a new highway garage to
paper clips.
In 1970, Lunenburg continued to grow one hundred and six zoning permits were issued, new roads were
accepted by the Town and more are in the offing. The P. J. Keating Company was granted permission to proceed
with a $3,000,000 expansion program. The five year street lighting programstarted in 1965 was completed, and for
the time being no extensive revisions to this program appear necessary. Renovations to the Town Hall (final phases)
were begun and hopefully the program will be completed in the near future.

The Town was presented a problem of major proportion when the State Department of Public Health informed US
that because of the national pollution problem, Lunenburg was to cease operation of its present town dump by July 1,
1971. The Selectmen have given many hours of deliberation to this problem and are at present in the process of dis-
cussing the solution of the solid waste disposal problem (perhaps on a regional basis) with several neighboring towns.
In July, the Board and the entire Town was saddened by the sudden death of our brother member, Carl W.
Proctor. Carl, although not a young man, brought to the office a progressive and renovation-oriented approach
coupled with years of executive experience--a rare combination. Carl was a decidedly valuable asset to the Town of
Lunenburg. His death created the need for the first special election in Lunenburg for many years, and the election
of F. Clifton Miller as the new Selectman. This death served to point out the wisdom and advantage of having
full-time secretary to the Board. Richard J. Eley, our secretary, was invaluable during the time the Board operated
shorthandedly. The Town is indeed fortunate to have Cf man of his calibre and ability.
We wish to express our appreciation to our fellow townspeople for their cooperation and understanding of the
problems of the Selectmen in attempting to fill a complex position on a part-time basis.
Board of Assessors
Board of Assessors: John F. Nicoll, Clerk Kathleen M. Deming, Chairman Leroy Allen and Chester J. Pliska.
In 1970 Lunenburg went to full and fair valuation. This was accomplished by revaluation of the properties by
the firm of Whipple-Magane-Darcy, Inc. These results were later reviewed by the Assessors, a land committee's
report was received and these recommendations were taken into consideration before determination of final valuations.
Less than 10% of the taxpayers filed for an abatement. In the opinion of the present Board of Assessors, the present
values are much more equitable and proof of this has been shown in recent sales.
The Tax Commissioner assigned a valuation for Lunenburg of fifty-three and one-half million dollars. Through
several appeals made by the Assessors it was reduced to fifty and one-half million for the years 1971 and 1972.
It was with deep regret that last year's Chairman, Arthur J. Brockelman, Sr., and a member of the Board of
Assessors for the last nine years, decided that he would not seek office again. His long and devoted service and his
interest in the community should not be overlooked. We would like to take this opportunity to say to Arthur-
"Well Done".

Town Clerk, Tax Collector, Treasurer
A Measurement of Growth
One way of measuring the growth of our Town in the last two decades is by
comparing the activity, functions and complexity of duties of the Town Clerk's
Tax Collector's and Treasurer's Offices during 1970 with corresponding items for
1950. Although the Federal Census figures for 1970 have not been verified, it is
agreed that our 1950 population of 3906 has just about doubled.
Town Government like everything else has become more and more complex
as the General Court over the years has added constantly to the duties and com-
plexities, problems, authority and responsibilities of the various Town Offices and
Officers. This is particularly true in the case of the Town Clerk, Tax Collector and
Treasurer whose duties stem from, are interlocked with and come under the juris-
diction of many segments of State and County Government including the Secretary
of State, Commissioners of Corporations and Taxation, Director of Accounts, At-
Mary L. Padula, Town Clerk,
torney General and the County Commissioners.
Tax Collector, Treasurer
As a result, on almost adaily basis, Town Clerks are now required to make decisions, interpretations and judge-
ments of a quasi-legal nature. As an example, the laws relating to elections and Town Meetings are most complex.
In the case of Town Meetings, failure to comply with literally hundreds of legal requirements can often invalidate
actions taken by the citizens in Town Meetings or cause challenges which result in court actions. Similarly, failure
to observe any one of the hundreds of relating to the registration of voters, the conduct of elections or the cer-
tification of election results, can erupt in legal problems. The fact that 190 Senate Bills and 43 House Bills, all
pertaining to elections, are presently pending before our State Legislators demonstrates the degree of change to which
these functions are subjected. Other areas which frequently involve the Town Clerk in on-the-spot judgements relate
to the administration of the Commercial Code, adoption of By-laws, Zoning and Subdivision Control and Vital Records
pertaining to births, marriages and deaths. The Town Clerk relates to nearly every Town Official in some way, as
well as to all Boards and Committees. As Town Government has become more and more complex the number of spe-
ial committees with which this office works and interrelates has also considerably increased.
The collection of taxes is strictly governed by State Laws and these laws, too, have greatly increased in number
and are subject to constant revision. Although the poll tax has fallen by the wayside, our citizens hardly need to
be reminded of the drastic increases which have occurred in other tax areas in the last twenty years. For whereas the
total motor vehicle excise collected in 1950 amounted to $23,166, the corresponding figure in 1970 was $229, 553
and whereas total taxes collected in 1950 amounted to $260,323, the total taxes collected in 1970 amounted to
$1,828,796. (A prudent Tax Collector would stop right here and explain that the Collector has absolutely nothing
to do with determining the amount of taxes to be paid. The gross amount to be raised by taxation is determined by
the voters at Town Meeting and State law governs what receipts and reimbursements can be used as offsets. It then
becomes the responsibility of the Board of Assessors to establish a net amount to be raised and, based on the values
which they assign to each parcel of property, to set the tax rate and determine how much each taxpayer's share will
represent.)
Although Bond Issues in connection with School Construction, establishing eligibility for State and Federal
Grants, more refined and detai budgeting procedures, the adoption of a Salary Administration Plan, and a multiplicity
of accounting functions related to the receiving and disbursing of funds have added to the scope and complexity of
the Town Treasurer's Office, accounting procedures prescribed under the State System of Accounts have changed very
little. As amatter of fact, it might almost be said that the State Accounting System, to which we must strictly adhere,
is archaic and should most certainly be updated to encompass more modern accounting methods and machinery. The
State Auditing Department has estimated that it takes new accounting officers at least five years to become thoroughly
proficient in this unique system. The amount of monies received and precisely accounted for in 1950 totalled only from
$448, 908 as compared to a corresponding figure for 1970 of $4,936,297 and total disbursements increased
$384,967 to $4,808,299. The scope and volume of work currently performed in the Treasurer's Office give further
testimony to the continuing growth of our Town.

Library Trustees
Resources for all Ages
Library Trustees: left to right-Janet J. Cragin, Jeanne L. Crocker, George R. Wallace 3rd,
Beatrice M. Fay, Carey D. Rhoten and Robert S. Tapply.
Your Town Library continues to perform its unique function as the one, unbiased, non-partisan bureau of in-
formation for all the people. Trustees and staff endeavor to maintain conditions so that townspeople of all ages will
feel welcome and "at home" in their library, will find the resources they need and enjoy a respect for one another
that will be of mutual benefit to everyone.
Circulation figures are as follows for the year 1970: adult books 16, 189, children's books 25,388, magazines
and pamphlets 1,698, for a total of 43,275. An increase in magazine circulation was offset by declines inboth adult
and children sections. Adult levels of circulation have remained relatively constant over the past several years,
but the circulation of children's books has declined as the school library programs are expanded. There isgreater
use of the library as a reference and study center in the evenings, so although circulation figures are down by 1,706
for the year, attendance is up.
Regional book truck deposits at this library numbered 582. One hundred forty-six regional films were shown
in Lunenburg and 117 interlibrary loan requests were completed for individual library patrons. The library purchased
500 adult books and 384 children's books. The McNaughton Rental Collection was increased to 500 volumes which
means an automatic exchange of 50 books per month. Discarded or "weeded" were 157 adult books and 184 children's
books. According to approved policy these are for sale at 25c a copy.

Town Water Study Committee
At the Annual Town Meeting held in March of 1969, it was voted to appoint a Committee of seven registered
voters to investigate the feasibility or desirability of the Town taking over the operations of the Lunenburg Water
District as a Town department. Laurence Ebersole, Wilbur Barney, William Clapp, R. William Brown, Jr., Ray
MacCracken and Orrin Foss were chosen by the Selectmen and Moderator, and Joseph Kibling was chosen by the
Board of Water Commissioners to serve on this Committee. At the 1970 Annual Town Meeting it was voted to continue
the study. Laurence Ebersole and R. William Brown, Jr. resigned from the Committee and Ernest Dow and George
Larkin were appointed by the Selectmen and Moderator to take their place.
During the course of its study this Committee met at intervals with the Board of Water Commissioners, the Board
of Selectmen, the Industrial & Development Commission, the Planning Board, the Fire Chief and the Conservation
Commission. It was also represented at a series of meetings regarding the extension of water mains on Route 13 in
connection with the planned expansion of industrial development on the so-called Niemi property.
The legal aspects of placing the operation of the Water Department under the control of the Town were dis-
cussed at length with the Town Counsel, Robert J. Larkin, and the financial aspects with representatives of the State
Street Bank of Boston. An in-depth study of the functioning of the existing Water District was conducted. An analysis
and evaluation of growth potential and future expansion of the water lines was made with respect to the existing set-
up as compared to the considered conversion. Immediate needs and long-range planning were taken into consideration.
After careful consideration of all the information at hand and of the opinions of the members of the various
departments and boards with which it met, this Committee concluded as follows:
1. That the Lunenburg Water District, as it exists, is an extremely efficient, well run organization.
2. That the existing set-up has, and will, continue to permit desired expansion programs.
3. That, therefore, it would not be desirable, practical or economically sound for the Town to take over this
department at the present time.
4. That the Conservation Commission should acquire and set aside on a continuingbasis parcels of land which
are contained in the 4000 acres which constitute the Town's watershed.
5. That the best interests of the Town will be served by having a new Committee appointed in two years to
reassess these findings.
In conclusion, this Committee wishes to thank the various Boards and Committees which parti cipated in this
study.

This was accomplished when a public inspection was held at the new housingsite at 131 White Street on De-

elderly tenants.
When inflationary costs were at an all time high for building, there was no turning back, for this project had
gone through all preliminary phases and had been authorized. However, several economies were made tobring the
costs down, namely-eliminating brick siding and simplifying the design. White stucco was chosen for the six apartment

peted floors and all-electric service.

for them at entrances. Special precautions were taken to comply with public health standards for sewage disposal,
and in fact the site was chosen because of the excellent percolation found in the soil. Of the ten available sites,
the Central sites failed to rate as acceptable.

Conservation Commission & Town Forest Committee
Environmental Awareness
In 1681 William Penn decreed that for every four cultivated acres in Philadelphia one acre must be left in
woodland. What good sense Mr. Penn had, and how prophetic are his recommendations for today! Experts not only
agree and recommend that every fifth acre should be in woodland, but also tell US that for every managed acre of
woodland a ton of wood plus a ton of oxygen is produced every year. Mr. Penn probably did not realize the full
implication of his sage advice, but he did know that there had to be a proper balance left for a healthy, as well as
esthetic, community.
The year 1970 has been heralded as the year of Environmental Awareness because of the widespread concern
regarding pollution, over-population, poisons, herbicides, trash, noise, etc. Finally after some seventy years in
the blind faith and false security that technology can solve all problems, Man has come to realize that he is, indeed,
a very real part of the eco system (just one small link in the cycle of nature) and that he cannot ignore this natural
process.
Lunenburg's Town Forest Committee and Conservation Commission has over the past ten years directed their
efforts to the planned acquisition of marshland and marginal land for the preservation and enjoyment of the people
of Lunenburg. Much credit for this programand its implementation must be given to the townspeople themselves who
are aware of the value of green areas, wildlife, and woodlands and have supported the program.
During 1970 forty-eight additional acres of land were acquired. The Chase Wildlife Area, forty acres of
swampy and steep land on Chase Road, and eightacres of swamp and woodland abutting the Cowdrey Nature Center,
were purchased.
In addition to land acquisition and the supervision of town-owned conservation lands, the Commission hasrep-
resented Lunenburg at three state-wide conservation meetings, one state-wide soil conservation meeting, six Nashua
River meetings, a Montachusett Regional Planning meeting, twelve regular local meetings as well as taking part in
Lunenburg High School's excellent Earth Day in April. The Commission continues to be impressed and gratified with
the active interest and support of both students and teachers in the quest of a stable and better environment.
The following is quoted from a letter dated December 30, 1970, from Mr. Arthur W. Brownell, Commissioner,
Department of Natural Resources, which was sent to Mrs. Nancy Sholl, Chairman of the Conservation Commission.
"On behalf of the Board of Natural Resources and this Depart-
ment, I am happy to forward this check for $1,050.00 to the Town of
Lunenburg as the Commonwealth's share in your recent openspace land
acquisition.
You are to be commended for your continuing effort which has
made your Commission a leader in open space planning & acquisition.
We hope that this Department may continue to assist you in your pro-
jected acquisition program."

Fire Department
"Dry Hydrant"
During 1970 the Fire Department answered 209 calls as follows: Rescue Calls 55;
Brush Fires 42; Service Calls 36; Building Fires 19; Car Fires 15; Accidents 13; Town
Dump 12; Mutual Aid 8; Bomb Scare 3; Asphalt Plant 1; Dog Rescues 2; Chimney
Fire 1; Railroad Car Fire 1; False Alarm 1; Accidental Alarm 1. Of these calls,
92 were alarms. Our Rescue Unit answered 55 requests and transported 51 patients.
Requests for 1970 numbered one less than 1969. The Rescue Unit is operated pri-
marily between 10:00 P.M. to 7:00 A.M. with the remaining hours being covered
by a private service. The Rescue Unit Squad strength is the same as in 1969 -seven
men on each of two squads alternating on calls. All squad members hold an American
Red Cross Advanced First Aid Certificate and attend an advanced refresher course
annually. The Rescue Unit is certified by the Massachusetts Department of Public
Health by anannual inspection. Three squad members attended a four day course in
Boston on the emergency care and transportation of the sick and injured held by the
American Academy of Orthopedic Surgeons.
The Fire Department held its bi-weekly meetings, drills and educational pro-
grams with attendance running at an all time high. The Department was well repre-
Fire

men attending. The Town's first dry hydrant put into service in 1970 was a big success. It makes available 700

as there are twelve to fifteen locations in Town where they would be advantageous.
Our equipment is in excellent condition and no replacement is planned for a few years. In order for our De-
partment to keep pace with the growth of the Town, additional space is needed. Our floor space is now filled to

also need space for storage and drying of hose, wash bay and room for the replacement of equipment.

Police Department
Citizen Cooperation - "A Police Aid"
Bombing threats, an incident of shooting at a police
officer, increases in breaks into private homes and
businesses, and the necessity of more specialized police
work, added to the multitude of complaints, ranging
from social problems to criminal acts, that confronted
the Police Department in 1970. Manned by the regular
force of Chief, Sergeant and three Patrolmen, an addi-
tional seasonal man was used in the summer to provide
coverage on Whalom Lakefront and during the critical
night-time hours.
The criteria for the Police Department should be
based on need as well as costs. The need is for ade-
quate protection for the Town of Lunenburg and for men
and equipment to efficiently and effectively meet the
demands placed upon our Department in these times.
Thought should be given to providing added manpower
to conduct special investigations, to serve as safety
officers and to perform duties that cannot be handled
realistically by cruiser officers on regular assignments.
All full-time members of the Department and some
12 reserve officers attended a series of classes on
fingerprinting with Lt. Richard Darcy of the Fitchburg
Police Department as instructor, Special training courses
Chief of Police, Frederick B. Hobbs
on narcotics were attended by three regular officers
with others slated to participate as the oursesbecome
available.
The traffic rules and regulations of the Town were amended and upgraded with previous regulations having been
in effect since 1955. Parking tickets became a first for our Town and they have helped control parking in the Center
and at Whalom Park. To further relieve the congestion and to provide more parking space in the Center, removal of
a small portion of the church lawn and of the Center Common is recommended. The new traffic rules made possible
the replacement and erection of new traffic signs with many yet to be installed. Other traffic control measures
recommended are the construction of a traffic island at the intersection of Townsend Harbor and Mulpus Roads and
the reconstruction of an island on Page Street and Lancaster Avenue.
Replacement of all incandescent lighting with modern vapor lights was completed in 1970, a project that was
spaced over a five year period. In the future, additional lighting will be based on need as determined by the Board
of Selectmen and Police Department.
The number of motor vehicle accidents decreased this past year due in part to the effective use of radar and to
a stricter enforcement of motor vehicle laws. Radar has also been used to control speeding on various secondary
streets thus reducing the number of complaints from residents relative to speeders. The Town will receive reimburse-
ment of $9,350 by taking advantage of state and federal grants for the purchase of school traffic lights and for radar
and radio equipment.

It is widely believed that any widening or straightening of roads, or opening up
of junctions is a road improvement. This is not necessarily true. The basic reasons for
improving roads are simply--to improve traffic flow and to reduce accidents. The aim
must always be to achieve both of these objectives, and the value of an improvement
must be measured accordingly.
The public is invariably misguided in the matter of road hazards, and to yield to
such pressures merely results in a non-improvement. Non-improvements can be seen
everywhere. An example is the realignment of the roadway without improving the sight
distances to match. Traffic speeds will increase, and so will accidents! An investigation
by the Road Research Laboratory on 16 sites where roads were resurfaced and better riding
quality was produced without improvements in other features showed an increase of 30%
in accidents. Resurfacing should, therefore, be accompanied by other measures, such as
improving skid resistance, better direction signing and the adoption of a higher standard
Richard W. Brown
of street lighting. Above all, in the selection of our roadworks, we should ensure that
Highway Superintendent
due priority be given to those which will prevent the most accidents.
The first law of Road Safety is that: The accident record at a site varies inversely with its apparent degree of
danger. There is nothing new in this. Shakespeare expressed the same idea in Hamlet 370 years ago- "Best safety
lies in fear". If all road hazards looked dangerous there would be fewer accidents. This explains the rather dis-
turbing conclusion in a report by the Road Research Laboratory on the investigation of 139 rural three-way junctions
that "the better the vision distance, the higher the accident rate". At junctions where vision distance is ample the
degree of danger is not apparent and caution is abandoned. Careful selection of our roadwork is the cheapest safety
measure of all, since it costs almost nothing.

with definite regulations to prevent further air contamination, especially by the promiscuous burning which produces
air contaminants. Action has been initiated to do away with the infamous and unsanitary "town dump".

to be the modern answer to effective solid waste disposal.

towns ona voluntary basis. These clinicshave been conducted first to protect dogs against the disease and, secondly,
to have the dogs act as a protective barrier for humans.

dog six months of age or older shall cause such a dog to be vaccinated against rabies by a licensed veterinarian using
a vaccine approved by the Department of Public Health". In November of the same year, the Public Health Council
adopted a set of Rules and Regulations to accompany the above law. Essentially, the regulations require that a dog
be immunized with a licensed vaccine and a suitable tag given to the owner to affix on the dog's collar. As a result
of the above law and regulations the number of dogs immunized in the 16 Nashoba towns increased from 1,713 in 1969
to 2,617 in 1970. The number of dogs immunized in Lunenburg during 1970 was 271.

QUESTION NO. 4 - PROPOSED AMENDMENT TO THE CONSTITUTION
Do you approve of the adoption of an amendment to the constitution summarized below, which was approved by the General
Court in a joint session of the two branches held July 15, 1968, received 181 votes in the affirmative and 66 in the negative, and
in a joint session of the two branches held May 15, 1969, received 208 votes in the affirmative and 54 in the negative
Summary - The proposed amendment to the Constitution amends the existing constitutional provisions which direct the division
of the Commonwealth into 240 representative districts and 40 senatorial districts. It provides for a census of the inhabitants of each
city and town in the Commonwealth in the year 1971 and in every tenth year thereafter. The census in 1971 is to be the basis for
determining the representative and senatorial districts for the ten-year period beginning January 1, 1975. Each subsequent census
shall be the basis for determining the districts for each ten-year period beginning the fourth January following each census, so that
the census in 1981 will determine the districts as of January, 1985, etc.
The amendment provides that the house of representatives shall consist of two hundred and forty members and the senate of forty
members. In its first regular session following each census, the Legislatureshall divide the Commonwealth into two hundred and forty
representative districts of contiguous territory and forty senatorial districts also of contiguous territory. Each representative district
shall contain, as nearly as may be, an equal number of inhabitants according to the census. Each senatorial districtshall also contain
as nearly as may be an equal number of inhabitants according to the census. A representative district shall not unite two or more
counties, towns, or cities, or parts thereof, or a city and a town, or parts thereof, in one district unless the same is unavoidable.
Further, no town of less thansix thousand inhabitants shall be divided in forming representative districts. It is also provided that the
County of Dukes County and Nantucket County shall each be a representative district, notwithstanding the foregoing. The senatorial
districts, each of which shall elect one Senator, shall be formedwithout uniting two counties or parts of two or more counties, unless
the same is unavoidable.
Each representative shall be an inhabitant of the district for which he is chosen at least one year immediately preceding his
election, and each senatorshall be an inhabitant of the district for which heis chosen at the time of his election and shall have been
on inhabitant of the Commonwealth for at least five years immediately preceding his election. Any representative or senator who
ceases to be an inhabitant of the Commonwealth shall cease to represent his district.
The amendment also provides that the Legislature (1) may by law limit the time within which judicial proceedings may be in-
stituted calling in question any such division and (2) shall prescribe by law the manner of calling and conducting elections for the
choice of representatives and of ascertaining their election.
Finally, the amendment annuls the existing constitutional provisions with respect to apportionment of representatives andsena-
torial districts, although those provisions will remain in effect until January 1, 1975.

"4-H in Action"
The year 1970 saw a further surge in 4-H activity in Lunenburg. Although two clubs were dropped, the total
membership has increased to approximately seventy members who belong to six active clubs. The Bridle Path 4-H
Club led by Mrs. Martin Moore, who took over from the founder Lois Camber in the Spring of 1970, now numbers
forty-eight members. All these youngsters own or have the complete care of a horse.
Other operating clubs are the Mixed Breeds Dairy Club led by Mrs. Edmond Bourque, the Kitchen Kapers Food
Club led by Mrs. Roger Raboin, the Foods and Sewing Club led by Mrs. Henry Anderson, a new Foods Club started
by Mrs. Agnes Price and the Pie Crust Kids Foods Club led by Mrs. Dean Gauntlett.
The year began with a 4-H Exhibit and Demonstration Night heldon January 30th in the United Parish Building.
Mr. & Mrs. Leo Jarkko, 4-H leaders in Fitchburg, were the judges for the demonstrations. On February 20th, a
Horse Health and Training Seminar was held in the Parish Building sponsored by the Bridle Path Club. On March
20th, several local members travelled to Worcester to participate in the County Demonstration Run-Offs.
On April 11th, the members of the Bridle Path Club attended a 4-H Horse Clinic held at the Townshend-
Morgan Farm in Bolton on the fundamentals of Fitting and Showmanship. In April Mrs. Howard Camber,along with the
4-H judging team, travelled out to the University of Massachusetts to attend a State Leaders Field Day held in Grinnell
Arena. This Field Day was sponsored by the Department of Veterinary and Animal Sciences and the Division of 4-H
and Youth Programs at the University.
The first of two horse clinics was held on May 29th with Dr. James T. Wright administering the shots for
tetanus, shipping fever and encephalitis. The second clinic to complete the series of innoculations was held on
June 19th. On June 13th, eleven local members attended the Junior Northeast Achievement Day held at Camp
Marshall in Spencer. All members participated in demonstrations and exhibits. They were accompanied by Mrs.
Thomas Currie and Mrs. Dean Gauntlett. Lunenburg members participated in the County 4-H Auction held on June
3rd by collecting and transporting items to the event held in Sterling. On June 14th, the Bridle Path Club sponsored
a 4-H Horse Show held at the North Leominster Rod & Gun Club which featured twenty-seven English and Western
classes. The response from other clubs was tremendous. The show was under the direction of Mrs. Howard Camber
and Mrs. Martin Moore.
Mrs. Ruth Anderson began her club in October of 1969 as a foods project. In February of 1970, her members
wanted to sewand started with culottes which were exhibited at the annual Grange Fair. The club has now expanded
to some ten members and includes both cooking and sewing. In the Summer of 1970, Richard J. Bishop ,a teacher
in the Lunenburg School System, was officially appointed as a Trustee of the Lunenburg 4-H Fund. The two other
Trustees are Roger Bigelow and Raymond Proctor. Other active members of the 4-H Town Committee include: Jean
Stockwell, Theresa Maki, Marcie Gauntlett, Susie Logan, Helene Bishop, Lois Camber, Carolyn Jokinen, Marjorie
Stillman, Richard Dustin, Ruth Anderson, Yvonne Bourque, Gloria Currie, Diane Moore and Barbara Raboin.
The Mixed Breeds Dairy Club under the supervision of Mrs. Yvonne Bourque participated in the 19th Annual
4-H Dairy Tour and Judging Contest which took place in the summer. The Don Rovin Farm in Sterling wasvisited
where Jersey judging was held and the Chedco Farm in Berlin where Guernsey judging was carried out. The group
then went to St. Benedict Center in Harvard where Holstein judging was held. The Lunenburg judging team consisted
of Judith, Carol and Karen Bourque and Martha Paquin.
October 4th to the 10th was proclaimed as National 4-H Week across the country and saw the beginning of the
Lunenburg 4-H Cookie Sale with Jean Stockwell again as Chairman. Twenty-six members of the Horse Club rode in
the Homecoming Parade on October 17th.
Officers elected in October for the 1970-1971 year were Chairman, Mrs. Dean Gauntlett; Secretary, Mrs.
Robert Stockwell and Treasurer, Mrs. Theresa Maki.

On August 1,1971, Dr. William R. Holland came to Lunenburg as Superintendent of Schools.

including teaching, administration, school building problems, and curricula & program develop-

Under Dr. Holland's leadership, the Lunenburg School System should be a dynamic, ever
improving, superior system. Our progress will be a studied, carefully planned, and thoroughly
carried out process involving administrators, faculty, students, as well as segments from the
community at large. His style, his appreciation of people and the part each plays, his warmth and
sincerity have already been experienced by many.
The School Committee knows that with a Superintendent of Dr. Holland's character, ability,
and background, coupled with an effective faculty, staff and administration, Lunenburg will
continue to achieve the educational goals which we know each citizen of Lunenburg desires for
his children.

THEMES OF THE 70' S
Looking back on 1970 and ahead to 1971 there are two basic issues that dominate the educational scene in
the beginning of this new decade. The first issue deals with financial support of education especially on the local
level. The major problems associated with school support through the local property tax are numerous and quite
complex. The fiscal crisis has arrived and won't disappear until other alternatives for school support are found.
In short, the local property tax can no longer support or in the future sustain our present educational program. A
major tax overhaul is needed on the State and National levels. Recently, there are hopeful indications on both
the State and local level that some tax reform is in sight. Such indications are welcome because it is becoming
near impossible to administer the schools of the urban-suburban 1970's under fiscal systems of the agrarian 1920's
and 30's. A positive by-product of this financial squeeze is a demand by taxpayers to have our school system run
more efficiently and with more creative skill. Accountability has suddenly become the in-phrase in educational

The Elementary Annex
A critical look has been taken this year at the Elementary Annex, a 1925 facility that currently houses 180
third grade youngsters and 7 teachers. A 25-page position paper that was prepared by our staff and presented to
the School Committee outlined a five (5) year long-range plan to update the Annex both physically and educa-
tionally. The plan, a result of several months of study, calls for spreading necessary expenditures in areas of
heating, sound-proofing, plumbing, electricity, etc., over aperiod time--many of these expenditures, e. g.,
a hot lunch program, have been mandated by the State while others are required or very strongly urged by the
State Building Inspector. Many recommendations contained in the report also call for changes which require very
little or no money. The need for updating the Annex is best explained in the Introduction of the report:
"The purpose of this study was to carefully study and assess the Elementary School Annex in terms of its
physical and structural adequacy as well as its educational adequacy. Is this a building that can physically con-
tinue to be used in the years ahead? Can the building serve the demands of changing times and directions in edu-
cation? Can it meet the "minimum" educational quality standards set by the State for elementary school programs

in the 1970's and 1980's? Can the Town of Lunenburg afford to close the Annex and undertake a new building
program that would include placing an addition on the Passios School and possibly an addition on the High School?
A search for some answers to these questions was the major reason for undertaking this study.
"After a careful review and as a result of the data compiled in this report, it is our professional opinion
that:
(I) Lunenburg is in no financial or psychological position to undertake a new building program.
(This didn't take much study to determine.)
(2) The Elementary School Annex with an agreed upon long-range plan of renovation and improve-

adequate elementary educational facility for the next fifteen years. This assumption is based

on the secondary level due to the opening of the Montachusett Regional Vocational School and
to some internal scheduling changes we could make to accommodate any slight or unanticipated
enrollment increases in this period of time. The assumption is also based upon the fact that a

SCHOOL COMMITTEE ACTIVITIES
To say that 1970 was a busy, hectic and rewarding year for School Committee members would indeed be an
understatement. In addition to hiring a new Superintendent, the Committee again spent considerable time
involved in the collective bargaining process. Of particular note were the contract negotiations with Lunenburg
custodians.
Custodians joined the AFL-CIO Custodial Union last Spring and contract negotiations began with the Union
representative and our custodians in June. A two-year, forty-one page master contract settlement was signed in
late December, a settlement that represented many clock hours on the part of all parties, especially school
committee members who met separately almost weekly for this purpose. The end result of the negotiations was a
very fair settlement that represents the best interests of the town, the school system, and the custodians.
Currently, the School Committee and Lunenburg Teachers' Association are involved in contract negotia-
tions. For reasons stated in the introduction of this report, salary discussions are particularly difficult this year
and at this point there is a possibility that a salary agreement with teachers will not be reached until sometime
after Town Meeting. The possibility of extended salary negotiations is not a happy thought. In the long run, no
95

one actually wins and the school program could suffer. The dilemma the School Committee faces in these times of
rising costs is one of sustaining a sound and modern educational program while at the same time considering the
ability of the town to pay. Unquestionably, public trust and confidence in School Committees is being tested in
an unparalleled way. Fortunately, Lunenburg has a history of enthusiastically supporting the recommendations
and actions of its School Committee. This past trust and confidence is primarily responsible for the fine quality of
education that currently exists in Lunenburg. Such public confidence must continue if we are to maintain and
improve our present educational program. Undue political pressure that challenges or denies the legal authority
of the School Committee can only result in a lowering of educational progress and quality in Lunenburg Schools.
The sincere dedication and commitment of present School Committee members should not go unnoticed. I am con-
fident that the community will continue to give the School Committee their wholehearted support this year as in
past years.

STAN HOWARD
After six years of dedicated service as a school committee member, Mr. Stanley H. Howard is stepping
down. Speaking for my predecessor, Ralph Brown, School Committee members, past and present, and all members
of our school staff, I would like to express our sincere gratitude to Stan for his many fine contributions to the stu-

CONCLUSIONS
As I conclude my first annual report, I would like to personally thank principals, teachers, students, School
Committee members, members of our non-professional staff, and gracious townspeople, for the warm and accept-
ing welcome given me and my family since our arrival in Lunenburg. I feel that the first six months have gone
quite smoothly and I look forward to the future with a genuine feeling of enthusiasm. Special thanks go to Mrs.
Welch, my superb secretary, who has been of invaluable help to me; to Mr. Archambault, our very capable
Supervisor of Buildings and Grounds; and to Mr. Goodrich, my extremely able assistant, who has assumed
increased responsibility since my arrival and handled this responsibility with great skill.
It is a privilege and honor to be part of Lunenburg's educational team. Lunenburg has a school system which
it can point to with pride. I intend to continue and improve upon that tradition.
DR. WILLIAM R. HOLLAND

WILLARD E. POWELL
1965-1970
On March 15, 1965, Willard E. Powell was elected by the Lunen-
burg School Committee to the position of Custodian in the Lunenburg
Jr.-Sr. High School. Willard served in this capacity until his unexpec-
ted death on December 1st of this year. He graduated from Lunenburg
High School in 1938 and served in the Medic division of the U.S. Army
during World War II. Willard performed his duties as School Custodian
with great devotion and sense of pride. He will be sadly. missed by
many.

RETIREMENTS
MRS. GLADYS L. BROWN
1931-1938
1948-1970
Mrs. Brown began her teaching duties with the Lunenburg School
System as Gladys A. Leonard in September 1931 when she was hired as
teacher of Grade I in the old Center School. She remained in this posi-
tion until June of 1938 when she resigned to be married. In November of
1948, when the schools went on double sessions, Mrs. Brown's services
were again engaged, and she became the teacher of a combination Grade
2 & 3 class. The following September, she transferred to the Brooks
House and taught a combination Grade 3 & 4 class, and then from
September 1950 through June 1955, she taught Grade 4.
In September 1955, Lunenburg began its first special class for the men-
tally retarded and at this time, Mrs. Brown assumed the responsibility.
She remained in this position until her retirement, effective August 21,
1970.
The following letter of commendation was sent to her by the Lunenburg School Committee and Superintendent
of Schools and aptly expresses the sentiments of everyone: "The members of the Lunenburg School Committee and
myself, would like to take time to express our genuine appreciation of the exceptional service which you have
rendered to young people over the twenty-nine years you have served in Lunenburg. Your record of service is
enviable. Your quality as a unique person filled with human warmth and dedicated interest on behalf of children,
the schools, and the community, are deeply appreciated."
Although Gladys has "retired" as a permanent full-time teacher, we are happy to see her around the schools
quite frequently as a "substitute".
WILLIAM F. MORAN
1962-1970
"Bill" first began his employment with the School Department on
April 30, 1962, when he was hired as a Custodian in the Jr.-Sr. High
School. When the new Turkey Hill Middle School opened, Bill was
unanimously appointed as the new Senior Custodian by the School
Committee in September 1968, assuming his duties effective December
30, 1968. He remained in this position until December 30,1970, when
it was necessary for him to retire by law.
On January 17, 1971, a Testimonial was held for Bill at the Middle
School where school personnel, relatives, and friends, presented him
with gifts and a money tree. A short program was held at which time
the Chairman of the School Committee presented him with a plaque
honoring his years of dedicated service. Bill Moran's sense of humor,
leadership, and efficiency, will be missed--we wish him much happi-
ness in his retirement years.

School Lunch Program
The school lunch program operated in three
schools during the 1969-70 school year. During this
period of 177 school lunch days, the cafeteria staff
served 226,653 paid student lunches; ,859 free
student lunches and 11,310 adults. These lunches all
followed the "Type A" pattern which is the state
approved, balanced lunch consisting of:
2 oz. of protein
3/4 cup serving of 2 or more vegetables
and fruit, or both
I slice of enriched bread or equivalent
I teaspoon butter or fortified margarine
1/2 pint of whole unflavored milk
C.NIEBEL
Nutritional needs of children require that
special attention be given to include adequate servings
of foods rich in Vitamin C, Vitamin A and iron daily.
In the special milk program at the Elementary Annex, 38,280 half-pints of milk were sold and consumed. In
all four schools, 288, half-pints of milk were purchased and consumed.
The lunch program received $19,949. in Federal Commodities, a big factor in helping our cafeteria operation
remain self-supporting. With rising food and labor costs very few school systems in Massachusetts are able to run
self-supporting program without local subsidy.
Participation-wise, our percentage was 70% in the Jr.-Sr. High School; 65% in the Turkey Hill Middle
School; and 53% at the Elementary School.
The summer school lunch conference at the University of Massachusetts for managers and supervisors was
attended by Mrs. Genevieve Nass, Cook-Manager at the Elementary School and Mrs. Mary Haueisen, Cook-
Manager at the Middle School, and myself. Mrs. Anita Forest, Baker at the Elementary School and Mrs. Gloria
Beaulac, Baker at the Jr.-Sr. High School, attended the conference at Stonehill College for cooks and bakers.
At present, the combined cafeteria staff consists of a cook-manager and baker at each school and fourteen
(14) general workers.
In closing, I wish to express my thanks to Dr. Holland and Mr. Goodrich, the School Committee, principals,
and teaching staff, and the custodians, for their interest and help with the program. I would especially like to
thank the school lunch personnel for their fine work and cheerful cooperation.
Respectfully submitted,
(Mrs.) LUCY W. CHARTRAND, Supervisor

School Nursing Report
The Board of Health employs two full-time
registered nurses--Mrs. Doris M. Lahti and Mrs.
Gladys V. Barney--and one part-time registered nurse
--Mrs. Jean E. Hobbs. These nurses, who work five
hours a day when schools are in session, take care of
all the Public Health Nursing as well as the School
Nursing.
A total of 140 visits were made to school
children for physical defects, absence from school,
and follow-up on vision and hearing failures.
Physical examinations are done annually on
students in Grades 4, 7, and 10, except for those stu-
dents who have returned a "Physical Form" stating they
have had an examination by their own doctor.
Dr. John DeLeo, School Physician, does not
only do the above physicals but also examines all Lun-
enburg athletes, who have their blood pressures taken
and their urine "Test-Taped" for sugar.
in 1970, German measles vaccine was offered to children in Kindergarten and Grades 2 and 3. This most
valuable immunization was given in hopes of helping eliminate some of the serious birth defects which have been
traced to mothers having German Measles during her pregnancy. It is advised that children have the vaccine
even if their parents think they have had this disease.
In March of 1971, we will again offer the german measles vaccine to Kindergarten and Grades 2, 3, 4,
and 5. We will also be offering the Mumps vaccine to Grades 6, 7, and 8.
The aforementioned programs and the programs listed below are given through the cooperation of the
Nashoba Associated Boards of Health:
Grade I only - Trivalent Oral Polio Series or Polio Booster for those who have previously
completed the oral series.
Grades 2, 5, and 8 - Dental fluoride program given by Dental Hygienist.
Grades I through 9 - Tuberculosis Tine Testing with follow-up on positive reactors.
Grades I through II - Diphtheria and Tetnus Booster and Re-vaccination program.
These programs, which are very well accepted, all require permission slips signed by a parent.
Vision and Hearing testing is done annually on all students in Kindergarten through Grade 12. Notices
of test failures are mailed to parents with a doctor's form being attached--a form which we wish returned to the
Nurses Office after the ear or eye doctor has seen the child. This information is valuable to the school in order
that we give your child every possible advantage.
"The Future Nurses Club" at the High School meets monthly. They have films or speakers on Health
careers. Their major project in 1970 was to make and deliver May baskets for a nursing home. Mrs. Barney
serves as advisor to the group.
In closing, the Nurses wish to thank the Superintendent of Schools, the Principals, and all other school
personnel for their help in making the school health program so successful.
Respectfully submitted,
DORIS M. LAHTI, R. N.

A summarized record of the major activities and developments of the past year includes the start of construction
of our school on May 11, 1970. This culminated the general contract award to Innamorati Brothers of Clinton in the
amount of $5,037,000 for building. The amount represents $23 per square foot, one of the lowest amounts in new
school construction this year. (Including site development and equipment, the full cost is $31.05 per square foot.)
Among the innovative construction techniques developed for Montachusett Regional was the first installation of gas
rooftop heating units for school building construction. On December 12, 1970, the units were placed in positionby
helicopter. According to the contractor the school will open in September 1971, if not fully, at least major sections.
To keep pace with the physical plant, the Montachusett Regional joined the Network of Innovative Schools
to develop plans for curriculum and teaching methodology. The Network consists of eleven other schools to help each
other develop educational plans.
Craft Committees continued to meet to streamline final plans for laboratories and shops including the procure-
ment and more economical use of equipment. Also, $57,000 worth of equipment was obtained without cost from the
National Industrial Equipment Reserve Headquarters. General Services Organization equipment was obtained equiv-
alent to the amount of $247,876. Donations of equipment continue to come in from many industries in the region.
A considerable savings to the District was accrued by the donation of storage space from local industries.
The second Licensed Practical Nurse graduation was held on November 22, 1970 when 23 graduates received
their diplomas. The graduates of the first LPN class scored 18 points above the national mean of 500 points when
they successfully passed their State licensing examinations in February 1970. The entering class of September 1970
started a 12-month program as compared to the previous 15-month program. Curriculum had been approved by the
Board of Nurse Registry. Mrs. Dolores A. Percy, R.N., was named the new Director of the LPN School replacing
Mrs. Simone Mitts, who resigned to further her education at the University of Massachusetts.
Six Nurse Aide Programs were conducted this year in cooperation with Rutland Heights Hospital under the di-
rection of Miss Bessie Wilkin, R.N. Seventy-six students have completed this program. As a result of a continuing
survey, nearly all of the LPN and Nurse Aide graduates are employed in hospitals and nursing homes in the area.
The first new key position to be filled in preparation for the school opening was the Coordinator of Occupa-
tional Programs, Albert G. Chouinard. Student enrollment policies were approved by the School Committee and
plans for student recruitment include staff and student orientation in all of the District schools. Mr. Orren F. Brad-
street, Coordinator of Pupil Personnel Services, has developed a program which has transformed the philosophy of
the school into an operational vehicle. Student selection and enrollment will commence upon the cornerstone laying
in March.
At the annual reorganization meeting on April 1, 1970, David P. Atkins was unanimously re-elected Chairman;
Dr. Clarence P. Quimby, Vice-Chairman; Kenneth H. Wilson, Jr., Secretary and Morrill G. Sprague, re-elected
Treasurer. Mr. Anthony Celuzza, Fitchburg; Mr. James English, Royalston and Mr. Paul Carroll, Barre, are the
new representatives to the School District Committee.
At the regular School Committee meeting on December 3, 1970, the Committee adopted an Operating Budget
of $1,086,964 for 1971. This includes the operation of the school when it opens in September 1971. Anticipated
revenue from the federal government for salaries and from the State for operating costs is $48,360. Atotal Capital
Budget of $622,000 reflects both debt service and debt retirement.
As a result of their careful planning, study and guidance, the School District Committee authorized the
in April of $3,500,000 bonds for 20-years at 6.4% interest. The remaining $3,000,000 bonds were sold on Decem-
ber 9, 1970 for a 15-year term at 4.7% interest. The School Committee was most fortunate in accruing considerable
interest savings to the District as compared with other recent bond sales.
