Board of Selectmen
Improved Communications & Understanding
In Lunenburg, the Board of Selectmen is also the Board of Health, Zoning Authority and Licensing Authority,
and the Board has direct responsibility for the Fire, Highway and Police Departments. All other elected officials
and most committees are responsible only to the voters. Accordingly, one of the objectives of the Board in 1969
was to improve communications with elected and appointed people who perform the various functions in Town. A
beginning was made along this line by meeting together periodically.
To provide a better understanding of the duties of the Board of Assessors, Town Clerk, Treasurer and Tax Col-
lector, Police Department, Fire Department, Board of Selectmen, etc., a program was initiated and has been incor-
porated into the 1970 curriculum to have a Local Government Day in our Junior-Senior High School. They will have
a mock election and the "elected officials and appointees" will spend time with their counterparts and reportback to
their classmates.
Lunenburg continued to grow in 1969. The Zoning Authority issued ninety permits. Four hearings in con-
nection with alcoholic beverage licenses were held and one trip was made to Boston to attend a hearing before the
Alcoholic Beverages Control Commission. For the first time in several years the Board has received requests to lay
out public ways involving a total of 4 streets. Work is proceeding on these and they may be submitted at the 1970
Annual Town Meeting.
Regularly scheduled meetings have been held every Monday evening except during the summer months when
they were heldon alternate Mondays. The Board meets every week to approve the Town Warrants. These are the bills
and expenditures for all departments. A total of 57 warrants were approved in 1969 amounting to $4,357,437.70.
Frederick B. Hobbs was appointed Chief of Police on January 27, 1969 and the force under his supervision
has carried out the responsibilities of this department admirably. We now have five full-time men on the force as
compared to four men in 1968.
The Rescue Unit or ambulance responded to 56 calls. Regular service is provided only during such hours as the
Montachusett Ambulance Service is unavailable. Under the supervision of Fire Chief, James P. Deming, response
to the calls was extremely prompt and the cost of manning the vehicle has been more than offset by billings to those
using the service. The Board set the rate of $32.00 per run which is in line with commercial ambulance service.
The Highway Department under Richard W. Brown's supervision. has continued to maintain our streets, keep
them passable through snow storms and rebuild Chapter 90 highways in a manner appreciated by the citizens of
Lunenburg.
The street lighting program was upgraded again in 1969 and it appears now that the program adopted in 1965
to provide more and better lighting for our streets in the interest of public safety will be completed in 1970.
The Boston Post gold-headed cane for the oldest citizen of Lunenburg was presented to Miss Edna Adams on
her 93rd birthday on April 25th.
Two Special Town Meetings were held during the year; one in January to approve more money for completion
of the Middle School, and one in June to consider six articles relative to zoning and appropriations. Excerpts of
these meetings are contained in the Town Meeting Report elsewhere in this book.

Town Clerk, Tax Collector, Treasurer
"Behind The Scenes"
Job descriptions can be tedious, more for the reader than for the author, who
is already thoroughly immersed in the functions of the Town Clerk, Treasurer and
Tax Collector of the Town of Lunenburg. In an effort to provide a little insight into
the real life of the Town Hall, and to "see ourselves as others see us, II wehave
solicited the viewpoints and commentary of townspeople and the employees of the
Town Office. More detailed reports of Town Office business can be found on pages
17 through 44 and 61 through 80, but what follows is a conglomerate view of "us"
sprinkled with a little of the levity that lightens the daily burden of Town Office
work.
The average resident of the Town of Lunenburg, or any small town for that
matter, thinks of his Town Hall as a quaint little building where he now and then
goes to pay an excise bill or get a copy of his birth certificate. Somehow the mere Mary L. Padula, Town Clerk,
age of the-building suggests a bygone era - no computers, tile walls or floors, or Tax Collector, Treasurer
indirect lighting. One would never suspect the volume of very up-to-date business
handled within.
This is as it should be in this day of increasingly mechanized life, we need our pleasant anachronisms. We
need to be able to walk into our "Hall" and do business with someone who very likely knows our name and will inquire
about our health, or our kids, or both, while at the same time taking care of whatever business is at hand. Still,
the Town is big business and as such, needs competent management, not to mention excellent equipment to carry on
the functions of the office. Under, around and in between several loosely organized piles (mountains?) of paperwork
can be found several very good IBM typewriters, a few well-exercised calculators, a formidable 3M Copier (with
a mind of its own) and an assortment of the necessary machinery to post the huge volume of mail, sign the thousands
of checks (13,034 in 1969) and in general keep the place running smoothly.
Behind the machines lurk several rather bright maniacs who seem to enjoy coping with both the machines and the
daily complications which arrive by mail, phone, or over-the-counter. All this, including the salaries of the
maniacs, are courtesy of the townspeople, who have always been most generous in allocating funds to keep US
up-to-date and functioning well.
Between the routine issuance of dog licenses (762 in 1969, though we suspect a few hundred more kicking
around here and there), the collection of excise taxes (5,654 bills for 1969) and real estate taxes (4,131), the
recording of vital statistics and the sale of fishing, hunting and trapping licenses (we boasted 712 such licensed
sportsmen in 1969), are the real challenges
the truly rewarding things we can do within and without the frame-
work of the office routine, to help people - helping a serviceman arrange his marriage around the proverbial red
tape, trying to arrange for an acceptable sanitary system on somebody's difficult lot, things the people have a prob-
lem doing for themselves by the mere fact of their not having the technical knowledge or the necessary contacts. This
is what makes everything worthwhile, and we hope that there will always be enough of such problems for US to solve.
It's a way of saying thanks for the townspeople's generosity and support.
(The Town Clerk wishes to expressher wholehearted appreciation for the competence, loyalty and cooperation of the
"maniacs" who make up the Town Office force: Ine Pihlaja, Gloria Fairbanks and Lynn Markham, who are employed
on a full time basis, Edice Gaffney who works for both the Town Clerk and the Selectmen, Joyce Moisan and Ann
Perkins employed on a part time basis and Fran Vaughan whom we occasionally steal from the Selectmen's Office.)

Planning Board
More Meaningful Controls
The Planning Board has met two evenings of each month for the past year. Several meetings were held when
problems arose that could not be handled at scheduled meetings. The Board was representedat two State-wide
meetings of the Massachusetts Federation of Planning Boards and at eleven meetings of the Montachusett Regional
Planning Commission.
Subdivisions have been the principle work of the Planning Board during the past year. Several are in various
stages of the approval process. One misunderstanding must be clarified. Final approval of a subdivision and return
of the money posted to assure work completion as handled by the Planning Board, in no way means acceptance by
the Town of Lunenburg of the streets involved in that subdivision. The Board of Selectmen of the Town of Lunenburg
are the only body that can recommend approval. Final acceptance can be made only by the vote of the Town.
Several joint meetings were held with the Board of Selectmen, Board of Appeals and individuals to clarify
specific problems. Hearings were held with the Development & Industrial Commission with respect to changing
certain Commercial Zones to Industrial Zones; and with abutters concerning the White Street subdivision.
The Planning Boardhas reviewed the complete Zoning By-law with our consultant. The purpose of this review
is to clarify any ambiguous or dual interpretive sections. This review has resulted in a number of proposed changes
which should aid the Board of Selectmen and the Board of Appeals as well as the Planning Board in betterand more
meaningful control of the Zoning By-laws. These changes, which are for the protection of the taxpayers and the
homeowners in the Town of Lunenburg, will be presented at a Town meeting for approval.

Low cost housing for the elderly became nearly a reality in 1969 with the considerable progress made by the
Lunenburg Housing Authority. The Authority operates according to the provisions of the laws of the Commonwealth
of Massachusetts and under the supervision of the Department of Community Affairs. The firm of Bastille-Neiley of
Boston was engaged as the architect early in January, and in July a six and one-half acre site at 131 White Street
officially became the property of the Lunenburg Housing Authority.
The forty-eight unit apartment complex will consist of six frame-constructed buildings of eight units each; and
in addition there will be a community building with a large common room, kitchen facilities to be used in conjunction
with the common room, laundry facilities for the tenants and an office for the Housing Authority. The apartments
will have all electric heat and utilities. Emergency alarm systems will be provided in each apartment as will all
appliances, carpeting and curtains. Parking facilities for tenants and guests will also be provided.
It is hoped that the natural beauty of Pearl Brook and the adjacent wooded area can be developed for the en-
joyment of the residents. The nearness of the new Wallace Civic Center enhances the attractiveness of this location
beyond that originally anticipated.
On December 11, bids for the general contract were opened, and Leominster Engineering Co., Inc. was the
low bidder at $695,000. As the year ends we are looking forward to signing the general contract and the start of
construction in the very near future. Announcement of the procedures for application for residency in the apartments
will be announced at a later date both in the newspapers and by posting at the Town Hall. Although the contract
for construction will allow 365 days for completion, it is hoped that the apartments will be ready for occupancyby
the elderly citizens of Lunenburg well before the year is finished.

Conservation Commission
Protect Our Environment
The members of the Lunenburg Conservation Commission and the Town Forest Committee are concerned about
the future of Lunenburg and hope many others are also concerned.
The cities are in big trouble: too much air pollution, too much water pollution, too many dump problems,
too much crime, too little open space and too little regard for the environment. It could be summed up by just
saying--too much of everything packed in too close together.
Lunenburg is a beautiful town but many of its brooks, streams, ponds and lakes are becoming polluted. Its
natural beauty is deteriorating. Air pollution, water pollution, erosion and a poor environment are just ahead.
We should act before it is too late. Protecting the world's environment and controlling the world's population is the
hope of all future generations. These are two of the most important issues facing US today.
The federal government with all its finances and leadership, plus the state government with its, perhaps, too
many controls and too little home rule, cannot perpetuate Lunenburg as a truly livable town. It's a job that has to
be carried out by our own Town, led by our own dedicated citizens.
The Conservation Commission and the Town Forest Committeeheld twelve joint monthly meetings, two special
meetings and attended three state meetings, a Westminster meeting, a Conservation District meeting at Sterling
and many small committee meetings. Five members attended four Conservation School meetings at the Waltham
Field Station. Members also attended three meetings of the Nashua River Basin Organization.

The Development and Industrial Commission recognizes that Lunenburg is faced with two major problems, neither
of which is easily solved: (1) The urgent need for a broadening of our tax base to ease the burden of the homeowner;
(2) The attraction of business without increasing the tax rate.
The popular concept that the luring of industry to a Townwill broaden the tax base is not always true. Usually
industry attracts people, people need homes, new homes mean more children, more children mean more schools, more
schools mean more taxes. As the Northern Worcester County area grows, as Route 495 expands, and with the reali-
zation of a North-South Freeway, the possibility of many more people building here is quite imminent.
Lunenburg, to begin with, has several drawbacks to industrial development among which are limited Town water
and, in general, no sewerage. The Commission, therefore, feels the sense of urgency about Lunenburg's situation. It
is their belief that certain areas in Town should be open for commercial use and that these areas should be ringed by
conservation land wherever possible. If the land is designated commercial, the possibility of home sites on that land
is unlikely. Furthermore, commercial land has a tendency to creep throughout a town. We believe that if the com-
mercial land is ringed with conservation, the possibility of encroaching on residential areas is minimized.
The Development and Industrial Commission in the past year has worked in this direction, meeting with the
Planning Board, Conservation Commission, and several "slightly interested" industries. It is hoped that more tangible
evidence of progress can be shown in 1970. Members of the Commission are: William A. Allen 2nd, Mendall W.
Colburn, Bigelow Crocker, Jr., Norman C. Cross, Rodney A. Karlson, John E. Rafferty and Frederick W. Stenuis.

TreeWarden
Ten Point Program
Trees are valuable citizens and constitute an important part of taxable property. When the number of trees
cut in a community exceeds the number of trees planted, the community faces a disaster of considerable significance.
Trees can make the difference between mediocrity and superiority. Therefore, investigations of the Dutch Elm
disease constitute important public benefit and may be the basis for future shade tree planting and care.
Effective control of Dutch Elm disease is a matter of community spirit and cooperation. Federal and State
agencies and local tree departments directly concerned with the control of Dutch Elm disease cannot do all the work
required to restrict disease spread. A ten-point program in which each citizen accepts his full share of work would
help all Massachusetts communities: 1. Remove and burn promptly all elm trees killed by the Dutch Elm disease.
2. Remove and burn promptly all cut elm wood. 3. Before April 1, prune and burn all dead material from elms.
4. Do not pile elm wood in the open. 5. Do not permit dumping of elm wood with bark attached in dumps. (Wood
piles and elm wood in dumps are common sources of disease outbreaks.) 6. Don't transport elm wood with bark at-
tached. 7. In all contracts involving elm trees (new road construction, road rebuilding, street widening, line
clearance, new line utility construction, etc.) require the burning or other satisfactory disposal of all elm. wood
removed. 8. Spray elms to control leaf-eating insects. 9. Enlist support of all municipal departments as well as
civic groups and public utilities in the detection and destruction of diseased and dead elms. 10. Keep elms as
healthy as possible.
Disaster struck our shade trees during the recent ice storms with scores of limbs and trees snapped off or broken,
and so numerous they had to be plowed off the highway. During 1969, 171 diseased trees were cut down and burned.
The Department of Natural Resources gave 3 days of sampling amounting to $105.00 and 9 days of work amounting
to $900.00 to assist in detection and removal of diseased trees. Approximately 55 samples were taken by the State
and sent to the Shade Tree Laboratory, University of Massachusetts, for culture. Eight trees

along

wagon trails and cow paths that linked scattered population centers and served as an
essential foundation for this Country's urban and rural development.
During the first half of this century, modernization of our streets and roads pro-
gressed to the point of providing better surfacing, better drainage, safer curves, longer
sight distances and easier grades. These improvements, however, hardly provided the
innovations required in the highway design and layout needed to handle today's traffic
with today's vehicles. Rural roads have more than double the mileage death rate of other
highways.

The year 1969 saw many changes in the Police Department. A new Chief, Frederick B. Hobbs, was appointed
in January; Roger A. Fitzgerald was promoted to Sergeant; and two full-time men were added to bring the force up

Arrests for 1969 doubled those of last year, totalling 713. Two hundred, twenty-six of these arrests were for
crimes against persons and property, and 487 for motor vehicle violations. There were 22 arrests for numerous nar-
cotics charges, ranging from being present where marijuana was found, to possession of heroin. Chief Hobbs feels
that it. is unfortunate that there is not sufficient manpower to fully investigate narcotic complaints, as the problems
in this area are of major concern to the community. The School Department should be complimented for the excellent
educational programs on narcotics that are being presented to students. It is felt that through education, the dangers
of drugs can be recognized, and the spread of this menace may be controlled.
With all crime on the increase, the breaking into private homes is one of the most frustrating for citizens and
the police. It cannot be emphasized too strongly that each of US must be his "neighbor's keeper". It is urged that
all suspicious persons and motor vehicles be reported at once, and that the police be notified of any period of
extended absence by occupants.

pelled by law to cause such device to be inspected, tested and serviced as oftenas necessary to insure that such de-
vice is correct. In addition, the Inspector of Weights and Measures, who is appointed each year by the Board of
Selectmen, may inspect and test any weighing or measuring device as often as he deems necessary.
In 1969 Lunenburg's Inspector, Wayne A. Balcom, inspected 24
scales and 43 weights. Of this number 1 scale was condemned. Fifty-
one gasoline pumps were inspected and 35 re-inspected.

General Laws of Massachusetts, all inhabitants or persons having usual
places of business within the townusing weighing or measuring devices for
the purpose of buying or selling goods, wares or merchandise, for public
weighing or for hire or reward, are hereby notified to bring them in to be
retested, adjusted and sealed.
The law also provides that any person who neglects or refuses to
exhibit his weighing or measuring devicest to a Sealer or who hinders or in

The following is a resume of work done for Lunenburg by the Nashoba Health Unit Staff during the year 1969.
Our Medical Advisory Committee consisting of five of the physician members of the Association (Drs. Cole,

Increased activity in communicable disease control followed a rise in the incidence of Salmonellosis and

Department's initial provision of Mumps vaccine. During the Spring of 1969, Nashoba contracted with Dr. Benjamin

this service. Diphtheria-Tetanus and Smallpox boosters were offered to pupils ingrades 1 and 11; Mumps vaccine to

community school nurse and the generous volunteer help provided in each town which make these clinics so successful.

TOWN MEETINGS
Each qualified inhabitant of the town has an indisputable right to vote
upon every question presented, as well as to discuss it, or there is no town
meeting. This is universally understood as the vital feature of the town
system of government as practiced from a long time before the Declaration
of Independence until the present.
Opinion of the Justices, 229 Mass. 601, 1918
"WHAT IS A TOWN MEETING
those who stay away must abide by the decisions of those who attend.
A town meeting is a gathering of all the people in the town who are eligible to vote. Of course they don't all come, but
It isn't just an ordinary meeting but a very special kind of meeting. Since its decisions affect the lives and the pocketbooks
of all the citizens, there are very special requirements which must be followed in calling and conducting the meeting. Any failure
to meet the requirements may invalidate the action of the meeting.
Here is a brief list of the most important items of business of the town meeting which come up from year to year.
1. The town meeting decides what officers the town shall have, what salary it will pay for each office,
and in some cases the length of term.
2. The town meeting elects citizens to fill certain town offices.
3. The town meeting decides for what purposes the town will spend money and how much may be spent
for each purpose.
4. The town meeting adopts and changes by-laws to govern the conduct of the inhabitants of the town
in local affairs.

The first educational function of "Montachusett Tech" began with the start of 1969. This occurred as a result
of the many requests by state agencies, hospitals and nursing homes to immediately assume teaching responsibilities
to help alleviate critical demand-occupation shortages. The Montachusetts Regional Practical Nurse Program at Bur-
bank Hospital started to function with the receipt of the first installment of a full funded $61,000 yeargrant. Thus
the school was transferred from the Trustees of Burbank Hospital to the School District As per policy, enrollment is based
on the Agreement of the District, at no cost to the participating communities, and free tuition to students. The first
graduation was held at Weston Auditorium at Fitchburg State College on November 23, 1969. All graduates had
previously passed the Board of Nurse Requirements with high ratings. The second 15-month course started in September
with an enrollment of 35 students.
Subsequent developments in the area of health skills education were the approval by the Division of Occup-
ational Education of the State Board of Education, of an evening Geriatric Aide Program (for personnel at hospitals
and nursing homes) that will be conducted at the LPN School and a one year Rehabilitation Therapy Assistant Training
Program to start early in 1970.
An eight-week Nurse's Aide Program has been in operation at Rutland Heights Hospital and the first group
graduated on December 30, 1969. All graduates obtained positions immediately upon graduation. The Nurse's Aide
day and evening program and the Geriatric Aide programs will commence every eight weeks. Hence, registration
for these classes will be continuous. The above programs are subject to the receipt of State funds.
After narrowly defeating two of the Bond Issue requests, the Town of Ashburnham in January requested the School
District to draw up an amendment to the Agreement providing for their withdrawal from the School District. Subse-
quently, a Bill was introduced to the Legislature "reconstituting the Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical
School District and authorizing the Bond Issue". The Bill was signed by Governor Sargent on March 5, 1969. In
March, the Emergency Finance Board approved the $6,500,000 Bond Issue.
With the start in mid 1969 of major work at the building site came the first need for substantial amounts of
money. We had planned to sell part of the authorized Bond Issue of $6,500,000 at this time, but the market was very
discouraging and interest rates kept climbing. Our Treasurer discussed the situation at length with our fiscal agent,
the New England Merchants National Bank of Boston and others, then recommended to the School Committee that the
District borrow on a short term basis sufficient funds to meet obligations until next Spring. There could be no guar-
antee but there was feeling in Bond circles that rates might moderate at least slightly by Mid-Winter. Accordingly
the Committee approved borrowing $650,000 to be due April 15, 1970. As this is being written, it is planned to sell
part or all of the Bond Issue before this is due.
The architect could not be authorized to prepare working drawings and bidding documents until after the Bond
Issue authorization. Preparation for such specifications required about six months. In the interim, the Committee
took advantage of prime working conditions (Summer and Fall) for site clearing and site preparation. The site clearing,
felling of trees, grubbing and removal of debris commenced June 2nd and was completed by the end of July. As soon
as the clearing was completed, the architect began engineering tests to determine weight bearing capacities, settling
rates, drainage, soil conditions, etc. The data thus acquired was used to develop the bid specs for the site develop-
ment. By the end of August the "specs" were published and the contract was awarded. At present, site preparations
for construction are nearing completion. By the end of January, this phase of the work is expected to be completed
and the prime contract will be advertised for bids. Thus, continuous workhas taken place from the moment the school
committee was able to award contracts.
In June, the District received the approval of the revised preliminary plans by the Bureau of Vocational Edu-
cation and the School Building Assistance Bureau. To aid in the planning of the equipment layouts of the shops,

"AN EXCITING NEW SCHOOL IS BORN"
Turkey Hill Middle School was to have been ready for occupancy in September 1968. When,
during the summer, it became obvious that the school would not open until sometime after the first
part of 1969, the decision was made to organize the pupils in Grades 5, 6 and 7 as a unit and
begin the school year operating as a Middle School on double sessions in the Elementary School
building. By so doing, we would be able to properly staff the school for the dramatic organiza-
tional changes being made in those grades at the start of the school year, SO that the transition
to the new school at the time of its opening would be as smooth as possible. Finally, the day for
the official opening arrived on Monday, February 24, 1969, a feeling of excitement was in the air
only to be dampened ironically by having to dismiss early on that day because of a heavy snowstorm
which developed to such an extent that the schools remained closed for the remainder of the entire
week. Despite this inauspicious beginning, the addition of this fine facility to our school plant
enabled us to initiate exciting new approaches to intermediate education and further provided the
opportunity once again of having all lovels of the school family adequately housed. Indeed,
Lunonburg was and is the proud possessor of an aesthetically. beautiful centrally located school
complex that could well be the envy of any community. The ability grouping by subject matter
disciplines and the modifications of the self-contained classroom concept at this level have been
explained in last year's report, so I will dwoll only briefly on some other phases of the Middle
School program which have been montioned only fleetingly in the past. The iddle School with its
exceptional specialized areas for the grade lovels served has enabled us to expose students to new

in this inclusive study, aided by State Department of Education Consultants as well as consultants
from among the entire staff. Out of this study has evolved an integrated, well articulated
Social Studies program for Grades 1 through 12 which has eliminated duplication and placed new
emphasis is upon deductive reasoning as to why things happen as they do in history. This new

- SCHOOL NURSING REPORT - -
The Board of Health employs two registered nurses, Mrs. Doris M. Lahti and Mrs. Gladys V.
Barney, and one substitute nurse, Mrs. Jean Hobbs, who not only do the School Nursing but also are
responsible for the Visiting Nursing Program in Lunenburg.
A total of 159 home visits were made to school children and their parents for absence from
school, physical defects and follow-up vision and hearing notices.
Dr. John DeLeo, School Physician, does physical examinations annually on students in Grades 4
- 7 - 10 except for students who have returned a "Physical Form" stating that they have had a recent
examination, signed by their family physician.
The School Doctor checks all athletes annually. These students also have blood pressure
checked, and a urine specimen is "Test Taped" for sugar.
Through the cooperation of the "Nashoba Board of Health", the following programs are offered
yearly:
Grade 1 only - Trivalent Oral Polio Series or Polio booster for those who have
previously completed the oral series.
Grades 2-5-8 - Dental fluoride program administered by Dental Hygienist.
Grades 1 - 9 - Tuberculin Tine Testing with follow-up on positive reactors,
Grades 1 -11 - Diphtheria and Tetanus Booster and Re-Vaccination,
In March, 1969- The "Mumps Vaccine" was offered to students in Grades 7-8-9
who had not previously had the disease.
The above programs all require permission slips signed by a parent.
The response to these programs has been most gratifying, with very few refusals.
We expect in the Spring of 1970 to offer "Mumps Vaccine" to Grades 4-5-6-7-8 and
"German Measles Vaccine" to Kindergarten and Grades 2-3.
Vision and Hearing Testing is done by the Nurses on all school children annually. Notices of
failures are sent home by mail to parents. It is most important that the Dr.'s form attached to the
notice be returned to the Nurse's office, so the school may comply with his recommendation and give
your child every advantage.
We hope after the 1970 Budget is approved we shall have better Nursing coverage at the Middle
School. The Budget request is for a part-time nurse, 5 hours a day, when school is in session.
The Nursing Service is grateful for the splendid cooperation of the Administration and School
Personnel.
Respectfully submitted,
DORIS M. LAHTI, R. N.
School and Town Nurse

School Lunch Program
With the opening of the Turkey
Hill Middle School last spring we now
have three lunch programs in operation
and a special milk program at the
Elementary Annex. In all, an average
of 1300 Type "A" student lunches are
served per day in our schools.
Cafeteria personnel number seven-
teen with some women working all day
and others during peak serving hours.
Each cafeteria has a Cook-Manager,
Baker, and General Workers, with the
total program being directed by the
School Lunch Supervisor.
The women attend school lunch con-
ventions each year as well as selected
workshops run by the State for training
in specific aspects of lunch programs
(i.e. baking, sanitation, meal variety,
commodity, purchasing, etc.). Each
"HAP} INESS IS LUNCH TIME AT THE
ELEMENTARY SCHOOL" according to
Mrs. Joan Deming (General Cafeteria
Worker) and Ross Lafarier (Grade 2).
school now does all of its own baking
of desserts, rolls, and quick breads.
This process provides freshness and
variety of bake goods while helping
to hold down the cost of operation.
I wish to thank the personnel of
all the kitchens for their hard work
and fine cooperation, without which,
our program could not be successful.
Especially, I thank Mr. Brown, Supt.
of Schools, and Ir. Archambault,
Supervisor of Buildings & Grounds,
for helping me this first year as
lunch program supervisor.
