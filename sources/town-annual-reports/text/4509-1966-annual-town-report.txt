Board of Selectmen
Programs and Progress
In the preparation of the Annual Report to the Citizens of Lunenburg, we find ourselves in the usual position of trying
to highlight for you the most important developments pertaining to various areas in which the Board of Selectmen function.
In general, the problems confronting this Board were similar in nature to those faced by previous Boards, particularly
in the areas of Public Safety, Licensing, Highway and Zoning. The introduction of Medicare brought about major
changes in the Health Department. In order to provide nursing services to our Senior Citizens at the lowest possible
cost of the taxpayer, our Town applied for and received certification as a Home Health Agency which was organized in
accordance with the Social Security Act to provide skilled nursing services and other therapeutic services in the home
on a visiting basis. Dr. Felix Heimberg was chosen to head up a professional advisory group to supervise the functioning
of the Agency and to approve and review policies concerning the services offered. Other members of the Agency are
Mrs. Ellen Kemp, a registered professional nurse, and Board of Health members.
Programs sponsored by the Federal and State Government necessitated the appointment of a Community Action
Committee and the Council for the Aging. In addition, the Board was represented at area meetings involving the Office
of Economic Development and Planning, the Transportation Coordinating Committee and the Montachusett Regional
Planning Committee. Because of these additional burdens on our time and, in an effort to increase our efficiency,
members of the Board operated individually in establishing a closer liaison between our Highway Department, Board of
Health, Zoning, Public Safety and Licensing. It is our feeling that this action produced the desired result.
Otherwise, the year 1966 was not much different from preceding years in regard to routine business. The Board
granted 636 licenses and permits, processed 106 zoning applications and selected qualified citizens to serve as jurors.
Continuing progress was made in our drainage program and Leominster Road was accepted by the State as a Chapter 90
road. A considerable amount of planning was necessary to obtain acceptance of the latter project by the State. The
second step in the renovation program in the Town Hall was commenced and is proceeding according to schedule.
Your Board was pleased to be represented at the dedication of the new Historical Society building and the new
Federal Post Office Building, as well as at the ceremony honoring Thomas C. Passios, former Principal of the Elementary
School. Noted with pleasure was the passage of the limited 3% Sales Tax the the General Court which was unanimously
endorsed by this Board in its 1965 report. We believe that the real estate taxpayer will see a substantial benefit as the
result of the State sharing the ever increasing burden of municipal expenses.
At the Massachusetts Selectmen's Annual Meeting, the Selectmen had the honor of accepting the First Place
Award in the Town Report contest. Again, we extend our congratulations to Town Clerk, Mrs. Mary Padula, and members
of her staff who participated in the preparation of the Annual Report
Selectmen: Normand J. Thompson, Chairman Merton S. Meloon, Bernard J. Paton

Board of Assessors
Full Treatment Urged
Should Lunenburg have a revaluation of all property in Town? The members of your Board of Assessors feel that
it is in the best interest of the taxpayers that such a program be instituted. We further consider this a most important
matter for the voters to consider and we therefore will devote most of this report to this subject.
We submit that a revaluation program would be an EQUALIZATION PROGRAM. In other words, such a program
would equalize the taxes assessed by bringing all valuations up to date and to their full-fair market value. Further,
the Assessors believe that such a program should be carried out by a professional firm of appraisers because we know
from experience, from advice we have had from the State Assessment System, from information obtained through our
contacts made with municipal officials in other communities and with officials in the Department of Corporations and
Taxation that this is by far the fairest and best way to achieve equalization. The Board of Assessors is of the opinion
that achieving equalization is the most important consideration in leveling taxes. We feel that the taxpayers should
also make this a most important consideration.
The taxpayer may well ask, "What is the cost of such a program?". In answer, the Assessors submit that they have
received preliminary estimates from several firms and submit that the cost involved would be in the vicinity of $22,000
to $25,000. This cost can be spreadover a period of two years. We consider this cost a good "investment" for the Town
and its taxpayers in order to achieve equalization.
We wish to point out to the citizens that should they vote for such a program, there are provisions made that any
taxpayer has the right to appeal for the purpose of discussing hisnew valuation with the Assessors and certain officials
of the appraisal firm / In addition, the taxpayer has the right to appeal to the Massachusetts Appellate Tax Board
should he feel that he is justified in hisappeal. Any decision of the Appellate Tax Board is binding on both the Assessors
and the taxpayer.
Last April the State Tax Commissioner assigned an "EQUALIZED VALUATION" of $38,000,000 to Lunenburg. You
Assessors felt that this valuation was too high and appealed to the Appellate Tax Board. Upon presenting their case before
this Board, they won a judgement which reduced this valuation to $34,000,000. As a result of this reduction Lunenburg
stands to gain about $35,000 additional in the State's distribution of Sales Tax monies in subsequent years.
The Assessors know that the Sales Tax Law, as passed by the Legislators and put into effect in 1966, did help to
reduce the spiraling of the Tax Rate. Although we look to receive more money in 1967 from this source of revenue,
nevertheless it is our considered opinion that the best controls overrising taxrates can be exerted in our Town spending.
The costs of Town Government are rising. It remains for the voters to control this spending in order to keep the tax rate
from rising too drastically.
Assessors: Leroy Allen, Chairman Arthur J. Brockelman, Clerk Kathleen M. Deming, Chester J. Pliska

Revaluation Study Committee
Up-Dating Preferred
WHAT WE WERE ASKED TO DO:
We were asked to review the present assessing practices and methods and recommend changes which would permit
correction of inequities which already exist and which would provide a continuing maintenance program, such recom-
mendations not to exclude a revaluation program if said Committee should deem it necessary.
WHAT WE HAVE DONE:
The Committee has held twenty-four meetings, two with the Lunenburg Board of Assessors, one with a represen-
tative of the State Assessment System, and two with professional appraisors. We have visited three other towns of similar
size: One of these has just completed a revaluation program by an outside firm; another which has recently increased
assessments to Full and Fair Market Value by means of applying a formula to previous assessments; a third which has a
full-time Assessor and is considering engaging an outside firm for revaluating their Town. We attended the Annual
North Eastern Regional Conference of Assessing Officers in Hartford, Connecticut on May 24, 1966, at which Mr.
Donald P. Fleming, a civil engineer and assessing officer from the District of Columbia, spoke on "Procedures for Assess-
ment Maintenance". Following his paper the Committee had an opportunity to talk with Mr. Fleming, and obtained
his professional opinions regarding the extent of inequities in Lunenburg and the procedures necessary in hisopinion,
to raise assessment to Fair Market Value.
THE BASIS FOR THE PRESENT PRACTICES AND PROCEDURES:
In 1952 Lunenburg employed the services of COLE, LAYER AND TRUMBULL, a professional appraisal firm, to
appraise all the buildings in the Town. A special committee, headed by this firm, was setup to determine land values
and furnish the Assessors with a complete assessment record for each parcel of land. This work was completed twelve
years ago.
Services rendered by the above firm included the following: Establishing of values of all buildings in Lunenburg
based on 1952 building costs; furnishing Town with individual lot cards which contained complete description of buildings
on lot, description of lot, record of ownership including date, book and page of deed recording, sketch of buildings,
replacement value of buildings together with amount of physical and functional depreciation and sound value of build-
ings; furnishing Town with dwelling specifications; measuring procedures; dwelling grade schedules; field pricing
schedules; commercial schedules and land pricing methods and charts to be used by the Board of Assessors for the purpose
of keeping assessments up to date and equitable with original revaluation schedule.
TOOLS AVAILABLE TO THE ASSESSORS:
Aerial maps of the entire Town showing each parcel of land and its owner or owners and maintained annually
by ALDEN S. MARBLE & ASSOCIATES, photostatic copiesof all transfers of property from which it is possible to obtain
the approximate price, log of "Assessment Sales" ratios showing the relation of the sale price to the assessed value,
State Assessment System membership, field books, guide lines set up by COLE, LAYER AND TRUMBULL, permits (a
Town Ordinance provides: "Pending the adoption of a municipal building by-law, no. building shall be built, erected,
reconstructed, moved, placed, externally altered, or changed in use, without a permit from the Selectmen"), fire-
proof vault for protection of vital records, subscription to trade journals, membership in assessing organizations, and use
of outside appraisers when needed.
PRACTICES AND PROCEDURES IN LUNENBURG:
Assessors are bound by oath to make full value assessments in their own best judgement. They employ a full time
clerk, and a part time clerk when needed. They have attempted to maintain the records left by COLE, LAYER AND
TRUMBULL by the following methods: Personal inspections aremade on the premises of alterations covered by building
or other permits; services of professional appraisers are obtained in connection with valuation of business properties;
assessment-sales ratiosare reviewed regularly; they have many meetings with other town departments and they are very
active in associations of assessors and in attendance at conferences; they are organized with a Chairman and each member
is responsible for a definite phase of the work; review of all abatements and decisions made on them; and there are other
duties of the Board of Assessors such as Excise Tax Billing, Transfers, Abatements, Personal Property Assessments, State
Reports, etc.

EVALUATION OF PRACTICES AND PROCEDURES:
It appears to the Committee that the equipment, the practices and the procedures of the local Board of Assessors
is as good or better than any comparable town studied. The records are more complete. The Assessors are more active
in following up changes covered by building permits. It was also evident to this Committee that the local Board of
Assessors have a great amount of interest in and loyalty to the Town of Lunenburg.
WHAT ABOUT FULL AND FAIR VALUE:
Some towns have gone to Full and Fair Value by multiplying the present assessed ratios by the necessary factor
to increase the valuation to full value, others have had an equalization program first and then have gone to Full and Fair
Value.
INEQUITIES:
Inequities exist when some taxpayers do not pay their fair share of the cost of the Town Government and School
Program. (Examples are given in full report.)
Revaluation Study Committee: Lawrence J. Montuori, Samuel J. Costa,
Carl W. Proctor, Wilbur W. Barney, Arthur R. LeBlanc, John F. Nicoll
METHODS OF ELIMINATING INEQUITIES:
A complete revaluation by a professional firm. ADVANTAGES: A team of professional appraisers will inspect and
determine the fair market value of all taxable land and buildings in the Town. This will be done in a relatively short
period of time and all valuation will be based upon one set of values. Office records would be up to date. Local
personnel currently serving would be involved in the revaluation and would be trained to use the material left for them.
DISADVANTAGES: In arriving at a decision as to what is best for the Town, one should be mindful of the fact that such
appraisals are relatively costly, and in themselves do nothing to prevent the recurrence of inequities between such
appraisals which might be necessary at relatively frequent intervals. (See note in complete report.)

Up-dating of present systems by the local Board of Assessors with some professional help. PROCEDURE: The
Assessors with the assistance of a small number of employed personnel trained by the State Assessment System would
inspect all residential property and verify information in present card system. Using this verified information the Assessors
through the use of up-dated formulae would make adjustments in valuations. Commercial properties would continue to
be assessed by the local Assessors with the assistance of the State Assessment System. Land assessments would be handled
by a Land Committee working in conjunction with the local Assessors and the State Assessment System. Personal property
would be handled as it is at present. New cards would be made up to reflect current information and values. This
could be done in one year. ADVANTAGES: A revaluation and equalization program can be provided at a lesser cost
because it will use the excellent system established by COLE, LAYER AND TRUMBULL. Since the local Assessors
would be actively and directly involved in the revaluation and equalization process, they would possess a wider
knowledge of local conditions and changes both present and anticipated. This method takes full advantage of the ser-
vices offered by the State Assessment System thus aiding in the reduction of costs. DISADVANTAGES: The local Board
of Assessors although qualified are not full time personnel. Again, this procedure in itself does not prevent recurrence of
inequities.
PROCEDURES TO MINIMIZE THE RECURRENCE OF INEQUITIES:
The Committee feels that in order to maintain either of the proposals the Board of Assessors should systematically
and annually inspect at least 25% of assessed property in addition to the inspection as the result of building permits.
Such inspection would include verification of all data contained on the basic assessment cards. They should also analyse,
with the assistance of the State Assessment System, land values as reflected in sales over a two yearrevolving basis.
The Assessors' progress in inspecting buildings and in analysing land values should be a part of their annual report. A
valuation book listing all properties could be published in Lunenburg. This is presently being done in many towns on
a three to five year basis. The Committee feels that there is some.merit to this and the book could be sold to interested
parties to defray the cost.
SUMMARY:
Our study has revealed clearly that Lunenburg now has a very fine assessment system. There were two problems
given US by the Town's vote: (1) Correction of any existing inequities and (2) Maintenance practices that minimize
their recurrence. Either of the proposals will solve the first problem. However, neither will do more then bring the
evaluations up to date and provide for current equalization. The Committee unanimously supports the second proposal
for two reasons. First, we feel that the present assessment system is not seriously in error. Secondly, the Committee feels
that the Town does not need to spenda large sum of money to correct the inequities that do exist. Concerning the second
problem the Committee unanimously recommends the maintenance program whereby the data of 25% of the assessed pro-
perties would be verified annually. Such a plan provides the continuous maintenance of the present system we have long
needed. A special advantage of the plan is that we take full advantage of the ability of the Assessors. Also this plan
provides maximum opportunity for the Town to realize the tax advantage provided by a continuing equalization program
year by year.

Town Counsel
Teachers First to Bargain
Of major importance to Lunenburg, as well as every other city and town in the
Commonwealth, two particular legislative enactments became effective in 1966. The
first of these, Chapter 763 of the Acts of 1965, became effective February 15, 1966.
Precisely, it brings into the area of municipal employment for the first time, collective
bargaining between municipalities and their employees. It applies to every county city,
town, and district in the Commonwealth. Excluded from its provisions elected
officials, board and commission members and police.
In Lunenburg, as in most other towns, our teachers have availed themselves of this
new law. This law provides that municipal employees may form their own organization
and through representatives of their own choosing, bargain collectively with the munic-
ipality on questions of "wages, hours and other conditions of employment and to engage
in other concerted activities for the purpose of collective bargaining, or other mutual
aid or protection, free from actual interference, restraint or coercion. The Lunenburg
teachers have selected the Lunenburg Teacher's Association as exclusive bargaining
Robert J. Larkin
agent and, as the law provides, the Association has already met and negotiated a con-
Town Counsel
tract with our School Committee.
These two bodies, the Association and the Committee, will continue to meet at reasonable times, including
meetings related to the School Committee's budget-making process, and confer with one another with respect to wages,
hours, and other conditions of employment, or the contract between them on any question arising under it.
The Contract contains a detailed grievance procedure which is intended to provide an agreed-upon means for
peaceful settlement of problems. Any questions of "wages, hours, and other conditions of employment" is subject to the
grievance procedure and, if a question of interpretation or application of the Contract is involved, it may end up
before the American Arbitration Association.
The second legislative enactment referred to is Chapter 14 of the Acts of 1966. The General Court directed the
State Tax Commission, in the year 1966 and every two years thereafter, to determine and establish for each city and
town a proposed equalized valuation which shall be the fair cash value of all property in such city or town subject to
local taxation as of January 1 in such year.
Of extreme importance to every city and town was the fact that the equalized valuation determined and established
by the State Tax Commission would, in turn, determine the individual town's share in the distribution of tax revenue
and, in particular, for "school aid". The formula for distribution is such that, as the valuation increases, the share
coming back to the town decreases. The Assessors believed $34,000,000 of valuation to be more realistic figure and
protested with vigor the State's determination of $38,000,000 which would have reduced Lunenburg's share of tax rev-
enue by about $35,000. At the request of the Assessors, an appeal was filed with the Appellate Tax Board in June
and the case was tried out in October, and on November 18 a decision was handed down in favor of Lunenburg, and
our valuation was reduced to $34,000,000.
The full impact of the law and its application has not yet been felt. It is quite clear, however, that because of
the difficulties which seemingly arise in applying the law to those towns not yet assessing at full and fair cash value,
such towns will quicken their pace in that direction.

Town Clerk, Tax Collector, Treasurer
From Wibbletown to Wobbletown It's 5 or 10 Miles
Much has been written regarding the origin of Massachusetts Towns. A simple
explanation is that "they grew by the exercise of English common sense combined with
the circumstances of the place". The development of communities from Colonial days
has been a process of adaptation to meet the needs of growing populations and changing
conditions. "This process is still going on and will continue as long as free people live
in Massachusetts."
There is one basic pattern for administrative officers which can be found in every
town, regardless of its size. Every town elects a Board of Selectmen, regardless of
whatever special provisions have been made for administrative organization, and with
few exceptions, every town has an elected Town Clerk and elected Treasurer. Every
town has a highway surveyor or superintendent who is in charge of maintenance of the
public ways. This basic complement of Town Officers has existed for well over three
hundred years.
Mary L. Padula, Town Clerk,
In spite of Home Rule provisions, the State Legislature pretty well controls what Tax Collector, Treasurer
a municipality can or cannot do. Cities and towns are subject to more control in financial matters than in any other
area of administration. Here, more than anywhere else, the Legislature spells out not only what cities and towns may
do, but also how they must do it. Massachusetts has one of the most comprehensive systems of compulsory audits of all
local accounts in the United States. This system provides protection for local officials and the public alike. The audit
report is a public record and must, by law, be printed in the Town Report. A report of the last State audit completed
in our Town appears on Page 35 of this report.
Although we must certainly recognize the merit in and necessity for rigid control of municipal financing we might
well criticize the State system of accounting. It is ironic that the State prescribes a system of accounting wherein we
commence spending monies in January which are 1. voted the following March; 2. raised by property taxes which are
billed the following June; and 3. actually received mainly in the last three months of the year. As an example,
$695,569 of the $989,593 in 1966 real estate taxes assessed were received after October 1, 1966 even though this is
the bulk of the money with which we are supposed to run our Government during the entire calendar year 1966. This
means that the Treasurer must borrow money in anticipation of the taxes and other lipated revenue until such revenue
is received.
The New England Town Meeting sets local government apart from that in all other sections of the Country. An
opinion of the Justices of the Supreme Court of Massachusetts handed down in 1918 stated---" Each qualified inhabitant
of the Town has an indisputable right to vote upon every question presented, as well as to discuss it, or there is no Town
Meeting. This is universally understood as the vital feature of the town system of government as practiced from a long
time before the Declaration of Independence until the present."
"Be it big town or little town, mill town or suburban town, in every town the Town Meeting follows the same pattern,
and is conducted under the same laws. Because within that common pattern there are allowances for variations, there
is flexibility and it is, therefore, sometimes hard to realize that the "all-day community get-together in the small farm
town" has anything in common with the formal evening meetings in some of the large metropolitan towns near Boston.
With practically no exceptions, Annual Town Meetings are held in February or March. Lunenburg's Annual Town Meeting
is set by Town By-law to be held on the first Saturday of March of each year. Our By-laws further provide that the
number of voters necessary to constitute a quorum at Town Meetings shall be fifty and that a number less than a quorum
may adjourn the meeting. Our By-laws also provide that the duties of the presiding officer shall be determined by the
rules of parliamentary law contained in 'Cushing's Manual" so far as they are adapted to Town Meetings. It is parti-
cularly fitting that Lunenburg's meetings should be governed by Cushing's Manual, not only because it is considered
the standard in this Country for proceedings of legislative and deliberative assemblies, but more so because Luther
Stearns Cushing, its author, was born and brought up in Lunenburg.

In hisbook entitled This is Your Massachusetts Government, Elwyn E. Mariner writes, "Today, Town Government
and the Town Meeting are a tradition. They are part of the way of life of over a million people in Massachusetts who
live in Towns. It is unusual to hear a resident of a Town advocating that histown give up its Town Meeting, and become
a city. On the contrary, one occasionally hears a resident of one of the smaller cities express the opinion that it would
be better for his city'to give up its charter and go back to the Town Meetings which it gave up years ago."
Fitchburg's Mayor Bourque has suggested that some of the area Towns may be better served by giving their
charters and becoming a part of that City. Some of Lunenburg's newer residents may not be aware of the fact that Fitch-
burg was once a part of Lunenburg. However, in March of 1757, the inhabitants of the westerly part of our Town pe-
titioned the annual Town Meeting to have that section set off and incorporated into a separate town, claiming that it
was an unnecessary burden upon them to be compelled to travel the distance of five or ten miles to attend Divine wor-
ship and transact the ordinary business of town affairs. Subsequent petitions followed and finally in 1764 the westerly
portion of the Town was set aside and incorporated under the name of Fitchburg. Later the same year a portion of Fitch-
burg was set off to form, with portions of Townsend and Ashburnham, the present town of Ashby. In view of the above,
and a careful comparison of financial statistics of the several small towns as opposed to the same statistics for the City
of Fitchburg, the citizens of Lunenburg might well ask Mayor Bourque if the citizens of Fitchburg would not, instead,
prefer to give uptheir Charter and go back to the Town Meetings which their city abandoned so many, many years ago.

Town Government Structure
No Pressing Need
A vote under Article 9of the 1966 Town Meeting Warrant directed that the Moderator and Selectmen be authorized
and directed to appoint a five member Committee as soon as possible after the Annual Meeting, such Committee, after
investigation and study, to report to the next Annual Meeting on its findings and recommendations concerning the
advisability of changing the structure of the government of the Town, including a Town Manager, Executive Secretary
to the Board of Selectmen, enlarging the Board of Selectmen or relieving the Selectmen of some of their present responsi-
bilities. Pursuant to this vote the Moderator and Selectmen appointed the llowing individuals to serve on such a committee:
John C. Shields; Philip M. Crouse; William G. Landis; Richard H. Sweet and Richard A. Bingham, and appointed
William G. Landis as Chairman. Mr. Sweet moved from Lunenburg and resigned before the study was complete.
The Committee met with the Selectmen (as a group and individually), Town Clerk, Town Counsel, PlanningBoard,
Revaluation Study Committee, Personnel Board, a Town Manager of another town and some of those who sponsored the
Article and visited a Town Manager Study Committee meeting in Acton, Mass. Some of the major alternatives con-
sidered were: Town Manager with Strong Charter; Town Manager with Weak Charter; Executive Secretary to the Board of
Selectmen; Enlarging Board of Selectmen; Relieving Selectmen of some of their present responsibilities, i.e., separate
Board of Health within Nashoba, separate Board of Health outside Nashoba, separate fulltime Town Engineer Selectmen's
Office physically separated from the rest of the Town offices. This office would be staffed by clerical personnel to handle
all clerical work directly under control of Selectmen.

We have attempted to find measures of our Town's Government. The Town's borrowing power is rated AA. This
speaks well for the Town's handling of financial affairs. Proper planning of the Annual Meetings has precluded the
need for Special Town Meetings to cover "forgotten items". The Town uses a coordinated system of application for
permits which automatically disseminates required information to Selectmen, Board of Health, etc. (It was noted,
however, that no systematic follow-up is made after completion of construction to assure final compliance with pro-
tective by-laws.)
It appears that Selectmen spend a large amount of time on routine matters leaving relatively little time for long
range planning. State and Federal programs are becoming more comprehensive and more complex. Therefore, more of
the Selectmen's time is required to assure that the Town will gain maximum benefit available.
The Bureau of Government Research at the University of Massachusetts reports in its study "Administrative Or-
ganization in Massachusetts Towns: 'The two predominant impressions one gets from the study of traditional Massachusetts
Town Administrative Structure are: (1) the lack of any clear cut executive office which fulfills on a continuous basis
the planning, organizing, directing, coordinating, reporting and budgeting functions; and (2) the independent nature
of the many Boards, Commissions and Departments which are responsible directly and only to the Town Meeting'.
CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
1. There is no immediate, pressing need for a major reorganization of the Lunenburg Town Government at this
time.
2. Of the alternative forms of government considered, this Committee believes that the "Town Manager with
Strong Charter" form will give our Townspeople the maximum benefit and maximum efficiency.
3. Under the current charter and stat-
utes the Selectmen have authority to make minor
organizational changes (such as assignment of

voter action. The Selectmen should make any
such permissible changes they deem to be in
the best interest of the Town.
4. In selecting Town employees Select-

every position being filled.
5. A committee should be appointed two
years hence for the purpose of evaluating the
desirability of changing from the present form
of government to the "Town Manager with
Strong Charter" form, such committee to report
its findings and recommendations to the next
following Annual Town Meeting after its ap-
pointment.

Sir:
In accordance with your instructions, I have made an audit of the books and accounts of the Town of Lunenburg for the period
from November 13, 1962, the date of the previous audit, to July 31, 1965, and report thereon as follows:
The financial transactions, as recorded on the books of the several departments collecting money for the Town or committing
bills for collection, were examined, checked, and verified by comparison with the books and records of the Town Treasurer and the
Town Accounting Officer.
The books and accounts of the Town Accounting Officer were examined and checked. The ledgers were analyzed, the recorded
receipts being compared with the Treasurer's books and with the records of the departments collecting money for the Town, while the
payments, as entered, were checked with the treasury warrants. The appropriation accounts were checked with the Town Clerk's
records of financial votes passed by the Town Meetings and with the Finance Committee's authorizations of transfers from the reserve
fund. The necessary audit adjustments were made, and a balance sheet, a copy of which is appended to this report, was prepared
showing the financial condition of the Town on July 31, 1965. The books and accounts of the Town Treasurer were examined and
checked in detail. The recorded receipts were analyzed and compared with the records of the departments making payments to the
Treasurer and with other sources from which money was paid into the Town Treasury, while the disbursements were checked with the
warrants approved by the Selectmen and with the Accounting Officer's books.
The cash book additions were verified, and the Treasurer's cash balance on July 31, 1965 was proved by reconciliation of the
bank balances with statements furnished by the banks of deposit, by verification of the cash memoranda on file, and by actual count
of the cash in the office. The maturing debt and interest payments were proved with the amounts falling due and were checked with
the cancelled securities on file. The outstanding coupons on July 31, 1965 were listed and reconciled with the balance in the bond
coupon account, as shown by a statement received from the bank of deposit.
The records of payroll deductions for Federal and State taxes, the retirement systems, credit union, teachers' association dues,
and for insurance premiums were examined and checked. The deductions were footed, the payments to the proper agencies were
verified, and the balances on hand were reconciled with the controlling ledger accounts. The records of tax titles and tax possessions
held by the Town were examined and checked. The transfers from the tax levies to the tax title account were compared with the
collector's books, the reported redemptions were checked with the Treasurer's recorded receipts, and the tax titles and tax possessions
on hand were listed and reconciled with the ledger controls.
The savings bank books and securities representing the investments of the several trust and investment funds in the custody of
the Town Treasurer were examined and listed, the income being proved and all transactions and balances being verified.
The books and accounts of the Tax Collector were examined and checked in detail. The tax, excise, and assessment accounts
outstanding at the time of the previous audit, as well as all subsequent commitments, were audited and proved. The recorded collections
were compared with the payments to the Treasurer, as shown by the Treasurer's and the Accounting Officer's books; the abatements,
as entered, were checked with the Assessors' records of abatements granted; the transfers to the tax title account were proved; and the
outstanding accounts were listed and reconciled with the controlling accounts in the Accounting Officer's ledger. Further verification
of the outstanding tax, excise, and assessment accounts was made by mailing notices to a number of persons whose names appeared
on the books as owing money to the Town, the replies received thereto indicating that the accounts, as listed, are correct. The records
of deferred street assessments were examined. The payments in advance were compared with the Treasurer's recorded receipts, the
amounts added to the tax levies were verified, and the apportionments due in future years were listed and reconciled with the ledger
controls.
The financial records of the Town Clerk were examined and checked. The collections on account of dog and sporting licenses
issued, and from miscellaneous charges, were verified and compared with the payments to the Division of Fisheries and Game and to
the Town Treasurer.
The records of departmental cash collections by the Board of Selectmen, the Inspector of Weights and Measures, and the Inspector
of Gas Piping and Gas Appliances, as well as by the Police, Health, Public Welfare, School, Library, and Cemetery Departments,
and by all other departments in which money was collected for the Town, were examined and checked. The payments to the Treasurer
were verified and the cash on hand in the several departments was proved by actual count. In connection with the fees for issuing
permits by the Chief of the Fire Department, attention is called to the provisions of Section 53, Chapter 44, General Laws, which
requires that all monies received by any town officer or department shall be paid into the Town Treasury upon their receipt.
The surety bonds of the officials required by law to furnish them for the faithful performance of their duties were examined and
found to be in proper form.
During the audit, cooperation was extended by the several town officials, for which, on behalf of my assistants and for myself,
I wish to express appreciation.
Respectfully submitted,
Milliam Schurtz

Police Department
And Peace Was Restored
Shortly after approval in 1966 of funds for the renovation of the police office space, work was commenced on the
project. Partitions were removed and alterations completed, giving the Police Department about three times the former
space. Storage cabinets and files were installed, lockers for the Officers were added, and a new floor and interior
painting completed the renovations. This project was badly needed and has given adequate space in which to operate.
For several years, the problem of loitering teen-agers on the Lake Front at Whalom has been of concern. Each
year the problem increases, as well as the police payroll. This "hangout" produces such activities as larcenies drinking,
blocking the flow of traffic and causing a general nuisance to the business establishments and public alike. To abate
this situation, it was decided to restrict parking on one side of the street and to place "No Parking" Signs across the
Lake Front. As a result, the unwanted visitors left the area and peace was restored. The police payroll wasalso reduced.
With the use of two cruisers and added personnel, constant patrolling has helped to handle the ever increasing
number of motor vehicle offenders. With more registered vehicles and operators on our highways, the problem grows
each year.
Recently Chief Winchester has expressed concern about the traffic problem in the center of Town. With the Post
Office relocated and a new shopping area about to be opened, this area is becoming very congested. The area starting
at the Town Hall and continuing north on Main Street is also very much in demand. With various events in progress at
the same time, it is difficult to pass on Main Street, let alone park a car. The Chief recommends that a traffic survey
of the usable space be made with new parking facilities in mind.
The Chief once more requests that the Board of Selectmen continue theirproject of numbering property in Lunen-
burg. Some areas are still using old postal numbers, apparently picked at random, as they do not run consecutively.
This posesa real problem in locating new families who are not known by name. There are still unused funds available
which were appropriated in past years for this purpose. This project would be very helpful to all Town Departments as
well as to individuals.

were sounded, an increase of 20 over the number in 1965. There were 60 more calls this

Civil Defense
Alert Today Alive Tomorrow
The concept of civil defense is not new to American life. Only the term itself and specific functions are new.
As technological conditions change, civil defense functions and procedures change. Civil defense is planning for
coordinated action to protect the population in periods of emergency utilizing existing governmental structure adopted
to emergency needs. Civil defense is based on extension of the principle of self-protection to include mutual self-
protection on the part of groups and communities. Effective civil defense calls for advance planning at all levels of
government, local, state and national.
Today, the instruments of war are the most destructive of the man-made causes of devastation ,and the potential
of these devices is very great. We must learn to understand and live with them and accept as fact that there can
be no complete security should such weapons be used. Methods of maximum protection must be devised. Blind fear in
the face of danger can only result in panic or inaction. Understanding, based on knowledge of the problem, make
protective measures known in advance which will lead to effective behavior in time of peril.
One of the key elements in the national civil defense program is that of establishing shelters for protection from
fallout of radioactive materials should nuclear attack occur. Persons close to the detonation of a nuclear weapon will
not survive the blast and thermal effects, and tens ofmillions of Americans alive after the blast can be killed by radio-
active fallout if they are not protected. Studies made by the Department of Defense both for military and civilian
defense planning purposes show that virtually all of the nation could be subjected to fallout following amajor attack.
Materials giving complete details about radioactivity are available from your local Civil Defense Department.
At this point it is important to know only that people who have no protection from radioactive fallout will get sick and
if they have too much exposure, they will die. The converse situation is of great significance-- people are protected
from fallout, sickness and death will be greatly reduced and, in many instances, averted. A consistent conclusion of
Department of Defense studies is that, under heavy attack, the number of lives that could be saved by fallout shelters
would be tens of millions.
Against all of the forces which make for war stands the will for peace. Hopefully then, the endless threat of war
will someday be overcome. In the meantime it remains for our generation to develop a sense of rationality about life
in a nuclear age and create an atmosphere of optimism about living in the future rather than pessimism about mere
survival. Even if waris eliminated, man will always be plagued with natural disasters. Civil defense is, therefore ,con-
fronted with the task of protecting and preserving life while searching for ways to make it better.

Conservation
While Nero Fiddled
Nero fiddled while Rome burned. Will history say that Lunenburg fiddled while it lost its natural resources,
fiddled away its opportunity to be a wonderful New England town with open spaces, beautiful forest and swamp lands
with its wild life, and hills and valleys and brooks and streams with the water resources and scenic beauty? Will
history say that Lunenburg neglected to set aside sufficient areas for its recreational, educational and aesthetic needs
and also for its economic advantages? This must not happen!
The Conservation Commission and Town Forest Committee have recommended that (1) the own ultimately set aside
four thousand acres, including that already owned, to be under the control of a conservation program, (2) this four
thousand acres be used by the inhabitants of the Town for recreational, educational and aesthetic programs, (3) water
resource and forest improvement programs be carried out on this area so that future revenue may be returned to the Town,
and (4) the Town add each year to the accumulating Conservation Fund one dollar per capita plus any anticipated re-
fund from the State or Federal government because of this program.
The Town now has approximately eight hundred fifty acres under the jurisdiction of both committees. It is planned
to set aside suitable areas in different parts of the Town so that Townspeople can enjoy the benefits in their own
neighborhoods. One hundred sixty acres were added in 1966 to Lunenburg's conservation area and it is hoped to add
sixty-two more in 1967.
The Cowdrey Nature Center, the first conservation land to be improved with trails, bridle paths, picnic areas,
fishing and boating has proven to be a great attraction. Hundreds of people have traveled over the five miles of trails
and enjoyed the mile and a half length of Mulpus Brook. Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, school, church and Fitchburg State
College groups are among those using this area which is used by many groups for educational purposes. In October the
Harvard College School of Forestry at Petersham, for the second year in succession, brought a group of twenty-four
professional resource men to inspect Lunenburg's conservation plans and also the Cowdrey Nature Center. Thesemen,
coming from four different countries, were enrolled in a special forestry group.
It is later than we think. It's now or never to get conservation areas. Lunenburg, like many other towns, seldom
gets around to obtain land for town purposes until it is too late. Let's not be too late this time. This conservation
program can be carried out only with the help, the understanding and the encouragement of the Selectmen, the Planning
Board, and the Finance Committee. It's too big a battle and too important for the Conservation Commission and the
Town Forest Committee to carry out alone.
The conservation program is indebted to the Highland Baptist Church Service Brigade of Fitchburg for building a
rugged bridge across Mulpus Brook; to a group from the Alpha Phi Omega National Service Fraternity of the Fitchburg
State College and their supervisor, Dean of Men William Fitzgibbons, for building trails on Lookout Pass; to Girl
Scouts for putting name tags on many trees, under the supervision of Mrs. Louise Shifler; to Roger Winchester and the
VFW Pack 4 Cub Scouts for clearing mountain laurel areas; to William Proctor for the free use of his tractor; to the
Lunenburg Grange and Lunenburg Woman's Club for sponsoring an open meeting for conservation and to Carl E. Brown, Jr.,
a former citizen of Lunenburg, and Mrs. Brown, who came from Winstead, Connecticut to attend this meeting to
show a wonderful set of nature slides. Irving Kimball, who is the consultant without pay for all phases of the conservation
program, spends the greater part of each summer carrying out a beautification program and a forest improvement program
on these areas. He is surely needed. Mrs. William R. Peabody continues to inspire the program by being a wonderful
hostess at the regular meetings.
Many towns have received gifts of land for conservation purposes from present and former citizens. Lunenburg
has received one such gift. Lunenburg's conservation groups will gladly accept with great appreciation such areas
regardless of theirsize or location and will, if the donor wishes, mark the land with the family name or any name desired.
It is recommended that the sum of $5150.00, no more, no less, be added to the Conservation Fund in 1967. This
is approximately the same amount as for the last five years. It is further recommended that $5360.00 be appropriated
from the Conservation Fund for purchase of land.

Development & Industrial Commission
No Radical Change
In January, the Development and Industrial Commission mailed a letter to most industrial and commercial
concerns in Lunenburg explaining the Commission's purpose and its services, and suggesting ways that it could be
helpful to the established businesses in the Town.
During the year, the Commission continued to be concerned with the possibility of establishing a so-called
"Industrial Zoning" by-law. Meetings were held with the Planning Board, with a potential developer, and with
Mr. Thomas Earley of the Massachusetts Department of Commerce. Mr. John Boursy of Lunenburg was called on to
help write this by-law. His knowledgeand experience were most helpful to the Commission. The proposed by-law was
submitted to the Planning Board for approval in July. At a joint meeting with the Planning Board in August, it was
demonstrated to the Commission that this whole matter is much more complex than was anticipated, and that a more
detailed study must be made before going further with the by-law.
During the year, inquiries were received from a group interested in building indoor tennis courts and a recreation
area in town, and from a local business college looking for a new location. Both inquiries were handled promptly
and courteously by the Commission, but nothing further has been heard from either one.
Lunenburg being essentially a residential area, and having limited water resources and municipal services, the
function of a Development and Industrial Commission is basically one of information and service. However, in the year
ahead, the Commission intends to explore ways in which it could be most useful to the Town, and investigate thoroughly
all potential possibilities for even limited industrial growth. It is the consensus of the Commission that our area would
be attractive to research laboratories, office buildings, higher educational institutions, and garden-type apartment
housing. Beyond this, the Commission believes the Townspeople would want it to be highly selective so as not to
radically change the character of the Town.

The idea of public responsibility for the poor is not a modern expression of political
or social theory. From earliest colonial times there has been recognition of the fact that
fellow human beings who cannot take care of themselves deserve help, not only for their
sake but for the sake of our civilized society.
It became the responsibility of each town to take care of its own needy citizens
while the State provided for certain other persons who were ineligible for assistance at the
local level. Eventually, with the Social Security Act of 1935, the federal government
began to accept a share of the responsibility for the welfare of people everywhere and has
progressively offered financial aid to State and local governments in our various categories
of public assistance.
Agent, Mrs. Gladys Goodale
Thus, our traditional instincts for helping persons in trouble or need have broadened and matured into recognition
that human values and the fate of the "other fellow" are essential to the survival of democracy and can best be achieved
through a collective, unified effort.
The money for our present welfare programs is, therefore, derived from three sources--Federal and State government
grants and the taxes of the individual cities and towns. This money is paid out locally. During the year 1966 we were
advised of increases in Rest Home rates, increases and changes in the ratings of Nursing Homes and increased hospital
costs. In April of 1966 we had an increase in the cost of living and our semi-monthly grants were adjusted to include
this.
The Social Security Amendments made important changes and these have affected the programs of the Welfare
Department. Title XVIII, Health Insurance For The Aged, became
effective on July 1, 1966. All persons 65 years of age or over,
with a social security number became eligible for Hospital insur-

Highways
On The Sunny Side of The Street
The following definitions have been included inanswer to the many people who
have asked what is meant by a Chapter 81 or Chapter 90 road.
Chapter 81 - General law which provides for contribution by the State for maintenance
of accepted town roads (at the present time the State contributes approximately $275
per mile provided that the Town contributes at least $75 per mile).
Chapter 90 - General law which provides for contributions by the State and County for
construction or reconstruction of Town ways provided that such ways connect the center
of two towns or connect two State highways, or a State highway and another Chapter
90 road. The State contributes 50% and the County and Town 25% each. This chapter
also provides for contributions by the State and County for maintenancé of roads com-
structed with Chapter 90 monies. The State, County and Town each contribute one
third of maintenance cost.
Many and varied services and activities are performed by the Highway Department.

Recommendations:
1. Purchase of a Power Sweeper.
The need for a Power Sweeper is immediate. This pièce of equipment would result in a year-round saving
to the Town. In the spring, it would greatly cut down the cost of "spring cleaning", and remove salted sand
from the sides of roads, thus lessening the danger of killing roadside grass, shrubs and trees. We have numerous
complaints from townspeople regarding this problem, and it is impossible to hand-sweep every road in Town.
We could also cut down immensely on the clogging of our drainage structures all over town. The expense of
hand-sweeping the roads before oiling would be lessened and the delay in this work reduced because of the
necessity of cleaning the roads before oiling. It would save money on cutting back shoulders, for where it now
takes the grader, cat tractor and dump trucks to perform this work, a sweeper could accomplish this task in
one operation.
2. Purchase of a Roller.
À good resurfacing job cannot be obtained without the use of a roller. It is increasingly difficult to hire
a roller, and in many instances has necessitated the rescheduling of work to meet the availability of rented
machine. There have been several occasions when it was impossible to rent a roller when needed. The Highway
Department is now constructing the Town's sidewalks, and a roller is imperative for this work.
3. Purchase of a Dump Truck.
It is recommended that a new truck be purchased to replace a 1957 International, in accordance with our
program of replacing older equipment.
4. Purchase of a Portable Welding Machine.
At. present, all of our electric welding work is hired. Welding jobs occur daily where a town-owned machine
would greatly facilitate this work. In 1966 approximately $1,072 was spent on hiring a welder, signifying that
a portable welding machine would pay for itself in less than-a year.
The Highway Department feels that all of this equipment is urgently needed. The Sweeper and Roller have been
requested for several years and, in the interest of economy and efficiency, it is felt that the above items should be
purchased this year.

Section 1. No person or firm shall keep or permit to be kept on any premises within the Town of Lunenburg more
than one unregistered motor vehicle, assembled or disassembled, unless said vehicles are stored within an enclosed
building.
Section 2. A special permit to keep more than one unregistered motor vehicle on any premises not within an enclosed
building, after a duly called public hearing to which all abutters to the premises have received notice, may be
granted by the Board of Selectmen, if it finds that such keeping (1) is in harmony with the general purposes and
intent of this By-law; (2) will not adversely affect the neighborhood and (3) will not be a nuisance.
Section 3. All such special permits granted shall limit the number of unregistered motor vehicles to be kept on
the premises by the holder, shall not run with the land, and shall be limited to a reasonable length of time.
Section 4. The provisions of sectionsone through five, inclusive of this article, shall not apply to motor vehicles
designed and used for farming purposes, to landowners or tenants who store vehicles out of sight of abutters and which
cannot be seen from public ways and private ways used by the public, nor to persons or firms in the lawful exercise
of licenses granted under General Laws, Ch. 140, Sections 58 and 59, as amended.
Section 5. Whoever violates any provisions of this Article of the By-laws she be liable to a penalty of five ($5.00)
dollars per day for each day of violation, commencing ten days following date of receipt of written notice from
the Board of Selectmen.
VOTED UNANIMOUSLY.

Superintendent's Annual Report
When in September, 1965, the citizens of Lunenburg voted down the much needed Elementary
School for the third and final time that year, the School Committee, its personnel and the students
were confronted with the dismal fact that despite increasing enrollments, school housing relief
seemed a long way off, particularly in view of the emotionalism which evolved around the school
issue. This action, of necessity, had great bearing upon planning the progress of the Lunenburg
School System during 1966. It was a time for the practice of both patience and perseverance.
From defeat, however, there rises added challenge and the need for the utmost of perseverance
to insure the good that must come. Brought sharply into focus was the sober fact that rather than
having additional school housing facilities in the fall of 1966, relief was more than likely at least two
years away.
Perhaps the most frustrating problems associated with this, was the fact that plans for educa-
tional progress that were being made would of necessity be further delayed and much energy would be
directed once again toward problems already thoroughly studied rather than concentrating energies
toward continued educational improvement.
It was obvious, that much precious time would once again be directed toward finding ways to
further improvise in order to meet the most pressing demands of housing the children now and for two
more years at least.
A new Building Committee was appointed and began its study with independent zeal aimed
toward seeking a solution to school housing needs for as long a time as possible.
Meanwhile, there was the immediate need of four Elementary classroom spaces for September,
1966. The School Committee's objective in solving this problem was to seek additional spaces rather
than indulging in limited double sessions or increasing to excessive, undesirable pupil-teacherratios
After considerable study, decisions were made to discontinue school lunches at the Elementary level

- POLICY REVIEW -
Recognizing the need to review and update School Committee Policies and Regulations, the
Committee adopted a new format which could easily be adapted to changes as they evolve. Up-to-date
policies are essential to the smooth operation of a well-informed and efficient school system. Accom-
plishing this assignment is and will continue to be an on-going undertaking of the School Committee
during the coming school year.
- SCHOOL COMMITTEE/TEACHER RELATIONS -
During late 1965, Chapter 763 of the Acts of 1965 was passed and enacted into law, the purposes
of which were to provide municipal employees, including teachers, the right to organize and to
collectively bargain with their employers in matters dealing with wages, hours and conditions of em-
ployment. The Lunenburg teachers elected to exercise the privilege afforded by this law and voted to
have the Lunenburg Teachers' Association serve as their bargaining representative, both in matters of
drawing up a formal contract as well as in negotiations over matters of concern. This was a totally
new experience to both the teachers and the Committee as one has evidenced through many news
releases concerning these activities in other school systems in past months. Much time and effort
has been given to this task during the year. It is to the genuine credit of both the members of the
School Committee and the Lunenburg Teachers' Association that progress was made in these initial ex-
periences with the maximum of good faith, hard work and perseverance. Progress and harmony in
these relations are essential to the continued effectiveness of the school system.
- PERSONNEL AND PHYSICAL PLANT -
Lunenburg can take justifiable pride once again in the extremely low turnover of teaching and
non-teaching personnel. There was a change of twelve (12) teachers resulting from retirement, family
transfers or pregnancy while there was no change in other personnel. Such stability does much to
preserve continuity and desireable improvement.
Your School Committee is deeply conscious of its responsibility to maintain both buildings and
grounds at as high a level of efficiency as possible so that citizens, students, and teachers may all
take justifiable pride in our physical plant. The interior of the Elementary School has been completely
repainted in a three-year program as has the interior of the Elementary Annex. Attention will be given
to the Jr. -Sr. High School this coming year since it is now going into its eleventh year of use. Home-
making facilities--ranges, washer-dryer, refrigerators, are being replaced and will be updated at two
year intervals from now on at a cost for shipping only. Effectiveness of this program is enhanced by
the use of up-to-date equipment.

- HOW IS YOUR SCHOOL DOLLAR SPENT? -
Since schools account for a large percentage of the Community's expenditures, it is very
necessary to insure that its money is being spent as efficiently and effectively as possible. With the
recent adoption of uniform methods of accounting for school expenditures, it is now possible to get
more meaningful comparisons than had been possible in times past.
School Management as of January, 1967 reports the following: "Out of every $10. added to the
instructional budget, $4.37 is eaten up by inflation. The rest goes for improvement." "Actually ,from
1965-66 to the present year, inflation is estimated to have risen just 2.2%." "What was purchased
for $317. a year ago costs just over $324. this year II The median expenditure for Central Administra-
tive Professional personnel "is $16.70 per pupil"; Lunenburg's is approximately $7. which would
indicate somewhat strongly that one person can no longer effectively do the supervisory assignment
that is needed.
With the accompanying circle graph, I would like to present a picture of how money has been

of the chart will show that 71.9% was spent in this category in 1966 which fell short of the National
median of 78.8%. Of this amount, 66% went directly toward Teachers' salaries compared to a National
median of 65% for this purpose. The National median has increased 2% in this category this year while
Lunenburg has remained relatively the same.

School Nurse
The Board of Health of Lunenburg hires two registered nurses--Mrs. Doris M. Lahti and Mrs.
Gladys V. Barney, to do public health and school nursing.
We were fortunate this year in being able to buy a new Titmus Vision Machine which, with so
little space available to do testing, has saved valuable nursing time and also means all children will
be vision tested during the 1966-67 school year.
Tuberculosis Tine Testing was done on First and Ninth grade students. About ninety percent
of the children in the above grades received the test. No positive reactors were found.
Physical examinations are done annually by Dr. John DeLeo, School Doctor, on students in
grades four, seven and ten, who have not previously been examined by their own doctor.
Diphteria-Tetanus Boosters and revaccinations were administered in the Spring by Nashoba
Associated Boards of Health of which Lunenburg is a member town. This program is available each
year to students in grades one, six and eleven.
A Flouride Program is also offered by Nashoba with Hygienist, Mrs. Elvira May, giving treat-
ments to grades two, five and eight.
Trivalent Oral Polio drinks are offered to all new students who have not been previously pro-
tected and a booster dose to grade one children only.
All these programs require signed parental permission and are well accepted with very few re-
fusals.
Vision and hearing testing of all students is done annually by the nurses. Notices of vision &
hearing failures, as well as physical defects are mailed home to parents with a form attached for the
doctor to fill out. This is an important part of the testing program, and we encourage parents toreturn
these forms so the school, may cooperate with the doctor's recommendation.
One hundred and eighty-two visits were made to parents of school children for: Absence from
school; Illness; Physical defects; vision and hearing failure; and health supervision.
Thirty-two children were transported to the doctor or dentist for treatment.
The Future Nurses' Club at the High School continues to be active. Five girls from the 1966
graduating class entered fields of nursing.
In closing, the nurses wish to thank the Superintendent of Schools, the Principals and all
other school personnel for their help in carrying out the school health programs.
Respectfully submitted,
DORIS M. LAHTI, R. N.

School Lunch Program
My fourth annual report of the School Lunch Program covering the school year, September 1965
to September 1966 reveals that 193, paid "Type A" lunches were served to students as well as
6722 free lunches to high school workers and the needy. 10,475 adult lunches, including those of
the staff, were also served in a period covering 174 lunch days for the Elementary School and 175 for
the High School, making an estimated 1,213 lunches as an average served daily. Next year will bring
a sizeable drop in lunch participation because of the closing of the Elementary School cafeteria.
150,997 half-pints of milk were sold and 2,336 half-pints of free milk under the United States Depart-
ment of Agriculture Special Milk Program, were provided with 4,587 half-pints sold to adults.
Financially, we were not as fortunate as in the preceding year because of the increased cost
of all foods as well as the fact that we did not receive as many Federal commodities as in previous
years. The opening balance was $7,403.31 for the beginning of the school year in September 1966
after all bills were paid in June. It is interesting to note that the School Lunch Program is self-
supporting with the exception of the School Lunch Supervisors' salary and fuel and electric costs. It
is required to operate on a non-profit basis, with meal costs to pupils being maintained at as low a
cost as possible.
Participation percentage-wise for the year in the Jr.-Sr. High School was 74% according to
attendance while in the Elementary School, participation was 70%.
The annual Spring School Lunch Workshop was held at Westboro for three days during the April
vacation. Mrs. Lucy Chartrand and I attended. On Friday, November 4th, in conjunction with the
Teachers' Convention, all personnel attended the County School Lunch Meeting held at the Burncoat
Jr. High School in Worcester.
A new-type conference featuring food demonstrations has been started for Cooks and Bakers
and was held at Stonehill College. Mrs. Lucy Chartrand, Head Cook at the Jr.-Sr. High School,
attended this conference during the last week of August.
Personnel in the -Sr. High School consisted of Mrs. Lucy Chartrand as the Cook and Mrs.
Gertrude Solovei as the Baker; while the General Workers were Mrs. Mary Thibault, Mrs. Gloria
Beaulac, Mrs. Mary Haueisen, Mrs. Anita Forest, Mrs. Ella Nicholson and Mrs. Barbara Lewis, a
substitute worker. Mrs. Ethel Nolan, one of our most faithful workers for a number of years, passed
away last summer after having been ill for some time.
Personnel in the Elementary School were Mrs. Rita Seguin, Manager; Mrs. Hazel Whitney,
Cook; and Mrs. Bertha Geigner as the Baker. The General Workers were Mrs. Genevieve Nass, Mrs.
Annette Thibodeau, Mrs. Phyllis Collelo and Mrs. Ellen Brown. Due to the closing of the Elementary
cafeteria, it was necessary to reduce the number of our personnel for 1966-67. The Jr. -Sr. High
School cafeteria was staffed in September 1966 by personnel from both schools on the basis of length
of service. This was an unpleasant task since the program depends so much upon these people who
have all been faithful workers.
In closing, I wish to express my thanks to all those connected in any way with the program --
Mr. Brown, the School Committee; the Principals; the Town Personnel and Boards; as well as Mr.
Archambault and the Custodians. I wish to thank particularly my own personnel for their hard work
and dedication.
Respectfully submitted,
(Mrs.) PHYLLIS W. PROUTY, Supervisor

School Building Committee
The present School Building Committee was appointed in October, 1965 and since that timehas completed a restudy of school
building needs. After fifteen months of continuous study and evaluation the Building Committee concluded as follows:
1. The classroom need is urgent and pressing as evidenced by: (a) The need to use present elementary cafeteria as
classrooms. (b) The need to rent off-site space. (c) Overcrowded Jr.-Sr. High School caused by presence of three
6th grade classes. (d) The need for additional lab facilities in Jr.-Sr. High School. (e) Delay in curriculum dev-
elopments at the Jr.-Sr. High School level caused by uncertainty about building program.
2. There should be no further delay in starting to build classrooms.
3. The impact of the recently enacted Willis Report was interpreted in our planningas: (a) Increased library facilities
at both elementary and secondary levels. (b) Rooms for public kindergarten. (c) Lower teacher to pupil ratios.
This work culminated in the conclusion that two possible building programs could solve the present school housing needs and
the projected growth through 1975-76. The majority of the Committee felt that a 22 classroom Middle School located adjacent to
present school property would best solve the school housing needs. This school was proposed to house grades 5, 6 & 7 and possibly
some classes of the 4th grade. The school would contain, in addition to the classrooms, specialized spaces for Art, Music, Home-
making, Shop, Science Labs, Language Lab and a Reading Machine Room. Since the Middle School is a relatively new concept the
Committee was advised to consider the long range needs of the Community and decide what function this school wouldultimately
fulfill. It was obvious that this school would ultimately serve as a Jr. High School. Thus, the design of the school had to be
basically that of a Jr. High School. A minority of the Committee felt that the best solution would be an 18 room elementary
school plus a 7 classroom addition to the present Jr.-Sr. High School. The proposed site for the elementary school was the town-owned
land on Pratt Street.
Since the two proposals were estimated to provide essentially equivalent facilities with little cost difference the Committee
voted to present both proposals to the Town for a preference vote. A bond issue would then be voted for the preferred proposal. This
plan was followed and a bond issue for $1,541, 500 to construct a Middle School was voted on January 16, 1967 by 748 to 132. The
Committee was empowered to proceed with building this school for occupancy by September 1968. Stoner Associates of Boston is the
architect for the building program.
The Committee wishes to express their thanks to the many Town Committees which assisted in the successful completion of this
study. The Committee also wishes to thank the citizens of Lunenburg for positively voting to provide adequate educational tacilities
for the children of Lunenburg.

Regional Vocational School
The Montachusett Regional Vocational Technical School District Committee has completed its first year of
operation. Any questions concerning the work of the Committee should be directed to its membership. The meetings
of the Committee are open to the public and visitors are welcome to attend.
The District was made up of two cities, Fitchburg and Gardner, and ten towns: Ashburnham, Ashby, Barre,
Harvard, Hubbardston, Lunenburg, Phillipston, Royalston, Sterling and Winchendon. On December 8, 1965 the
organizational meeting took place and the following Officers of the Committee were elected: Chairman, Orren F.
Bradstreet of Lunenburg; Vice Chairman, Francis A. Carbone of Fitchburg; Treasurer, Morrill G. Sprague of Harvard
and Secretary, Arthur H. Murphy of Winchendon. It was voted that the regular meetings be held on the first and third
Wednesday of each month and the meeting time for the Committee was set for 7:30 to 10:00 P.M. Meetings were held
at the Fitchburg City Hall and Gardner City Hall until an office was rented at 759 Main Street, Fitchburg. The Bulletin
Board in the Fitchburg City Hall was designated as the official posting place of the Committee.
It was voted to amend the site area to include an area within a six mile radius instead of the original four as
proposed in the Agreement. Two amendments to the Agreement were voted. Amendment Number One had to do with
the increase in the area of the site selection and Amendment Number Two was for the admission of Westminster to the
District. Both amendments were subsequently approved by all members of the District and the Town of Westminster,
which became a member of the District effective June 23, 1966. Although incorporated in the Agreement (Section III)
it was voted unanimously by the Committee on February 16, 1966 to incorporate Grades 13 and 14 in the original plans
for the school.
It was voted to retain the New England Merchants National Bank of Boston as fiscal agent, and Storey, Thorndike,
Palmer & Dodge as Bond Counsel and to engage the services of Attorney Bernard D. Ward of Fitchburg as Legal Counsel
for the District. Attorney Ward has proved to be of invaluable service to the Committee and doing a most excellent job
in a very involved business. The District School Committee voted to engage the architectural firm of Korslund, Le Normand
and Quann, Inc. of Norwood, Massachusetts to design and draw the plans for the proposed school. Other Regional
Vocational-Technical Schools planned by this firm include South Shore, Greater Lawrence, Southeastern, Fall River
and French King.
Representatives from forty-five area industries and businesses have written to, or called the Committee indicating
an interest in serving with the Committee as an Advisory Group to help with the planning and operation of the School.
This spirit of cooperation and interest is indicative of the importance these people place on Vocational-Technical educa-
tion. Meetings with members of this Advisory Group will be planned to review proposals for courses in the various occupa-
tional categories. Meetings have been held with Mr. John Hodgen of the School Building Assistance Bureau, Mr. Richard
Clark of the New England Merchants National Bank of Boston, and Mr. James Burke of the Bureau of Vocational Education.
On July 20, 1966, Walter B. Dennen, Administrator for the Worcester Vocational School Department, addressed
the Committee on Trade and Industrial Education. On July 27, 1966, a Curriculum Committee meeting was held at the
District Office. In attendance with the Committee and Superintendent-Director were Miss Grace Nangle, R. N.,
Supervisor of Health Services for the Bureau of Vocational Education, President Arthur Haley of Mt. Wachusett Community
College and Professor Leon Bedard. It was strongly advocated by Miss Nangle that space be provided in the initial
school plans for a nursing laboratory and a flexible lab with moyable equipment for training operating room technicians
and other assistants in health occupations as needed. With the exception of Nurse Aide training, which is recommended
for vocational home economic pupils in Grade 11 or 12, all of the recommended health training would require one year
at the post-high level. Providing an associate degree program for registered nurses is strictly a community college function.
The Committee met with John 1.Buckley, Field Coordinator, Economic Development Administration, Bostory officials
from the firm of Korslund, LeNormand & Quann, members of the District Finance Committee and the Superintendent-
Director and steps were initiated to secure Federal Funds to assist in planning and construction. Earlier the Architect
met with the Superintendent-Director and members of the Site Committee at the District Office and then proceeded to

An operating budget for the year 1966 was finalized at the December 15, 1965 meeting. The total budget was
for $21,425.00. What to do about the Capital Cost Budget has been continually discussed and tabled for further study
and discussion. It is essential that all possibilities for State and Federal financing be checked and that a reasonable and
adequate request be carried to the communities for the Capital Cost Budget. On November 16th the 1967 Operating

recommended that $12,500.00 be made available to the District to defray the cost of the Superintendent-Director's
salary for the first year. It may be possible that a request will be submitted to the member communities in 1967 for
Capital Cost Bond Issue monies for the planning costs which will be needed by the Committee to carry on the business
of site procurement, surveys and tests of the site, etc.
The official name for the School and District was voted by the Committee on May 4 and 18, 1966. The Montachusett
Regional Vocational-Technical High School and the Montachusett Regional Vocational-Technical School District were
the names selected. On October 19th, the name of the school was changed to Montachusett Regional Vocational -
Technical School. This leaves out the word" 'High" for the school, as proposed, is to be an educational center for youth
and adults alike in order to better serve the communities.
On June 1, 1966 the Committee voted to elect Mr. Franklin M. Angevine as Superintendent-Director for the
District and School. Mr. Angevine served eleven years as Director of Fitchburg Vocational High School and formerly
headed the Machine and Tool Drafting Department at Worcester Trade High School. He assumed his duties as of July 1,
1966. Mrs. Margaret L. Aubuchon was engaged to fill the position of Secretary to the Superintendent-Director and began
her duties on July 11, 1966. She is also serving as Clerk for the Committee. The untimely death of Mr. Angevine
occurred in September. The work of the Committee has been slowed down somewhat and Chairman Bradstreet was
authorized by the Committee to do as much as possible on a part-time basis to carry on the work. It was agreed to ad-
vertise nationally for a Superintendent-Director and the Committee is now in the process required to make the selection
which does involve some time.
At a meeting held on December 7th, the Committee unanimously voted to engage Orren F. Bradstreet as Director
of Guidance and Curriculum, effective January 1, 1967, to work on a full-time basis to develop the curriculum and
take care of other work that must be done until a Superintendent-Director is elected. This appointment, including
salary reimbursement, has been approved by the Bureau of Vocational Education and the State Department of Educa-
tion. Mr. Bradstreet has, therefore, resigned as Chairman of the Committee and as Lunenburg's representative.
