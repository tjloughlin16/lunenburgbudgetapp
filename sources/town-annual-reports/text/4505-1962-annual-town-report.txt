John C. Shields, Chairman
David A. Eyles
Normand J. Thompson
Each year, as we address ourselves to the task of preparing our annual report, we scan
reports of past years. It is not surprising to find that the problems with which we dealt dur-
ing the year are similar in kind to the problems which faced other boards in other years.
The problems associated with general health, expanding growth, drainage, general highway
needs, and increasing costs are, indeed, perennial. Such is but the natural and normal
course of events. While it would be desirable, and actually enjoyable, to get ahead and
remain ahead of these problems, nevertheless they serve as a challenge to spur US on to do
a better job. During the year, many of our citizens, singly and in groups, have met with
US to discuss this or that aspect of such of those problems as have engaged their concern.
We come such visitations; we cannot think of a betterway to keepus informed of matters
which require our official attention and action. While we can guarantee attention, we can-
not always guarantee action because of the limitation of funds or facilities.
One of the greatest aids we have in our constant battle to control some of the problems
is our co-ordinated system of permits. The filing of anapplication to do some particular
job automatically alerts the administrative officer or Town employee to the probabil of
need of permits for related matters. Thus, an application for a building permit immediately
signals the need for permits in all areas of zoning, plumbing, and sanitary disposal. The
requirements of a full disclosure of intentions and plans as to the particular project, together
with a follow-up inspection, is of tremendous help in controlling and preventing future pro-
blems for the Town. We that the general public is becoming more with these
requirements as time goes on. Generally, there is a realization of the personal as well as
community need for these regulations and, therefore, a willingness to comply. The Nashoba
Associated Boards of Health, of which we are a member, places a trained person at our dis-
posal once a week, regularly, and more often if needed, to advise and aid us in handling
problems within the areas of sanitation and general health.
The Highway Department's efforts in the White Street project have brought about a ma-
jor accomplishment there. Springtime, 1963, will undoubtedly see the finishing touches to
that project. The paving of the roads with in Rolling Acres has, we think, added to the comfort
and convenience of those living in the area. We have made the construction of sidewalks
a long-range project. In those areas where the need appears greatest, we have constructed
new and extended existing sidewalks. We intend to continue with this practice. The Board
of Selectmen has, for some time now, constituted Whalom Clearing Drainage as a long-term
project and it is estimated that two years will be required to complete it.
The somber and ominous threat of international unrest and discord has caused US to take a vigorous interest in Civil
Defense. Consequently, we have had numerous conferences concerning it and have taken steps to strengthen Civil Defense
in Lunenburg. Because the very lives of our people are involved here, we will continue to focus our attention on the subject.
At the annual meeting of the Selectmen's Association, in Provincetown, we were indeed proud and pleased to
accept "First Prize" for Lunenburg's Annual Town Report for 1962. It was adjudged the best of all town reports in
our population group. Lunenburg was also one of four Massachusetts towns cited in the New England Municipal Re-
port Competition, the New England Council having placed Lunenburg's report in the runner-up position, second only
to Old Lyme, Connecticut. For these honors, for ourselves and in behalf of our Townspeople, we wish to thank and
congratulate our Town Clerk, Mary Padula, and her staff. Only genuine interest and ceaseless effort on their part
made this possible. All of US can be satisfied and justifiably proud in knowing that our Town is better informed
through its annual report than other towns of comparable size, and, no doubt, many of the larger towns. It is our
hope that our Town will continue to conform to such high standard.

One category where your Assessors feel that valuations can be increased is in Land Valuations. Due to the
building boom, real land values have increased but assessed values on land have remained about the same. This is
true not only in Lunenburg but in practically every municipality in the State. The Department of Corporations and
Taxation recommends that in order to equalize the tax assessment and broaden the tax base, each municipality should
institute a revaluation program in the near future.

LEGAL
Robert J. Larkin, Town Counsel
In recent years, there has been an increase in legislation, both Federal and State, the effect of
which is to provide for the distribution of funds at various levels for varying governmental needs,
highway, schools, libraries, and the like. All of such measures are, of course, subject to regulation.
Likewise, there is an increased awareness in cities and towns of much legislation providing benefits
and security for municipal employees, generally. Much of this latter legislation has been in existence
for years. However, due to new concepts in municipal government, increased cost of living, in-
creased cost of government, and an increase in the responsibility of government, the proverbial
"small town" has, in ever increasing numbers, become subject to these laws by which it was hitherto
unaffected for the most part. Some of these laws become effective by mandate and others by adoption
by the voters. Laws providing for zoning, planning, inspection of one kind or another are laws,
which, although of long-standing, are comparatively new in many a so-called "small town". There
are many more, not mentioned here, which, in time, will become applicable in our Town.
All of these varying kinds and types of enactments become basic in operating our Town. These
changes and developments have come about gradually and almost unnoticed, but in retrospect, the
complete departure from things as they used to be is significant enough to call attention to it. The
need for legal guidance, explanation and interpretation, has therefore, become correspondingly greater.
There were the usual legal documents to be prepared during the year such as easements, agree-
ments, deeds, and contracts. Some damage claims, mostly street defect cases, arose during the
year. One pending court case involving a school bus accident was closed out without cost to the Town.
Anappeal case was tried before the Appellate Tax Board resulting in a decision favorable to the Town.
Most of the regular meetings of the Selectmen were attended by the Town Counsel and on several
occasions advice and opinions were given to almost all other Town departments. Legal action was
taken in one or two instances for the recovery of money advanced under the old age assistance
program.
There is presently pending a new law commonly known as the "Conflict of Interest Law". Unless
postponed, the law will become effective on May 1, 1963. There aremany pros and cons concerning
this law. Many feel it should be postponed for further study and revision. Your Town Counsel
favors such postponement, as he is concerned regarding the workability of the law in its present
form. There seems to be no opposition to the desirability and need for such a law. However, it
appears to many who have studied the law that, in its present form, it applies to situations which,
obviously, were never intended to be affected. Such a lack of clarity in a law which will have
wide application will only serve to make enforcement difficult and thereby the original objective
will have been lost.

The image of Massachusetts Town Government has been described as a rural settlement with a
white town hall, staffed principally by a town clerk and numerous part-time boards, several colonial-
style churches abutting an elm-dotted common, a small commercial center where business is trans-
acted on a first-name basis in a rather leisurely, friendly manner, and a long tradition of local
participation in town affairs. Although, fortunately, this picture is an accurate image of the out-
ward appearance of our Town, a closer look at the "balance sheet" discloses that Lunenburg's devel-
opment, through the years, into an urban community performing many of the same municipal functions
as far larger towns do elsewhere, has been masqued by its colonial facade.
Realizing that a form of government which was adequate and suitable for the needs of people
twenty-five years ago could not cope with today's municipal problems without considerable modifi-
cation, we have endeavored through continuous organizational improvements, more effective
utilization of personnel and equipment, and the up-dating of facilities, to reshape the various functions
of the Town Office so as to meet new challenges and new situations and yet preserve much of the
time-honored traditions and customs.
The unusually large number of independent boards, commissions and departments which charac-
terize the administrative structure of most Massachusetts Towns has been described as an administrative
weakness. Lunenburg's unique combining of the administrative functions of the Board of Selectmen,
Board of Health, Zoning Board and Licensing Authority with those of the Town Clerk, Tax Collector
Treasurer and Registrar of Voters, appears to have been administratively sound, and a particularly
convenient arrangement for the Townspeople.
At one time or another the Town Office has been called upon to assist every independent board
and officer in the Community. In addition to managing the office of the Selectmen within the
policies laid down by them, this office serves as a "clearing house" for citizen complaints and
inquiries, participates in the budget-making process in cooperation with the Finance Committee,
and serves as liason with all Town departments.
Excerpts from the Town Clerk's Minutes and detailed vital statistic records are reported elsewhere in
this book. Detailed financial reports of the Treasurer and Tax Collector may also be found on the follow-
ing pages. The Treasurer's report would not be complete without referring to our overall financial picture.
Five years ago, Lunenburg, already listed among the 10 Massachusetts towns with the highest debt ratio,
faced debt approximating 25% of its assessed valuation and a tax rate of $85. Through a reappraisal of
school housing needs, willingness on the part of department heads to tighten their belts and their boot
straps, and most of all, thanks to the tireless efforts of the Finance Committee, as of 1962 we have met
our school housing needs, expanded and improved the functioning of the various Town departments, put
$50,000 aside in a stabilization fund, reduced our debt ratio to 3.7%, and held to an $80 tax rate.

TOWN POUND
LUNENBURG. MASSACHUSETTS
1750-
Town Pound
The Lunenburg Town Pound, located on Highland Street next to Marshall Pond, has been the
object of much interest during the past year. With the generous aid of the Lunenburg Boy Scout
troop, the area has been cleared of brush. With the cooperation of the Town Clerk's office and
the Lunenburg Historical Society, information for a sign was obtained. Incidentally, the first
pound keeper was also the Town's first settler, Samuel Page. It is believed that the Pound, built
about 1750, is one of the oldest pounds in the area.
Donald H. Cragin
Keeper of the Pound

POLICE
Sherwood G. Winchester, Chief
Frederick B. Hobbs, Sergeant
William R. Sullivan, Patrolman
Elwood C. Erickson, Patrolman
The year 1962 was a busy one for the Police Department, which among other things,
found itself confronted with a record number of breaks and larcenies. Whereas most of
the breaks occurred in business establishments, asmall number were in private homes and
two in our public schools. Although the Elementary School break has been solved, as of
the writing of this report the one at the High School has not.
During the year, the Department made 251 arrests. Motor vehicle violations
totalled 126 and continued to top the list. The most common violations recorded were
speeding (46), operating without authority (15), operating to endanger (12), operating
under the influence (9), stop sign violators (9), and operating after suspension (8). One
hundred twenty-five persons were arrested for crimes against persons and public order.
The three most common crimes investigated were intoxication (43), breaking and entering
(17), and larceny (14).
The threat presented by the "Cuban Crisis" brought about several meetings with the
Civil Defense Director, Karl Manelius, and with the Board of Selectmen to discuss overall
emergency plans. Regular police personnel worked with Mr. Manelius assisting wherever
possible. Several members of the Auxiliary Police were assigned to the Civil Defense
Department.
Chief Winchester has recommended that the street numbering project be continued as he considers numbered
houses a valuable aid in effectively protecting persons and property.

Robert 1. Andrews, Captain
Members of the Auxiliary Police accumulated 1850 work hours during 1962. These
men averaged 40 hours at meetings, 60 hours on traffic control and assisted the Civil
Defense Director in the preparation of evacuation maps, and the establishment of check

Two members were appointed to the regular police department. One member has
completed his training for and will be certified as a qualified first aid instructor. In
October, Robert E. Grenier retired as Captain of the Auxiliary Police after havingserved
the Department for 8 years as a member and 6 years as its head. In recognition of this

James P. Deming, Assistant Chief
Henry D. Kelley, Jr., Captain

During the year, the Department responded to 119 alarms: 17 building, 58 grass or brush,
16 car or truck, 6 auto accident, 6 electrical appliance, 6 oil burner, 2 chimney, 2 rescusitator,
5 Town dump, and 1 false alarm. The above numbers totalled 34 more than the previous year.
Under the terms of the Mutual Aid agreement, our Department responded to calls in Shirley,
Leominster and Lancaster. Neighboring fire companies responded to calls in Lunenburg on an equal
number of occasions.
Damage to buildings was estimated at $37,868. Insurance paid to cover this loss was $33,918,

Lunenburg can be justifiably proud of their volunteer fire department.

CIVIL DEFENSE
Karl Manelius, Director
What is the Civil Defense function? Briefly, it is to prepare for and deal with any natural,
accidental or man-caused disaster which is too wide-spread and serious for the usual public safety
forces to cope with. Such preparedness requires continuous planning, organization and training.
Although Civil Defense disaster action is primarily carried out at the local level, an effective civil
defense, in addition to requiring the participation of every citizen, calls for advance planning at
every level of government, local, state and national. Responsibility for organizing community civil
defense protection falls on the state and local governmental units, but because the job is an extremely
difficult one, the Federal Government is preparing to assist the Local Government with technical
help and matching funds for certain programs.
Following the Cuban Emergency, the Board of Selectmen, Police and Fire Chiefs, held several
meetings with the Civil Defense Director to discuss the reorganization of our local Civil Defense
Program. The Auxiliary Police Department came to the immediate assistance of the Civil Defense
Director by voluntarily undertaking and carrying out such vital projects as mapping evacuation routes,
establishing check points to control traffic movements during a disaster and making readiness and
availability checks on public and private radio equipment, radio operators, and heavy duty equip-
ment. One program given highest priority has been the establishment of plans for the schools in the
event of an emergency.
Although not a single town building inspected under the National Shelter Survey could be certi-
fied as suitable for a public shelter, our local Director is in the process of determining which buildings
could offer some measure of protection. Upon completion of this survey, the buildings will be marked
and the Federal Government will be asked to give permission for their use. It is strongly recommended
that every new public building be constructed so as to assure safety against fallout radiation.
The need for strong action to improve local civil defense activities was demonstrated during the
Cuban Crisis. While the intensity of the situation may have waned, the danger certainly is not past.
That these dangers are still very much apparent was pointed out by the President while decorating
fliers for their photographic missions over Cuba, at which time he remarked, "The danger certainly
is not past we shall continue to live in crisis and danger certainly through this decade."
In a recent message, Steuart L. Pittman, Assistant Secretary of Defense for Civil Defense, aptly
summed up the task which is before US as follows: "There is much to be done for ourself-protection
by each community. Having been reminded that we want and need all practical protection against
nuclear attack, let US recognize that effective preparation takes time and effort during the quiet
periods. Let's get on with this work even when the threat fades and there is little appetite for the
dismal subject of survival. We owe this to our Country. It makes US a tougher nut to crack, and
that makes war less likely."

The Dog Officer has failed to submit his annual report. Rumor has it that he has gone to the dogs" but the

TREE WARDEN
Richard W. Brown, Tree Warden
Moth Superintendent
Shade tree planting, the cutting and removal of dead and dangerous trees, and insect control
were the major activities of the Tree Department during 1962. Approximately seventy-two trees
were cut and cleared away. One hundred thirty trees were purchased and planted along the road-
sides with emphasis having been placed on the replacement of trees which had been removed for
one reason or another.
For many years, Massachusetts cities and towns have waged war to bring Dutch Elm Disease
under control. It is difficult to assess the results of this effort, and, as more and more trees succumb,
the swiftness with which the disease has spread may create the feeling that to control it is a hopeless
cause. However, according to the "experts" in the Department of Natural Resources, this is far
from the truth.
Three schools of thought are in evidence among those who are directly concerned with the problem
of control. The first believes that protection from the bark beetles can be obtained by the dormant
application of 12% DDT to the trees. The second group maintains that the disease can be controlled
by prompt and complete removal of the dying and dead elm wood on which the elm bark beetle breeds.
The third belief is that prompt removal of elm wood, which is diseased, a process referred to as
"sanitation", plus a dormant application of DDT on the highly valued trees in the center of Town, is
the most effective method of suppressing the Dutch Elm Disease. Locally, the emphasis has been on
sanitation, mainly because control by spraying, although theoretically sound, is difficult to accomplish
for several reasons. First, the desired results cannot be obtained unless a full dosage of DDT is
applied. In addition to being costly, unwanted side effects may occur when DDT is applied full
strength. It is further impossible to spray a tree on a street in the dormant season without some of
the spray landing beyond the tree. Ideal conditions for spraying occur when the weather is dry and
there is little wind. It has been stated that disease protection cannot be obtained unless enough
material of the right kind is applied at the right time and under the right weather conditions.
That healthy trees in the vicinity of diseased elm trees become infected very rapidly is borne
out in our Town by the fact that all twenty-onesamples taken from elm trees and sent to the University
of Massachusetts during 1962 indicated the presence of the disease. These trees, together with
sixty-nine others which were obviously diseased, were cut, cleared and burned.
The $2,499.65 spent for the control of this disease during 1962 did not allow for any spraying.
An effective program against Dutch Elm Disease in any town would include spending the few favor-
able spring days spraying the critical trees in the center of Town. The $3,000 allocated for the
control of public nuisances in the 1963 Budget and the time allotted does not allow for a spraying
program.

and beautiful fields, through the brambles and briers, up and over ledges, among the trees and through the forests,
studying the wild life and enjoying the natural beauty. They didn't find Niagara Falls, the Grand Canyon, the Great
Lakes, the Mississippi River, or anything as grand or on as large a scale. They did, however, find so many fascinating
scenes, winding brooks, rugged ledges, tall forest trees and wild life retreats that they were filled with apprehension
for fear that Lunenburg could lose all of this, as has happened in so many places where the population has exploded
and land, water, forest and wildlife resources have been used unwisely or destroyed.
Fortunately, Lunenburg is, and has been, a "Conservation Minded" town from whence have come many outstanding
naturalists and conservationists. For many years the Grange, the Women's Club, the Girl Scouts, the Boy Scouts,
4-H and Sportmen's Clubs have worked enthusiastically to preserve and protect our natural resources. The Lunehburg
Grange has made outstanding contributions to the advancement of conservation in our Town. Conservation cannot
wait, nor is it the concern of conservationists alone. To develop and use our natural resources to provide for the
needs of people today and insure their continued service to generations yet unborn should be of vital concern to each
and every one of US.
The Conservation Commission, with the help and advice of many local sportsmen, other Townspeople, and experts
in the field of natural resources, and with the encouragement and advice of the Massachusetts Department of Natural
Resources, has designated as desirable for conservation areas, approximately two thousand acres of land. These areas
can, by purchase, gift, or easement from the owners, become Town-controlled. Members of the Conservation Commis-
sion and of the Town Forest Committee (who meet together once a month) have, together with the Planning Board,
charted on aerial maps the two thousand acres so designated.
The so-called "Cowdrey Lot" has been described from a conservation standpoint as being the most desirable parcel
of land in Town. This land, through which Mulpus Brook flows, contains approximately two hundred ten acres, includ-
ing seventy acres of fifty-year old pines, seventy acres of hardwood, and seventy acres of wet land. Under the Con-
servation Fund Act a Town can appropriate for conservation purposes, in any one year, one-twentieth of one percent
of its total assessed valuation. The Conservation Commission recommends that the $4800 appropriated in 1962, and
$4310 of the $5300 requested for this purpose in 1963, be transferred for the purchase of the "Cowdrey Lot". As this
lot has already been approved by the State, approximately fifty percent of the $9110 purchase price will be reimbursed
to the Town by the State.

WELFARE
Gladys M. Goodale, Director
The major objective which public welfare agencies at all administrative levels are expected to
achieve is the improvement of social services to strengthen family life, the safe-guarding of child-
ren in hazardous home situations, and the reduction of dependency through an effective program of
rehabilitation services. This objective, which was set forth by the United States Department of Health,
Education, and Welfare, indicates the new direction for public welfare programs on the national level.
The primary responsibility of the Department of Public Welfare is concerned with the administra-
tion of financial assistance to eligible needy persons covering five categories of relief. Four of
these categories are part of the Federal Social Security plan and the cost of relief is shared by the
town, state, and federal governments according to a specific formula.
These divisions are (1) Old Age Assistance, (2) Aid to Dependent Children, (3) Disability Assis-
tance, (4) Medical Assistance for the Aged. The fifth category is General Relief which covers those
in need who do not qualify for the Social Security plan. This expense is borne almost entirely by the
town.
None of the five categories is to be considered a form of insurance or pension, but is a program
under which assistance is made on the basis of need.

skating pond, one outdoor basketball court and a running track and jumping pits.
The cost of maintaining these areas in 1962 was $986.97. In addition to usual playground main-
tenance, this figure includes the cost of extensive work on the baseball diamond at Marshall Park,
reconstruction of the High Jumping and Broad Jumping Pits, plowing and general maintenance of
Marshall Pond and repairs to playground equipment. We are once more indebted to Mr. Irving
Kimball, who, on a voluntary basis, trimmed numerous trees in the various park areas.
Although by and large the recreational program is still geared to the needs of the young people,
increasing emphasis on physical fitness has caused the Park Commission to reappraise their respon-
sibilities and include in the plans for the future additional leisure time activities for adults. This
year the adult program included softball and horseshoe leagues and tennis instruction and tournaments.
Attendance at the summer program was greater than ever before and all phases of the program
were met with enthusiasm. Mr. Emory Merrill served as Director of the program. Miss Linda Knight
supervised the center playground and Miss Helen Rinki the one at Wallis Park. Mrs. Irene Maxim
headed the arts and crafts programs with Mrs. Doris Robinson serving as her assistant. In addition
to general playground activities the summer schedule included arts and crafts, basketball leagues
for boys and girls, boys baseball, girls softball, tennis instruction, track, and several special events
such as the Soap Box Derby, a trip to Benson's Wild Animal Farm and swimming and picnicing at
Willard Brook. A highlight of the summer program was the winning of the interstate league and play-
offs by the Girls Softball Team. Skating and ice hockey continued to highlight the winter program.
At the Annual Meeting in March, the Park Department was authorized to enter into a new
lease with the owners and operators of Aqualand, subject to substantially the same terms and condi-
tions as in the original agreement with the following exceptions: that the lease could not run for
more than three years; that the yearly admission charge to residents of Lunenburg be fixed at no
more than $2.00; that tickets may be purchased on an accrual basis; that the closing hour may be
changed to 9:00 P.M.; and that residents be given free use of the dressing rooms.
Articles to be inserted in the 1963 Annual Meeting Warrant will include a request for $1,500
for a little league fence and backstop at Marshall Park; $3,294 to resurface and Jennite the courts
at Wallis Park; and $800 for the purchase of a second-hand truck.
In 1962, the total cost of carrying out the various Park Department programs and maintaining
the several park and playground areas was $7,882.43.

The Cemetery Commissioners held seventeen meetings during the year to plan the maintenance
of the two Town-controlled cemeteries. In addition to mowing, trimming and raking of leaves, in-
cluded in the maintenance program were; filling, grading, loaming and seeding a section of the
North Cemetery which will be available for the sale of lots in the near future; filling and reseeding
of sunken graves; improvements to the older sections; and other minor improvements in both cemeteries.
The roads in both cemeteries have been kept open during the winter months. The Cemetery
Commissioners are indebted to the Board of Selectmen and Highway Superintendent with this regard.
During the year, the practice of winter burials was inaugurated.
An air compressor, complete with attachments, was purchased from the P. K. Lindsay Company
with funds appropriated for this purpose at the Annual Meeting. This equipment has, on several
occasions, been used by the Highway Department.
The grave of Lunenburg's first settler, Samuel Page, was reseeded. The iron fence which
surrounds this grave and the plaque which commemorates his memory were refinished.
In a report to the Board of Selectmen, the remaining members of the Cemetery Commission have expressed regret that Mr. Raymond
Butterfield, who has taken an active part in the operation of our cemeteries for over thirty years, will not be seeking re-election.
Mr. Butterfield's extensive knowledge of and interest in the cemeteries will be greatly missed. A dedicated public servant, he has
always given freely of his time and knowledge.

QUESTION NO. I
PROPOSED AMENDMENT TO THE CONSTITUTION
Do you approve of the adoption of an amendment to the constitution summarized below which was approved by the General
Court in a joint session of the two branches held May 13, 1959, received 143 votes in the affirmative and 118 in the negative,
and in a joint session of the two branches held March 29, 1961, received 144 votes in the affirmative and 121 in the negative?
SUMMARY: The proposed amendment imposes no new taxes but gives the General Court the power to pass an income tax at
graduated or proportioned rates and is a change in the constitutional limitations now in effect on the power of the General Court
to pass tax measures. The proposed amendment would add a new Article to the Constitution of the Commonwealth by which full
power and authority is granted to the General Court, in the alternative to the power and authority to tax incomes in the manner
provided in Article XLIV of the Amendments to the Constitution, to impose a tax on incomes at rates which are proportioned or
graduated according to the amount of income received, irrespective of the source from which it may be derived, and to grant
reasonable exemptions, deductions and abatements. It further provides that any property the income of which is taxed under the
provisions of the proposed Article may be exempted from the imposition and levying of proportional and reasonable assessments,
rates and taxes as at present authorized by the Constitution, and that the Article shall not be construed to limit the power of the
General Court to impose and levy reasonable duties and excises.

Superintendent's Annual Report
A few years ago I came across a statement in a bank window which read something
like this, "Good luck is the lazy man's definition of a worker's success", I have
often reflected on the genuine truth there is in such a statement. Its truth applies
to our school system, for the success it now enjoys has come about through the com-
bined, dedicated, and often frustrating, efforts of many sincere and conscientious
people; Teachers, Secretaries, Custodians, Lunch personnel, Administrators, School
Committee, Finance Committee and the Citizens of the Town.
We live in a time when probably the greatest asset remaining to America is the
potential which lies in its youth. Without question, our future greatness, yes, even
our survival as a great nation, depends on them. On US lies the burden and respon-
sibility to think and see big enough that Lunenburg children receive the advantages
of education which are second to none. To continue to fulfill this obligation is a
real challenge to the various interested peoplesin our community.
The operation of the Lunenburg Public Schools has become big business when we
consider the fact that about 4/5 of a million dollars will be spent this year in
support of schools, With this fact in mind, the School Committee moved in the
direction of setting up a new Budget format which makes use of a new system of
Accounting. This was a rather important decision to make since it involved indoc-
trinating all personnel associated with the School System, not only with a new Super-
intendent, but also with a new Budget format. This step was taken in anticipation
of action by the Massachusetts Department of Education in requiring this system of
Accounting for all school systems in Massachusetts. This Accounting system is
currently used and recommended throughout most of the nation. Massachusetts is
nearly alone in not using a similar standard system. The financial accounting
manual used in the changeover was developed through the efforts of five nationwide
educational organizations and the United States Office of Education over a two-year
period. With the installation of IBM machines at the State level, all school
systems within the Commonwealth will be required to change to this commonly used
system of Accounting within two years. Lunenburg has chosen to make this step
before being required to do SO. The major advantages for such a move are threefold.
(1) It establishes a sound basis of Cost Accounting; (2) It should improve the
accuracy of local, State and National summaries of expenditures; and (3) It should
facilitate comparisons of financial information among communities and among states.
So that we may have some basic understanding of this system, we are providing a
simple breakdown with thumbnail descriptions of each category. In future budgets,
we will try to relate each year's request to these accounts in our report so that
citizens may be able to see where increases or decreases in the Appropriation
Request are reflected. It has been especially gratifying to me to see the compe-
tent way that Teachers, Department Heads, Clerical personnel and Principals have
adjusted to this new system. The cooperation of the Finance Committee in under-
standing and approving this format has been especially gratifying. Such understand-
ing cooperation has made this transition effective, successful and relatively smooth.

- SECONDARY PROGRAM OF STUDIES -
American educational practices, since the advent of Sputnik in 1957, have been
the target for much criticism. It has become almost popular to berate what we are
doing in our schools. This alertness on the part of interested citizens has been
good for education since it has kept educators continually alert to the need for
constant self analysis SO that our educational program might be truly meaningful and
effective. With this in mind, self analysis, evaluation of the Program of Studies
at the Junior-Senior High School was initiated about two years ago which culminated
in the adoption of a new Program of Studies at the secondary level which became
effective in time for the 1962-63 academic year. More than a year of study was re-
quired in its development involving the combined efforts of teachers working with
their respective Department Heads and supplemented by weekly meetings of the Admin-
istrative Staff, Department Heads, and Supervisors to coordinate the program being
made. This new program was an effort to upgrade and strengthen the school's offer-
ings by incorporating new courses in some instances while deleting others in the
light of recently developed needs, demands and expectations of high school graduates.
After presentation to the school committee for their study and after several joint
meetings, the new Secondary Program of Studies was officially approved in April of
1962.
This Program required that all High School students must take five(5) required
major subjects rather than the traditional four (4). With this increased require-
ment, the number of earned credits to graduate was stepped up to eighteen (18)
rather than the traditional sixteen (16). It has been interesting to note that a
considerable number of students have through their own initiative and through the
advice of the Guidance personnel taken six (6) major subjects this year with
apparent success, With the increase in the number of required subjects, additional
courses had to be provided. Earth Science, a second year of Biology and Botany
combined, Conversational French for the non-college bound student, more advanced
languages, and specialized English and History courses were added. To make such
additional courses available brings with it the ever increasing cost of education.
New offerings required additional textbooks, increased laboratory materials and all
the equipment that make the advances meaningful. To accomodate the increased re-
quired courses the Activity period during the day had to be eliminated. This was a
time when special interests in Music, Art, Dramatics, Publications and other co-
curricular subjects functioned. With the addition of this time to the subject
areas, the academic day has in effect been lengthened to about six (6) hours allow-
ing twenty-five minutes for lunch. Indeed, the Academic time has increased by
moving the co-curricular interests to the end of the day which has created problems
for these areas of interest. In a community such as Lunenburg where a large major-
ity of students are "bussed" many transportation problems are encountered as the
result of a wide scope of after-school activities. Possibly, we are saying that
some sort of transportation should be provided for those participating in after
school activities although this opens up a whole new area of expense. This is a
matter which needs honest reflection and a hoped-for solution.

Careful planning and thinking on the part of parents is vitally necessary to
aid the student's acceptance for college training. It is not too soon to begin
making college plans by the eighth grade. This should include conferences with
teachers and guidance counselors to determine the student's scholastic aptitudes
and achievement.
Visits to college campuses should be initiated by at least the tenth grade,
as college costs are rising, with a prediction from the Federal Office of Education
that they double by 1970. If the economy continues to spiral upward, parents are
urged to plan several years before college enrollment for means of meeting college
expenses. Most parents will find it helpful to consider the various financial aids
available. These are: (1) Working your way; (2) Scholarships; (3) Grants-in-aid;
(4) Loans (Federal, State and Private); (5) Deferred tuition payments; and (6) In-
surance plans. Detailed information about these assistance plans is available from
your guidance services. Parents should encourage their sons and daughters to make
inquiry about these plans and schedule an interview with a counselor for the
parents' information.
- ADULT EDUCATION PROGRAM -
On September 30, 1957, the community voted an enabling act to establish and
- 98 -
===PAGE 101===
maintain State-aided Vocational Education. Quoting from the Town Report of that
year: "This vote indicates the result of a growing community and, the desires of
its citizens for programs to expand their knowledges in useful, beneficial and
cultural directions." This program started February 27, 1958 with three classes,
and opened again on October 9, 1958 with five classes: two in Basic Clothing, one
each in Upholstery, Rug Bralding, and Decorated Ware.
In the interim period the program has had its difficulties with money not
being appropriated to continue the program. Currently, however, the program is
being carried on with five courses being offered as follows: Basic Sewing, Advanced
Sewing, Rug Braiding, Upholstery and Furniture Refinishing. These courses cover
two ten-week periods during the year with half of the cost for the program being
reimbursed by the State. Currently, eighty-four citizens are participating in the
in-town program, while sixteen citizens are enrolled in Adult courses in other
communities taking courses not offered in Lunenburg since we do not have the
specific facilities for these courses.
The Advisory Committee for this program and the School Committee are anxious
that any evening Adult Program might truly reflect the interests and desires of the
community; they have sent out a questionnaire to parents asking them to indicate
their interests. With this information at hand, it is the hope that the program
might be enhanced in 1963 by using the State-aided Vocational courses as a nucleus
and adding possibly limited academic or cultural courses reflecting Community needs
as determined by the survey. These additional courses would have a reasonable
tuition fee that should make the course practically self-supporting. It is my sin-
cere hope that this program as a result of current plans will come to be one which
will truly fill Adult Education interests of the community.

School Nurse
SIGNE E. SCHULTZ, School Nurse
Following is a report of school nursing for the year:
Booster shots for diphtheria-tetanus and Salk polio vaccine were made available
to students in Grades 1, 6, and 11. One-hundred fifty-two diphtheria-tetanus and one
hundred ninety-nine polio boosters were given.
Mantoux testing for tuberculosis were done on children in Grade 1. Five had a
positive test and were required to have a chest x-ray along with members of their
families. X-ray findings were negative in all cases, A large number of teachers,
school personnel and bus drivers had Mantoux tests and chest x-rays as required by law.
Sabin Oral Polio Vaccine became available in the Spring to children under seven
years. Types I and III of the oral vaccine were offered to children in Grade 1. They
were very well received as evidenced by the fact that all but a very few were given
permission to participate. This vaccine has now become available to all age groups
and we are now scheduling dates for giving it throughout the schools.
A dental fluorine program is held annually for children in grades 2, 5, and 8.
Nearly all children participate in this program which includes cleaning and exam ination
for dental defects and application of stannous fluoride. Miss June Duteau is the
Dental Hygienist-in-charge Notices urging early dental care for thosewho are in
need of it are sent home.
Through the sponsorship of the P. T. A., a dental education program was carried
out during Dental Health Week in February. The talks and demonstrations were given
in Grades 1, 3, and 4 by Mrs. Clarence May, Dental Hygienist.
Vision and hearing tests are given annually to all students. Notices of defects
are sent home and most of the forms have been returned signed by an eye, ear, nose
and throat specialist.
Physical examinations are made routinely involving children in Grades 1, 4, 7,
and 10 by Dr. John DeLeo, School Physician. All students who participate in competa-
tive sports have annual physical examinations.
The Future Nurses Club which was organized last year continues to be popular; the
monthly meetings are well attended. Members of the group have assisted us in our work
on several occasions and their assistance is much appreciated.
In closing, I wish to express my appreciation to the school administrators, the
teaching staff, the school personnel and volunteer helpers, who have cooperated in
making the school nursing program successful.

School Lunch Program
RUTH A. CORRELL, Supervisor
This is my Seventh Annual Report of the Lunenburg School Lunch Program and is
for the school year, September 1961 to September 1962.
During this period we fed 161,810 "Type A" lunches to students and 10,518 lunches
to adults for a period of 171 lunch days, which is an average of 1,007.7 lunches per
day in all schools combined, Beyond these figures, we sold to students 133,052 half-
pints of milk under the United States Department of Agriculture Special Milk Act Pro-
gram, as well as 4,511 half-pints to adults. That is a total of 309,891 half-pints
of milk purchased for all schools during the school year.
Financially at the end of 1961 the program ended in a deficit of $271.71 which
was paid by a transfer of $2,000.00 by the Finance Committee. After the deficit was
paid, this left an opening balance of $1,728.29 for the beginning of September, 1961.
After all bills were paid, September ,1962, there was a balance of $4,051.16 for the
beginning of the school year 1962.
Commodities donated by the United States Department of Agriculture were a big
factor in helping the program during the year. We received butter, lard, frozen lamb,
beef, whole wheat flour, rolled wheat, dried milk powder, pea beans, peanut butter,
cheeses, potatoes, dried raisins, rice, and fresh cranberries, canned cranberries,
corn, tomatoes, applesauce, sliced apples, peas, sauerkraut, grapefruit sections,
pumpkin, red sour cherries, peaches, green beans, apricots, and canned pork and gravy.
These are available different months and in quantities in keeping with the number of
students fed. The total valuation of these donated commodities was $15,962.40.
Participation percentage-wise for the year in the Junior-Senior High School was
77% according to attendance and 74% according to membership. In the Elementary School,
participation according to attendance was 67% and 63% according to membership.
Personnel in the Junior-Senior High School consists of Mrs. Dorothy Maki as the
Head Cook, and Mrs. Emelian Perry as the Baker. The other workers are Mrs. Edith
Burger, Mrs. Myrtle Dickey, Mrs. Edna Huff, Mrs. Fannie Ovaska, Mrs. Florence Scionti
and Mrs. Mary Thibault. In June, Mrs. Dickey and Mrs. Scionti resigned for personal
reasons and were replaced by Mrs. Ethel Malone, who was with us before, and Mrs. Louise
Nicoll. Mrs. Maki had to resign in August because of ill health and Mrs. Lucy Char-
trand, from the Elementary School, took her place.
The personnel in the Elementary School consists of Mrs. Phyllis Prouty as the
Manager, Mrs. Hazel Whitney, the Head Cook, Mrs. Helen Hebert as Assistant Cook, and
Mrs. Lucy Chartrand, the Baker. The general workers are Mrs. Elizabeth Gilchrest,
replaced by Mrs. Genevieve Nass; Mrs. Bertha Geigner, and Mrs. Rita Seguin. Mrs.
Seguin replaced Mrs. Chartrand as the Baker and a new worker, Mrs. Irene St. Jean,
was hired.
I feel we have had a very fine and dedicated staff in the lunch program for the
past two years and it shows in our financial report.

At the Thanksgiving Dinner this past November 20, 1962, we served 1,607 "Type A"
lunches. At our Christmas Dinner, we served 1,569 lunches on December 20, 1962. The
Elementary Annex pupils joined us at the Junior-Senior High School to enjoy the holi-
day meals. The Elementary grade students and teachers made very appropriate table
decorations for the holidays and Mrs. Drumm's art students decorated the High School
cafeteria very beautifully.
The Annual Spring School Lunch Workshops were held in Boston and Westfield for a
three-day period during our April vacation. The cafeteria personnel split into thirds
so a group attended each day of the workshop in Boston. I attended the three days in
Westfield and received a certificate.
Mrs. Prouty, the manager in the Elementary Cafeteria, and I, attended the Super-
visors and Managers Conference at the University of Massachusetts for the second year
after school ended in June. Lectures and courses were given by the staff of the Uni-
versity and other State universities. We were assigned to different classes in
accordance with the particular position held in order to make the meeting most mean-
ingful. Both of us received certificates from the University of Massachusetts,
The Annual Convention of the Massachusetts Food Services Association was held
this year in Worcester at the Sheraton Hotel, September 28 and 29, 1962. As I am
presently secretary of the Association, I attended the two day period while Mrs. Prouty,
Mrs. Whitney, Mrs. Chartrand, and Mrs. Perry were in attendance on the second day
only.
On Friday, November 2, 1962, in conjunction with the Teachers' Convention, a con-
vention for all Worcester County School Lunch personnel was held at Oakmont Regional
in Ashburnham. Lunenburg was represented 100%.
I completed a course on "Emergency Feeding", sponsored by Civil Defense, in
Bourne, Massachusetts, doing an actual "cook-out" and demonstration for a couple hun-
dred people.
Due to the change in activities in the High School this Fall, I have had to
train twenty-six students to use the cash registers and make change in the hallways.
Besides these students, twenty-seven boys are available as regulars and substitutes
to clean tables between lunch periods. They are on a rotating basis and seven work
each day for a lunch. This includes the sixth grade in the Junior-Senior High School
as well.
We felt we had to change our cash and money operation this Fall in both schools,
SO a new system of collection was begun in the Elementary School, December 3, 1962.
Eventually I would like to have one register, the same as that used in the High School,
and have everything on a day-to-day basis thereby relieving the faculty of money
collection.
In closing, I wish to express my thanks to you, Mr. Brown, and to the School
Committee, also to the Principals of the schools, the faculties, the town personnel
and boards, to Mr. Archambault and his custodians, and to my own personnel, who are
assuming so much responsibility in making our lunch program truly effective and
successful.
