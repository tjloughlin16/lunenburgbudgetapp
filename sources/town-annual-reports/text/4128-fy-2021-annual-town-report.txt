Emily M. (Moore) Foster, 96, a longtime Lunenburg resident, passed away peacefully on June 28, 2021.
From Cub Scout Den Mother to beloved Children's Librarian to Meals on Wheels volunteer to busy Senior
Center member, Emily was active in the Lunenburg community, and enjoyed golf and bowling leagues and
the "Bookworms" club. Emily loved her "Story Hour" kids from her 20+ years as the Children's Librarian
of the Ritter Memorial Library in Lunenburg. After retirement, she became an active member of the
Lunenburg Friendly Seniors and the Eagle House Senior Center. She also worked for years as a poll worker
for elections in Lunenburg and assisted in the yard sales for the United Parish of Lunenburg.
Robert M. Herrick, "Mickey" Herrick. 73, passed away on May 9th,
2021, surrounded by his immediate family. He was a graduate of
Lunenburg High School during which time he was Vice President of his
class and an accomplished athlete. Mickey continued his love of sports
having played men's softball on numerous local teams throughout his life.
He truly cherished the lifelong friendships and memories that were made
while he was playing. Mickey was a prominent figure in the community
and a local business owner for over 45 years. He was most known for
"Mickey's Place,"
', a Lunenburg staple which served wonderful food and

could be found coaching T-ball, Little League and LHS Varsity Softball,

sports fan and loved spending his free time falling asleep in his chair watching a Boston Red Sox or New

Lunenburg was settled in 1718 and incorporated in 1728. It occupies a land area of approximately 26.4
square miles. Lunenburg is located in north central Massachusetts approximately 43 miles from Boston. It
is bordered on the west by the Town of Ashby and the City of Fitchburg, on the south by the Town of
Lancaster and the City of Leominster, on the east by the Town of Shirley and on the north by the Town of
Townsend.
Local legislative decisions are made by an open town meeting consisting of all registered voters in the town.
Subject to the legislative decisions made by town meeting, the affairs of the town are generally administered
by a five member Select Board and a Town Manager. Local school affairs are administered by an elected
seven member School Committee while local taxes are assessed by a three member Board of Assessors, all
are appointed on an at-large basis for staggered three-year terms.
Lunenburg is a semi-rural community located in northern Worcester County. Three water bodies account
for somewhat of a clustered approach to the town's historical development. Lake Whalom and the former
Whalom Amusement Park delineated the Whalom District from the remainder of Lunenburg. This district
these cities more than of the rest of Lunenburg. The Hickory Hills area around Hickory Hills Lake, was
developed as a private community comprised largely of vacation cottages. The third body of water is
Shirley Reservoir, a public water body also developed with mainly vacation cottages that have now been
modified to year-round residences.
Commercial and industrial development is permitted in several areas of town, primarily along the two major
arteries (Routes 2A and 13) and the town's peripheries, due largely to the close proximity of Leominster's
and Fitchburg's sewer and water systems. Although many farms and orchards had slowly developed into
single family housing, farming continues on in Lunenburg. In 2013 the Annual Town Meeting voted to
establish an Agricultural Commission to represent, preserve and enhance farming in the community and to
administer the "Right to Farm" bylaw.

• The Select Board finalized their Goals for FY 2021 in September of 2020. One of the Select Board goals

regarding the use and/or re-use of the Old Primary School building, and/or the land on which it sits. The
Board held a site visit to the property at the end of October 2020 to review the property and boundaries. In
January the Board met with the Architectural Preservation District Commission and the Historical
Commission about the Select Board plans to see happen with that property and how to navigate a path
forward. The Town Manager and the Select Board worked with Monty Tech on conceptual drawings to
demolish the existing old Primary School building and create an a passive recreation/open space area for
the Farmers Market, band concerts and other community events. At the May 2021 Annual Town Meeting
the Town approved funding to hire a Landscape Architect to prepare conceptual plans for this site and a
cost estimate for the overall project.
• Another one of the Select Board goals for FY 2021 was to review, discuss and revise (where needed) the
re-appointment process of all annual Select Board appointments. In February the Board held a workshop
to formalize their process into an appointment policy. Ultimately the Board mostly deferred to the original
Annual Select Board Appointment Process but defined the timeline and notifying chairs of the committees
of persons wishing to be appointed/reappointed.

• Also in July the Select Board met with Sean O'Neill of Bright Farms (MA Greenhouse LL.C) relative to a
proposed major greenhouse that they were looking to build on the land behind Walmart on Massachusetts
Avenue and their request for a Tax Increment Financing (TIF) Agreement. The proposal included an
initial investment of $10 million and the creation of jobs that would be offered first to Lunenburg
residents. After negotiating with Bright Farms over the terms of a TIF agreement, this was voted upon
favorably at the November Special Town Meeting. The final TIF provided a 10 year tax discount to the
company with a 100% discount in real estate taxes on the new construction in year 1 and decreased by
10% each year of the agreement until year 10 where the Town would be receiving 90% of the real estate
taxes. In Year 11 Bright Farms would be required to pay the full tax assessment. At the November 2020
Special Town Meeting the Town approved the TIF agreement as presented. Ultimately the corporate
offices of Bright Farms chose another location that better fit their business model and the project did not
move forward.
• At the beginning of this fiscal year the Town Manager reported that she worked with the Open Space
Chairman, the Finance Director, DPW, and Conservation Administrator to ensure that all requirements of
the PARC and LAND Grants to purchase the Saliba property were done in order to close on the property
in time to submit for reimbursement of these grants. The Town Manager submitted all the required
documentation for the LAND and PARC grants, totaling $500,000, to ensure the Town received this
reimbursement to purchase this park and open space property.
• The Town Manager and Select Board conducted an annual review of the Select Board's policies in
August and finalized the review in September.
• This year was another productive year for infrastructure projects in Lunenburg. Besides the continuation
of the $4M Pavement Management Plan on local roads, the Town began the reconstruction of the Pleasant
Street Bridge that was completed in the fall of 2021. As part of paving in the area of Whalom Road, the
ADA accessible ramps were incorporated. The Complete Streets design work was finalized during the
fiscal year and the construction portion went out to bid in March of 2021. This project wrapped up in the
fall of 2021. The Town Manager also worked with the DPW Director on continuing the discussions about
the drainage issues on Flat Hill Road and engaging McCarty Engineering to look at drainage solutions.
On another section of Flat Hill Road, a culvert project that had received funding in the FY21 Capital Plan
had to be delayed due to a revised estimated construction cost for the project due to rising construction
and materials cost due to the pandemic that increased the total cost from $428,000 to $750,000. The Town
Manager and the DPW Director worked with the engineer to apply for the Small Bridge Grant Program,
and seek earmark requests from the State. To date the Town is waiting on the whether the Small Bridge
Grant application that was submitted will be approved for funding.
• Due to another building at Tri-Town being built for persons over 55, the Town received a $210,000
Density Bonus Payment per MGL c. 40R (Smart Growth Zoning District) from the Department of
Housing and Community Development. These funds would close out at the end of F21 and positively
impact the certified free cash available in FY22.

The necessary actions in response to the pandemic continued throughout fiscal year 2021. Changes in
legislation, continuous changes to capacity limits, COVID policies and procedures, and the rollout of
vaccines in the latter half of FY 2021 became an integral part of how we needed to approach local
governance, operations, and public health outreach in vaccination efforts.
• The limited availability of vaccines due to the number of doses Massachusetts was allocated from the
federal government and the high demand was a stress on our community during this time. The Council on
Aging assisted seniors with scheduling vaccine appointments and worked with the Nashoba Associated
Boards of Health on a list of individuals requesting vaccines at vaccination clinics.
• The towns within the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health requested state approval to create a regional
vaccination clinic, which was approved by the State in April. The impetus for this was that towns that
were not in a regional health district were being allotted vaccines by population and this was
disproportionate to the limited number each of the towns in the Nashoba Associated Boards of Health
was receiving.
• In March of 2021 the American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) was signed into law by the federal government.
This funding, also called the Coronavirus Local Fiscal Recovery Fund had a distribution of $3.507M for
Lunenburg. At the inception these funds had very restrictive uses related to the pandemic but over the
course of many months the US Treasury issued rules that allowed for flexibility on the uses of these
funds.
• The State extended certain COVID-19 laws such as outdoor seating for restaurants serving liquor and
fully remote public meetings.

The Building Department is located on the second floor of the Ritter Memorial Building located at 960

serves as the Building Commissioner as well as the Zoning Officer. Lisa Normandin serves as the

minimum level of state regulated safety. In addition to Building, Plumbing, Gas, and Electrical Permits, the
Building Department also oversees annual inspections for places of assembly, safety inspections for liquor
serving establishments, and forms of intent required to issue business certificates from the Town Clerk.

1570 total permits issued

software, and all applications for work requiring a permit can be done online. In addition, paper applications
are available at the Building Department office.

DEPARTMENT PHILOSOPHY
Honesty and integrity are absolutes. They characterize the only forum in which anything worthwhile can be
accomplished.
Efficiency: Make every effort to proceed correctly the first time. It may take a bit longer but will not require
repeated attempts.
Loyalty up and down the chain of command is essential to success.
Every employee is essential. Those at the top are support people. The real work is done by the men and
women who drive the trucks, operate the equipment, turn the wrenches, and provide a seamless flow of
administrative support.
DEPARTMENTAL OBJECTIVES
Public Works staff is either working or on call 24 hours a day 365 days a year prepared to respond to any
weather, infrastructure, roadway, or facility related emergency that may arise.
We strive to enhance the awareness of the value Public Works provides to our Town, and ensure others are
aware of our contribution. This is not for an expectation of praise, but rather to make sure efforts are not
dismissed as they merge into the fabric of our everyday expectation of service.
Major events, particularly in the form of devastating storms, over the past years have highlighted the fact that
Public Works has a seat at the table and is a major player in emergency management as first responders.
Town Officials, Police, and Fire acknowledge that Public Works is critical in order to facilitate the required
services of others. We must continue to build on this awareness.
This department must reach out to the public we serve, continuing to make them aware of the fact that the
common services they receive do not just happen, but rather are the efforts of a dedicated staff of individuals,
service companies, and vendors. Safe roadways, wastewater control, solid waste collection and recycling
operations, tree removal, storm water management, grounds maintenance, winter snow and ice removal,
cemeteries and parks are necessary services supporting everyone in the community. We will continue to
engage the public in order to set reasonable expectations and gather support for annual budget requests, capital
requests, and specific projects.
DEPARTMENTAL GOALS FOR FISCAL 2022
• Continue to coordinate administrative directives with the activities of all public service departments and
organizations within the DPW to improve service levels. Strive always for a higher level of efficiency.
• Coordinate with those who work daily in Town facilities to best prioritize capital improvements. DW
plays a major role in the enhancement of the work environment of all Town employees.
• Increase involvement with our insurance provider to make better use of available resources in an effort to
obtain credits toward lessening premiums. Safety is our first concern.
• Encourage and facilitate DPW personnel to obtain and maintain required licensure in order to provide an
optimal level of service.

the DPW. Along with the Storm Water Task Force, Land Use Director, and our Storm Water Consultant the
DPW works to meet the obligations of the permit and required reporting each year.
The DPW provides maintenance and care of the town's storm water management system. This includes but is
not limited to: catch basing cleaning, street sweeping, pipe jetting, pipe replacement and swale cleaning. The
department responds to calls for clogged drains and pipes to help control ponding and flooding.
Wastewater
Working with the Sewer Commission, the DPW continues to be responsible for the day to day operation and
maintenance of the wastewater collection system. Maintenance of the collection system inclusive of 10 pump
stations is outsourced to a specialty vendor but continues to be the responsibility of the DPW. Business
management, inspections, and permitting remain in-house and the responsibility of the DPW.
Tree Removal
Storm damage together with natural attrition creates a backlog of tree felling and pruning that typically
exceeds the department's allotted budget for tree removal. The department strives to fulfill each resident
request but must prioritize work based upon the perceived degree of danger to residents and property. With
the establishment of a Hazardous Tree Survey, a more efficient approach to defects has been established
through prioritization. Currently, the amount of work far exceeds the funding available for it. As this is the
case, the DPW will continue to act in a reactionary manner as needs arise. All work aside from brush
removal, chipping, and the felling of small diameter trees will continue to be outsourced due to the necessity
of a bucket truck or crane and a licensed professional. Each spring DPW staff works to remove and dispose
of woody debris within the town's right-of-way along with a town-wide program of selective curbside
mowing and vegetation clearing. An aggressive program by Unitil in recent years has lessened the burden of
the Town by removing those trees that may cause an interruption of electrical service to the community.
Landfill
The capped, closed landfill off Young's Road remains in compliance with the inspection protocol mandated
by the Division of Environmental Protection. The site is monitored regularly for excessive methane gas as
well as groundwater contamination.
The frequency of the testing reduced through a special permit, as a result of consistently favorable results,
remained constant throughout the year.

Lake Shirley Dam
In the early 1990's with the structure in need of repair/rebuilding, the Lake Shirley Association turned
over ownership of the dam to the Town. After completion of a total reconstruction, responsibilities for
maintenance, monitoring, and record keeping was completed by a small number of volunteers from the
Lake Association. In 2004-2005 custodianship of the dam, compliance with State dam regulations, the
establishment of an operations manual as well as an emergency action plan was placed under the umbrella
of the Department of Public Works. As with the storm water program and other EPA/DEP mandates,
compliance with current and future regulations is critical. We rely on the expertise and support of a

positive report and we remain in total compliance.
Winter Operations
Snow and ice present a major challenge. The safety of the public depends on the ability of the DPW to
maintain our roadways at a high level of safety despite the severity or the duration of snow and icing
events. To that end, we have developed a winter operations procedure based upon a detailed plan with
flexibility to accommodate the unpredictability of each storm. The expectation of immediate service is
high and must be delivered to every part of Town in a timely manner.
Departmental preparedness begins with successful implementation of the Capital Plan. Financial support
is necessary to provide effective fleet rotation ensuring minimal interruption of service due to breakdowns
and repairs. In the fall salt is delivered to the DPW facility. Lunenburg is in a consortium with 14 other
communities in an effort to procure the lowest possible price. From late summer to early fall vehicle
maintenance staff inspects and prepares the trucks, spreaders, and plows for winter operations. During the
construction season road crews address right-of-way issues, overhanging branches, and pavement defects
in order to eliminate obstacles to snow removal. Each division within the DPW is involved in winter
operations. Typically, DPW staff combines with subcontractors to provide comprehensive snow and ice
operations.
Generally, it is not the amount of snow but the duration of the event that determines the overall cost. For
example, a fast moving snow storm of 6-8 inches will cost significantly less than a long duration freezing
rain event even though the accumulation is much less. Historically and as allowed by State law, the
department's snow removal accounts operate in a deficit due to a yearly allocation that is below the cost
of snow removal operations for an average winter.
The Town and its residents are fortunate to have a dedicated group of DPW personnel and private
contractors who tirelessly and successfully challenge the worst nature has to offer regardless of severity or
duration.

The Mission of the Lunenburg Public Schools is to provide all students with the skills, confidence, and

The Lunenburg Public Schools is committed to becoming a leader in education by focusing on its values of

The Strategy is a living document which is reviewed annually and revised as needed. Annually in August,
the District Leadership Team reviews the Strategy, its objectives and action plans. The Strategy objectives
for the next three to four years are:
Transforming Teaching and Learning: To ensure teaching and learning are student-centered and inclusive
by: (1) establishing effective teams of educational leaders to accomplish the essential work for
transforming practice, and (2) hiring and developing excellent educators who are continually learning and
adapting practice to meet the needs of all students.
Cultivating Climate & Culture: To create a culture that values data use and fosters continuous learning in a
climate that is safe, caring, inclusive and reflective of our core values.
Communication & Operations: To improve operational efficiencies, including communication, in order to
increase the organization's capacity to execute the district's mission and vision.
Equity, Access, Achievement & Opportunity for All Students: To ensure all students are provided equal
access to a high-quality education, that is collaborative, interactive, and relevant with an appropriate level
of support and challenge so that they can become well-rounded, resilient, confident, and capable young
adults adequately prepared for future challenges.

Teaching & Learning Models and PD Update
Lunenburg Public Schools returned to a full learning model for the 2021-2022 school year. Students are
invested in learning, and teachers are working diligently to evaluate student progress, identify learning gaps,
make adjustments to practice, and provide scaffolds accordingly.
We have implemented structured common planning time for teachers in grade level content areas so that
teachers may unpack and plan for units of study, align to standards, and look at data and student work. Our
district literacy specialist and our math instructional specialist meet with teachers during common planning
time to support curriculum development and help foster student growth. High school department heads
structure department meeting time to facilitate the necessary curriculum work needed to move each
department forward and improve learning for students.
Students in grades K-8 completed the fall and winter Ready diagnostic assessment in both reading and math,
and teachers are participating in data cycles to analyze data, plan for targeted support, and adjust instruction.
Personalized learning platforms through iReady provide differentiated instruction, systematic practice, and
scaffolded feedback in both reading and math.
The Lunenburg Public School District is participating in the Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS)
Literacy Academy and Social Emotional Learning Academy through the Department of Elementary and
Secondary Education (DESE). The Literacy Academy provides coaching sessions and technical assistance in
specific grade levels for literacy, including a focus on understanding the science of reading and standards-
based lesson planning. Both Academies assist our district with creating multi-tiered systems of support with a
focus on tier I, II, and Ill in academics and social emotional learning and behavioral health.
A K-5 math council including grade level teachers, special services teachers, EL teachers, and administrators,
has been working with DESE's Evaluating and Selecting High-Quality Materials Network to review and
select a core math curriculum. We are planning to begin a field test and pilot of several programs beginning
in February and make a final decision regarding purchase this spring.
Math teachers in grades K-5 have been working with a math consultant and have garnered new strategies for
mathematical discourse routines. Twenty staff members from throughout the district, including guidance
counselors, social workers, and teachers were trained and have been implementing the Trails to Wellness
curriculum, which is an evidence based social emotional wellness program rooted in cognitive behavioral
ideology.
Our Professional Development Committee continues to check-in with the district to evaluate needs and
prepare for upcoming professional learning including future book studies and workshops. Our administrative
team and our curriculum leaders are working with a consultant on workshops pertaining to diversity, equity,
and inclusion and plan to proceed with this work to include the entire district staff. The team is also
participating in a book study based on the book Caste, by Isabel Wilkerson, which takes a close look at social
constructs in the United States. Our team is studying these constructs in connection with our school system
and identifying ways we can improve student inclusion and experiences in Lunenburg.

Lunenburg Middle School
Lunenburg Middle School provides a rigorous academic curriculum as well as strong programs that support
students' social and emotional growth. These programs ensure that each child becomes a self-motivated,
creative, lifelong learner in a safe, stimulating environment that encourages diversity and open honest
communication. The dedicated and accomplished staff of professionals within the school embraces the
philosophy of educating the whole child academically, socially, and emotionally. This philosophy is a
means by which all students become positive community members that possess the skills to be successful in
high school and beyond.
Lunenburg Middle School is in its sixth year as a co-located middle-high school serving grades six through
eight. Effective, student centered middle schools embrace interdisciplinary teams of teachers that enable
teachers to work with smaller groups of students. Smaller groups of students allow teachers to learn and
engage students in their preferred learning style, making them more confident and successful.
Our effective middle school model is student centered and provides opportunities to explore a variety of
different interests and talents through special areas and enrichment classes and extracurricular activities.
One of our goals is for students to discover something they are passionate about and want to continue
exploring as they move through high school and beyond. We will continue to seek out opportunities for
students that foster critical thinking and problem solving skills that prepare them for high school and beyond
or where their passion may lead them to.
Return to full in person instruction
The global pandemic that began in March of 2020 has disrupted and interfered with the method of
instruction our school community has become accustomed to. Since that time, we have participated in fully
remote learning, hybrid model of learning, and in August of 2021, we returned to a full in person learning.
The pandemic took a toll on students, teachers and families, both academic and socially and emotionally.
Lunenburg Middle school has prepared and planned for the full in person return, and has placed personnel
and programs in place to ensure students are serviced in all components of their well-being.
Lunenburg Middle School now has a full time social worker to work with students who may experience an
increased level of anxiety in returning to the building full time. The social worker also is able to run small
groups for students to develop executive function skills, strategies for managing anxiety and stress and help
develop social skills. Our social worker has also done an outstanding job with managing and facilitating
crisis management and assessing student's well-being and aligning out of school services for students and
families.
Athletics and clubs
In our return to a full in person model of learning we also returned to extracurricular activities and athletics
at LMS. In the fall, we successfully participated in middle school Field Hockey and Boys and Girls Cross
Country and this winter we returned middle school basketball after many years of it being eliminated.

the school wide adherence to these new rules seemed to be a quiet demonstration of their understanding. And on May

over a month remaining in the 2020-2021 school year, students and staff welcomed the change, anxious for the halls
and classrooms to return to a more normal capacity. While masks, social distancing and numerous hand sanitizing
stations were still present, the return of students into the building was a welcome sight for all.

homework help, Monday through Friday, to school age children of Monty Tech faculty and staff.

In an effort to build connections with our school supporters and to "show off" the talents of our students, we held our
first Holiday Sale, which quickly sold out! More than 400 charcuterie trays, designed and handcrafted by our

The 2020-2021 school year was unexpected, challenging, and at times, disappointing, yet there were so many
moments to be proud of. I remain humbled and honored to lead such an outstanding organization, and thank you for
your continued support.

The 2020-2021 school year proved to be a test in many ways for the Monty Tech educational community, requiring
1:1 Chromebook initiative, so students and instructors had previous experience with the tools and technology needed
to learn in a remote or hybrid setting. To support the additional and now required use of this technology, the
Technology Department spent countless hours during the school year upgrading the network and increasing the
bandwidth coming into the school to sustain video and audio conferencing.
Department personnel upgraded the learning experience for students, by purchasing laptops for students and teachers,
building new iMacs for the Graphic Communications program, and rebuilding surplus computers for full remote
students in CAD Drafting & Design, Graphic Communications, Business Technology, and Engineering, so that they
may work from home on their vocational assignments. A ZOOM license was purchased, and the Instructional
Technologist worked diligently to purchase and deploy software for all teachers. Camcorders, wireless headsets,
interactive displays and a variety of apps were purchased, installed, and put into the hands of our educators to engage
learners at all levels.
Finally, to ensure "business as usual," we invested in Splashtop, VPN client and configured the firewall, so that
faculty and staff laptops could be fixed remotely if a software issue presented itself, and so that the Business Office
would have remote access into the school to maintain a secure working environment, even when the building was
closed.

Monty Tech School of Continuing Education
The Monty Tech School of Continuing Education had an exciting FY21, receiving the program's first-ever training
grants. School leaders applied for and received Career Technical Initiative Grants (Rounds 1 & 3), totaling $290,000,
as well as a $30,000 Rapid Reemployment Grant. These grants allowed the School of Continuing Education to
rollout new training programs, offered to qualified participants (must be unemployed or underemployed) at no charge.
In addition, each participant who successfully completed the training would be offered job placement with a business
partner. Comprehensive 200-300 hour programs that culminated in industry-recognized credentials in Welding,
HVAC and Allied Health were developed and advertised. While the Allied Health (CNA) program did not see
enough interested participants to field a cohort, HVAC and Welding were successful in their initial endeavor.
Program leaders continue to track student progress and placements, and expect to pursue additional funding for future
offerings.
As we continue to monitor workforce needs and skills gaps, the School of Continuing Education works closely with
the MassHire North Central Career Center to determine specific training programs that are most in-demand. Our
overarching goal to support the North Central Massachusetts workforce readiness pipeline with an increase in career
and licensure courses is evident in new programs that are developed, existing programs that are refined and in
traditional journeyman/apprenticeship programs that remain a hallmark of our institution.
The pivot to a hybrid learning model seemed to be beneficial to both our career/licensure students as well as our
personal enrichment students. During the Fall 2020 and Spring 2021 sessions, we realized the resiliency of our
students and their willingness to persevere with their training despite the challenges. Our instructors worked
tirelessly to maintain training programs timelines.
Our medical students' skill set was and continues to be in high demand due to the pandemic. We were able to
support our local medical facilities with newly licensed healthcare workers, to include Pharmacy Technicians,
Medical Billing and Coding Specialists, EKG Technicians, Phlebotomists, and Patient Care Technicians. We hope to
offer another CNA training program in the future, as the need for trained Assistants remains great.

better prepares students for NACE (Nursing Acceleration Challenge Exam) success at FSU.

involving substance abuse and mental health clients, to help to prepare our graduates to work in a substance abuse
clinical setting.

Registration in Nursing, highlighting goals that include stabilization of faculty positions and pursuance of ACEN
(Accreditation Commission for Education in Nursing) accreditation to more accurately reflect the changing
environment in nursing education.
Looking Ahead
Undoubtedly, the 2020-2021 school year was challenging and difficult for students and staff, yet the school community
demonstrated a remarkable resilience and commitment to education and service that proves, yet again, that Monty Tech
is truly a special place to work and learn. While adapting curriculum to the needs of all students both at home and in-
person, adhering to state and federal safety and health guidelines, and finding opportunities for students to demonstrate
their creativity and technical skills, certainly kept statt busy, there was always an element of improvement, vision, and
forward-thinking that kept moving the school forward. Successful grant applications, new coursework, and increased
school-to-business partnerships that came about during this difficult year are a few of the more notable initiatives that
we expect to have a positive impact on our school and students in the coming years:
Grant funding to improve and expand training programs: Monty Tech has applied for and received six Skills Capital
Grants, totaling $2,029,686. School officials anticipate a FY22 application may be forthcoming, and as a result, have
met to determine which program is in need of a shop renovation, updated equipment, and/or new technology that
reflects current industry standards. The school's Dental Assisting program, which effectively prepares students to enter
the dental field may be the next vocational program to benefit from grant funding, should an application be approved.
Instructors have advocated for a renovated instructional space, as well as new chairs, tools, and equipment, so that
students are trained using the exact instruments and technology found in area clinics. This improved facility would
certainly benefit our high school students, and would also allow Monty Tech's School of Continuing Education to
develop an all-new evening training program, which would help address the critical shortage for trained Dental

Teachers Association's national conference, Monty Tech science instructors are prepared to deliver new course materials
that engage students in important and timely science concepts.
Building partnerships for improved job placement: 2020-2021 brought the school's first-ever approval for a Pre-

demanding workforce.
