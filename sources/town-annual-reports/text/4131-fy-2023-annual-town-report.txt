IN MEMORIAM  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Stanley Barney passed away on July 12, 2023. Stan was  born to the late Wilbur Walker Barney and Therese 
Annette (LaChance) Barney on August 14, 1950. 
Stan is survived by his loving wife Suzanne (Anderson) Barney, daughter Stephanie Barney and her partner 
Alyssa Sharpe, sister Marie Barney, brother Michael G. Barney and wife Janice, nephew Michael R. Barney 
and his fiancée Jillian Doucette, and several nieces, nephews, cousins and extended family. A son, Patrick M. 
Barney, predeceased him in 2019. 
Stan was blessed with a circle of lifelong friends, many of whom were at his side at the time of his passing. 
In his lifetime, Stan was involved in a wide range of activities: working at the family business (Barney’s Res-
taurant and Dairy Bar), caring for animals, gardening, woodworking, and serving as captain of the Blue 
Knights Football Team (#61!). He was an avid Boston sports fan, and was a Celtics season ticket holder for 
many years. An avid gardener, Stan maintained beautiful flower and vegetable gardens. He gave away most of 
the produce and flowers he grew, including pumpkins, jars of pickles he made with his mother and sister, and 
fresh cut flowers. He loved the outdoors and even built his own lumber mill at his residence. Stan loved fishing 
and snowmobiling with his friends. Stan was a life member of the Eastwood Club. 
A 1968 graduate of Lunenburg High School, Stan attended Merrimack College and graduated from Fitchburg 
State University. He married the former Suzanne Anderson of Great Barrington Massachusetts on October 6, 
1973. Devoted to the Town of Lunenburg, Stan served for 38 years on the Lunenburg Police Department, 
moving through the ranks to Sargent in 2000 and finally Lieutenant in 2013. He cherished serving the commu-
nity as a police officer along with his son. Stan was also a lifetime member of the Lunenburg Police Relief As-
sociation, serving as its President in 1984. He also served several terms as a Cemetery Commissioner for the 
Town of Lunenburg, overseeing the maintenance and care of town cemetery grounds.  
Stan was also employed by the law enforcement division of the Massachusetts Society for the Prevention of 
Cruelty to Animals from 1986 to 1991, serving throughout 40 cities and towns. Devoted to animal rescue, Stan 
built a barn/stable at his Lunenburg residence and provided a safe residence for rescued horses, including draft 
horses and Clydesdales.

Lunenburg was settled in 1718 and incorporated in 1728. It occupies a land area of approximately 26.4 
square miles. Lunenburg is located in north central Massachusetts  approximately  43 miles from  Bos-
ton,  It is bordered on the west by the Town of Ashby and the City of Fitchburg, on the south by the 
Town of Lancaster and the City of Leominster, on the east by the Town of Shirley and on the north by 
the Town of Townsend. 
 
Lunenburg is a semi-rural community located in northern Worcester County.  Lake Whalom and the for-
mer Whalom Amusement Park delineated the Whalom District from the remainder of Lunenburg. This 
district was developed as a housing base for many factory workers working in the mills and factories of 
Leominster and Fitchburg. In many ways the characteristics of the Whalom District closely reflect charac-
teristics of these cities more than of the rest of Lunenburg. The Hickory Hills Lake area was developed as 
a private community comprised largely of vacation cottages. Shirley Reservoir is a public water body also 
developed with mainly vacation cottages that have now been modified to year-round residences.

REPORT OF THE TOWN MANAGER 
FY23 
It is my honor to submit the annual report on the activities of the Office of the Town Manager during Fiscal Year 2023. 
There was a substantial amount of progress during Fiscal Year 2023, yet the year proved to be a continuation of the 
effects of the pandemic and a mid-year tax assessment crisis that affected lakefront property owners occurred that im-
pacted the capacity to do more during Fiscal Year 2023. Since 2020, the Town has struggled with staffing levels in var-
ious departments, including attracting and retaining employees. The Town Manager and Assistant Town Manager have 
worked on various ways to address staffing, including recruitment strategies and employee engagement. An interim 
valuation adjustment through the Assessors triggered hundreds of abatement applications to be filed and the effects 
were widespread. In the aftermath of the increases in lakefront valuations, certain processes and corrective actions have 
been undertaken by the Select Board, Town Manager and Board of Assessors that will prevent a similar occurrence 
from happening again.  
Below I would like to highlight the topics that were addressed during Fiscal Year 2023 and the accomplishments with-
in the Office of the Town Manager: 
 In July of 2022, as part of the annual goal setting process, the Selectmen reviewed the and voted to accept the 
Town Manager’s annual goals for FY23.  The Town Manager’s goals for FY 2023 included continuing to take the 
necessary steps towards the redevelopment/reuse of 925 Massachusetts Avenue, enhancement of long term plan-
ning in the areas of capital assets, staffing and infrastructure, and focusing on employee recruitment, recognition, 
and retention. A majority of the action items related to these three goals were completed. 
 In regards to 925 Massachusetts Avenue, the Town Manager continued to work Joe McLaughlin of BETA Group, 
MassDEP, the EPAand MassDevelopment about possible cleanup funding. In FY 2023 the EPA paid for removal 
of the fuel tanks and canopy as part of the Targeted Brownfields Assessment funding. The Town Manager also 
applied for funding through MassDevelopment’s Real Estate Technical Assistance funding to create conceptual 
plans, perform market analysis, and develop a Request for Proposals in order to dispose of the property. As it relat-
ed to one of the action items under the Town Manager’s annual goals for road and bridge planning, the DPW Di-
rector worked with BETA Group to update the Town’s five year pavement management plan and identified the 
costs of creating a similar plan for bridges and culverts, that would be undertaken in FY24. 
 In July 2022, the Select Board set their goals for the upcoming year: they amended their FY22 goal/work on the 
old Primary School at 30 School Street, work with the Town Manager and Finance Director to establish a means-
tested senior citizen property tax exemption or abatement, and lastly to review and update Select Board related by-
laws and policies.  
 The Town Manager reported to the Board in July that the $500,000 Small Bridge Grant that she worked with the 
DPW Director and engineering firm on submitting was awarded to the Town for the Flat Hill Culvert project. This 
funding was needed to supplement the Town’s local appropriation of funds, due to the estimated construction costs 
for the project. 
 The Town Manager worked with town counsel and Until to finalize the Purchase & Sale and License Agreement 
in order to purchase the streetlights from Until. This was a necessary step as well before going out to bid to con-
vert the streetlights to LED.

In August of 2022 the Town Manager notified the Board that the Town was awarded the $187,140 for 
the competitive Green Communities Grant she submitted, which included $172,140 for the conver-
sion of the streetlights to LED. 
 As previously mentioned, Fiscal Year 2023 proved to be another difficult year for retaining staff and fill-
ing vacancies. The pandemic created opportunities for employees to move to new careers and made it 
more difficult to attract and retain employees. In addition to this, the Town identified that wages needed 
a market adjustment to attract candidates that ultimately would need to be addressed through the Salary 
Administration Plan Bylaw for non-union employees and collective bargaining agreements for union po-
sitions. The Assistant Town Manager/HR Director, who is responsible for advertising vacancies and 
working with the relevant departments to bring on new employees was constantly reviewing applications, 
interviewing, and trying to fill vacancies throughout the year. The following are a list of positions that 
were filled, either due to vacancies or creation of a new position, during FY 2023: Police Officers (3), 
Reserve Police Officer, Facilities Director (2), Conservation Administrator, Public Access Videographer, 
Public Safety Desk Officer, Civilian Traffic Members, IT Administrative Assistant (new position), Rec-
reation Director, Tax Collector Principal Clerk (2), Alternate Electrical Inspector, Heavy Equipment Op-
erators (4), Public Access Production Technician (new position), Building Commissioner, Planning 
Board Administrative Assistant, Seasonal Beach Staff, part -time Police Administrative Assistant, Con-
servation Administrative Assistant (2), and Meal Site Manager.  
 The Town Manager, the Assistant Town Manager, and the relevant staff worked together on putting a 
variety of projects out to bid in FY 2023, including the DPW Road Materials Bid, the Hollis Road Win-
dow Replacement, Master Planning Consulting Services, Wetland & Professional Land Surveyor Ser-
vices for Marshall Park, 30 School Street Hazardous Materials Abatement and Demolition, Cell Tower 
Lease for Town Hall, the Page Street Culvert Construction, Auditing and Consulting Services for the 
LED Streetlights project, the Removal of Solid Waste Materials at the Woodruff Property (no bids re-
ceived), Town Hall Building Envelope Repairs (all bids rejected), Tax Assessment Services (to handle all 
the inspections related to tax abatement applications), Real Property Data Collection Services, Five Year 
Assessing Revaluation Services, Roadway Maintenance for Fog Sealing, Public Safety Carport Project 
(rejected all bids), Guardrail Repairs, and the Senior Center ADA Outdoor Patio.  
 The Town Manager had a Fraud Risk Assessment performed that was partially paid for with a MIIA Flex 
Grant and $7,800 for a Recycling Dividends Grant that helped fund the Trash & Recycling Coordinator 
position. 
 Over the course of the fiscal year the Board discussed evaluation criteria and development of a process to 
review and commit the $3.5M in ARPA (American Rescue Plan Act) funding through the Coronavirus 
State and Local Fiscal Recovery Funds program.

By the end of the fiscal year the Board had committed funds for the Public Safety Building Security 
Camera System ($81,054), funds for tax assessment services and related investigation ($150,000), funds 
to purchase a new ambulance ($506,000), two large DPW trucks ($480,000), funding for the Marshall 
Park project ($1,000,000), funding for consulting services for ARPA reporting ($60,000), funding for an 
LPS Athletic Field Study ($50,000), and supplemental funding for the five year revaluation ($30,000). 
 The Special Town Meeting in November 2022 included a number of articles such as amending the FY23 
budget, payment of prior year bills, funding for the first year of the collective bargaining agreement with 
the Police Union, amending the Sewer Enterprise budget, transfer available funds to the Sewer Capital 
Reserve Stabilization Fund, funding for engineering for Marshall Park, amending the Salary Administra-
tion Plan Bylaw to add certain positions, acceptance of MGL Ch. 32 sec. 4(2)(b), an article from the 
Planning Board, an article from the APDC to amend the bylaw, an article to override the denial of the 
certificate to alter the Ritter Building by the APDC, an article to amend the bylaw on capital appropria-
tions, and two citizen petition articles. 
 In December, the Board approved all the annual liquor licenses, common victualler, weekday entertain-
ment, automatic amusement, jukebox, and motor vehicle licenses.  Throughout the year, the Board re-
views Earth Removal permits, liquor license transfers, flammable storage licenses, pole petitions, toll-
booth requests, and First Right of Refusal Requests for Chapter 61 properties.

was to address staffing needs conservatively due to the projected revenues and non-discretionary costs. 
This would mean pausing the 10-year staffing plan for the Police Department due to the impact that year 
2 of the Police Union Collective Bargaining Agreement would have on the budget. The 5-year Fire/EMS 
plan to get to a full time Paramedic Level Service was realized in FY23, so there were no plans to add 
staff to the Fire Department in FY24. Other priorities included additional IT equipment, cemetery im-
provements, constructing a small kennel at the Public Safety Building, hazardous tree removal, addition-
al funding for ADA public rights of way improvements, drainage improvements, and additional funding 
for library programming.  At that point in time health insurance costs were estimated to be an 8.5% in-
crease, the retirement assessment would be increasing by 13%, and other insurances would be increasing 
by 5%. 
 In January the Town Manager presented the Preliminary Capital Plan for FY24 for the funding of 6 pro-
jects totaling $1,360,500 and a 10 year look ahead at projected capital needs. The capital plan was modi-
fied to include an additional 6 projects prior to town meeting, as the Select Board decided to fund three of 
the projects using ARPA funds. The final plan included 12 projects for a grand total of $2,025,010 
(including $986,000 from ARPA funding).

     At the January 10th meeting, a large number of residents around Lake Shirley, Hickory Hills, and Lake 
Whalom attended the Select Board meeting to speak during public comment about their 3rd and 4th quarter 
tax bills increasing significantly. Many residents voiced that their property valuations increased more than 
50%. On January 17th the Select Board held a special meeting at the Middle/High School Auditorium and 
hundreds attended. The meeting was scheduled to allow the public to ask questions to the Board, Principal 
Assessor, and Board of Assessors. Ultimately the Select Board voted to conduct an investigation into the 
effects that the pandemic had on property values in 2021, the corresponding impact on the FY 2023 tax 
assessments, and the process by which the Town conducted property valuations and distributed tax assess-
ments to residents. The Chairman of the Board led the investigation, with the assistance of the Vice-
Chairman, and in coordination and collaboration with the Town Manager. The following six months of

BOARD OF ASSESSORS 
The Mission Statement of the Board of Assessors is to accurately determine the value of all Real 
and Personal Property within the Town of Lunenburg, as outlined in Massachusetts General Law 
Chapter 59.  The assessed value in every community in the Commonwealth must reflect fair market 
value as of January 1 prior to the start of any given fiscal year.   
The Assessors are also responsible for the administration of all property tax data records.  It 
maintains accurate parcel ownership data based on recorded property transactions at the Worcester 
North Registry of Deeds.  The Assessors process and administer auto excise as well as personal real 
estate tax exemptions for the Senior and Surviving Spouse, Blind, and Disabled Veterans.  
The Board of Assessors is elected and consists of three members. Each Assessor must complete a 
required DOR Training (Course 101, Assessment Administration: Law, Procedures, and Valuation for 
assessing board members).  In the Town of Lunenburg, the Assessor’s office staff consists of a full 
time Principal Assessor and a full time Assessors Clerk.  
 
The last 18 months have been tumultuous for the Board of Assessors.  
There has been a 100% turnover of the Board of Assessors’ membership.  1 new Member was 
elected in May 2023, while 2 new Members were appointed to fill vacant slots until May 2024.   
The Principal Assessor resigned and was replaced with an Interim Lead Assessor. After a lengthy 
search process, a full time Lead Assessor has been identified and will start late March 2024.  
The Town has issued a historic number of Property Tax abatements in FY 2023.  We handled a 30 
x increase in Abatement applications (440 vs 14).  The overwhelming majority of the abatements 
were settled in a timely fashion.   
FY 2024 is a state-mandated 5-Year Revaluation for the every property in Lunenburg.  The Prop-
erty Tax abatement effort led to an unprecedented delay in completing that revaluation and de-
termining the tax rate for FY 2024.  This resulted in a delay in distribution of the final FY 2024 tax 
bills. Because of the unusual and significant work load, two outside agencies, Vision Government 
Solutions, Inc. and Municipal Financial Services (MFS), have been engaged to provide assessment 
personnel for the abatement process and DOR filings for the 5-year Revaluation.   
The Assessor Operations continue be resource-constrained.  Currently, we only have 1 Interim 
Lead Assessor, and are temporarily short the Assessor Clerk.  In the MFS report on the FY2023 
Abatement process, it was recommended that Lunenburg add a part time person to help per-
form the normal Assessor Office work load.  This included site inspections, office coverage, data 
analytics and records maintenance.  Currently, the resource constraint is being partially ad-
dressed by the external contractors previously mentioned.  
 
The Board of Assessors is committed to restoring the trust of Lunenburg tax-payers.  We hope to 
accomplish this through improving transparency, better out-reach, and enhancing visibility and ac-
cess.  
We have begun this by scheduling all meetings during evenings (with both PACC coverage and 
Zoom access), so everyone can participate more easily.  We created a Data Collection Manual for use 
during the 5-year Revaluation.  We are also putting the finishing touches on a guide for “How to Read 
Your Vision Property Card”, that will be available in hard-copy and via the internet.

The Town of Lunenburg Building Department is Located in the Ritter Memorial Building located at 960 Massachu-
setts Avenue. The Building Department works to  efficiently, effectively, and consistently ensure a safe, sustainable, 
and vibrant community through effective oversight of construction and development activities by enforcing the Mas-
sachusetts state Building, Plumbing, Gas, and Electrical Codes, The Architectural Access Board Regulations, and 
Lunenburg’s Zoning Bylaw and Town Code. The Building Department also plays a critical role in assisting business 
through the process of obtaining permits and ensuring compliance with all relevant codes and regulations. A commit-

ner.

jects while maintaining thorough and consistent review procedures. 
 Utilizing technology to streamline internal processes, enhance data management, and provide better online ser-
vices for applicants and property owners. 
 Regularly reviewing and assessing departmental processes and policies, seeking opportunities for improvement,

 Coordinating with other departments to ensure a streamlined approach to permitting and enforcement as needed. 
 Receiving and evaluating feedback from the public and other interested parties, and implementing changes as 
needed to ensure the department goals are being met.

CONSERV ATION COMMISSION 
The Town of Lunenburg Conservation Commission is a seven-member commission, each appointed to 3-year terms by the 
Select Board, whose duties are codified under the Conservation Commission Act MGL chapter 40 s 8C. The Commission 
enforces the Massachusetts Wetland Protection Act MGL chapter 131 s 40 and Chapter 239 of the local bylaws for the pro-
tection of wetlands.  The Commission also manages, and controls town owned lands retained for conservation and passive 
recreation use. 
During 2023 the commission had a very busy and active agenda of public hearings for a wide range of projects from com-
mercial development, subdivisions and small single family upkeep projects and larger scale industrial/commercial pro-
posals. 
The Commission also held many inspections with the Commission Administrator on weekends prior to meetings to famil-
iarize themselves with the upcoming proposals on the agenda. The Commission continues to work with the town on famil-
iarizing itself with the continually changing storm water mandates from the EPA and working towards compliance both by 
procedure and with any necessary codification as well as aiding with the community implementations the “MS4” regula-
tions. 
The Conservation Commission continues to implement forest stewardship plans developed using grants as outlined in the 
last three annual reports. The upcoming management effort will be concentrated on the Rayno property on Northfield 
Road. The Hollis Road property stewardship plan is oriented toward enhancing bird wildlife habitat with the Mass Audu-
bon Society as part of the DCR "Forestry for the Birds" program.  The funding from the sale of harvested timber provides 
the seed money to make it possible for the Commission to apply for grants to maintain and improve Lunenburg’s conserva-
tion lands. In 2023, the Conservation Commission was the beneficiary of the Shawn H. Kelley Estate which will further 
support ongoing maintenance and improvements.  
The Pollinator Habitat portion of the Hollis Rd property provides for a diversity of native flowers throughout the growing 
season. The Habitat promotes insect egg laying and nesting sites for pollinators. The Habitat provides food sources for 
birds and numerous insects including native bees and butterflies. This unique “Hidden Gem” helps control soil erosion and 
oxygenates our air.   
The Commission has made significant progress on “You Are Here” trail map signs at trail heads and trail intersections to 
expand the safe use of Lunenburg’s existing trail network. In April of 2023, Conservation partnered with Parks to coordi-
nate a month of Earth Day events across Lunenburg. The joint project improved 13 properties and brought 361 man-hours 
of improvements to Lunenburg. In June of 2023, the Conservation Commission was awarded a MassTrails grant for fund-
ing planning of parking areas in Table Rock and Rayno Conservation Area, and improved access to the Harold Harley 
Wildlife Viewing Platform in the Cowdrey Conservation Area. The Lunenburg Snow Riders continue to support the 
maintenance and improvement of the trail system on Lunenburg’s conservation lands.  As part of their Recreational Trails 
grant award from 2022, the Snow Riders recently completed the Lane Conservation Area trail improvement project with 
aid from Baer Construction, Dan Deleo Plumbing, Gatsby Grounds, M. Proctor Masonry, and the assistance of 17 volun-
teers, including numerous volunteer tractors. The Commission would like to express our gratitude for all the hard-working 
volunteers that work so enthusiastically in maintaining and improving Lunenburg’s conservation lands!  
The Commission has also continued to work closely with Lake Shirley and Hickory Hills on their lake management plans.  
These plans set goals and action items designed to give a better picture of the overall health of the lakes and define various 
strategies to ensure continued protection of these valuable resources. 
During the year 2023, there were changes of personnel along creating staffing shortages. In July, Conservation Administra-
tor Anna Petrie resigned. Approximately May, Conservation Administrative Assistant, Rebecca Manley resigned. In July, 
Ashleigh Denio was hired to backfill Rebecca. In October, the Land Manager, Adam Burney resigned. Adam was the front

residents and visitors of all ages with priority given to in-person, live events.  
The Lunenburg Cultural Council, with grants from the Massachusetts Cultural Council, funded twenty-two art, cultural, 
music, and science programs for the benefit of Lunenburg residents and members of the surrounding community in 
2023. 
Some of the programs funded include: Clear Path for Veterans New England, Discovery Museum, Fitchburg Art Muse-

Breathe Life series, on one wall of the Boston Medical Center.

The Lunenburg Cultural Council is looking forward to providing more programming, and increasing community involve-

The Department maintains roads, bridges, sewer infrastructure, and provides services essential to the welfare and quality 
of life for the citizens of Lunenburg.  The staff consists of one Director, one Executive Assistant (shared with the Facili-

Departmental Mission: 
The mission of the Department of Public Works is to provide high quality, cost effective, efficient, and safe services to 
protect, preserve and improve the Town’s roadway infrastructure and related assets in a manner which meets and en-
hances the current and future social and economic needs of the community; to contribute to a healthy, safe and quality 
environment for the Town’s citizens and visitors; and to provide environmentally sound means of solid waste and 
wastewater disposal.    
Departmental Goals and Objectives 
 Be prepared to respond to any weather, roadway infrastructure, or accident-related emergency that may arise on a 
twenty-four hour per day, three hundred and sixty-five day per year basis. 
 Maintain and enhance a highly qualified and dedicated staff of public works professionals. 
 Provide preventative maintenance in a proactive manner to reduce reactive maintenance as follows: 
 Maintain and update the sewer system and associated pumping stations. 
 Maintain and repair roadways in accordance with the 2022 pavement management plan. 
 Maintain and repair sidewalks in accordance with the 2020 PROW ADA Transition plan. 
 Increase the number of roadsides mowed by developing a roadside maintenance plan. 
 Increase the number of catch basin and stormwater structures cleaned and the number of miles of roadways 
swept per the June 2020 Operations and Maintenance Plan prepared by Comprehensive Environmental, Inc. 
 Establish a comprehensive evaluation of bridges and culverts, and prioritize by condition, use, and plans for 
rehabilitation. 
 Increase training offered by our insurance provider to obtain credits to lessen premiums.   
 Identify new sources (Federal, State, and other grants) of funding to supplement the operational budget. 
 Continue to maintain a high level of courteous communication and productive interaction between members of 
boards and commissions as well as Town residents. 
 Strive to modernize the operations of the Department by obtaining and using the most up to date equipment and 
methods, including asset management.

Fog seal is a type of pavement preservation treatment applied to an existing asphalt pavement surface to preserve its 
functional and structural integrity and delay a more costly rehabilitation treatment in the future.  Fog seal penetrates up 
to 3/8 inch into asphalt pavement surfaces, softening the asphalt binder as it goes. This improves cohesion and slows 
cracking, reducing the loss of fines and aggregates.  It is a simple, low-cost spray application which extends road life by 
up to 3 years.   
 
Storm Water 
Staying in compliance with our EPA NPDES MS4 Permit continues to remain an important function of the DPW.  Along 
with the Storm Water Task Force, Land Use Director, and our Storm Water Consultant the DPW works to meet the obli-
gations of the permit and required reporting each year.  The DPW provides maintenance and care of the Town’s storm 
water management systems.  This includes, but is not limited to catch basin cleaning, street sweeping, pipe jetting, re-
placing pipes, and swale cleaning.  The Department responds to calls for clogged catch basins and culverts to help con-
trol ponding and flooding.  Storm water related issues continue to rise. 
Tree Removal   
A Tree Risk Management Inventory of almost 1,200 trees on the roads in Lunenburg was completed in 2015.  The inven-
tory prioritizes the removal and/or pruning of the trees.  However, storm damage and natural attrition creates a backlog 
of tree work that exceeds the Department’s allotted budget for tree removal.  The Department strives to fulfill each resi-
dent’s request for tree work but must prioritize work based upon the determined degree of danger to person and property 
and location.  The DPW continued to act in a reactionary manner as needs arose.  All tree work other than brush remov-
al, chipping, and the removal of small diameter trees will continue to be outsourced due to the lack of a bucket truck or 
crane and a licensed tree professional.  Each spring and after storms, the DPW staff remove and dispose of woody debris 
within the Town’s right-of-way.  An aggressive program by Unitil in recent years has lessened the burden of the Town 
by removing those trees that may cause an interruption of electrical service to the community.  The DPW appreciates and 
thanks Unitil for their responsiveness in removing hazardous trees when asked to do so. 
Roadside Vegetation Maintenance 
The DPW staff responded to many requests to cut and clear overgrown vegetation from roadsides.  This work was done

2022 and arrived in December of 2023.   The DPW is developing a town-wide program of selective curbside mowing 
and vegetation clearing using the new tractor and boom mower that will significantly enhance our roadside vegetation 
maintenance program.

FINANCE COMMITTEE REPORT
 
Fiscal Year 2023
 
Annual Town Meeting is an important event for community members to come together and vote on warrant articles that 
directly impact how we manage our town. Amongst these articles is the FY23 budget, capital plan, authorizations for 
various enterprise and revolving funds, and appropriations relating to stabilization funds and reserves. Throughout the 
year the Town Manager and department heads carefully review expenses and have thoughtful conversations about how 
to best serve and manage the town. While developing the budget they considered funding, contracts, and mandates, as 
well as what the community wants and needs to thrive. The Town Manager formulated a recommended budget, which 
she presented to the Finance Committee on February 17th. Over six subsequent Finance Committee meetings, depart-
ment heads presented overviews and plans relating to their budgets and capital requests. A Public Hearing was held on 
March 31st, as required by the Town Charter, and the Finance Committee voted on recommendations pertaining to fi-
nancially relevant articles. 
 
Armed with all the information gathered during this process, the Finance Committee recommends approval of the Town 
Manager’s omnibus budget, as outlined in this warrant. 
Overall, our financial condition is good. Revenues are stable, aided by strong collections, increases in state aid, new 
growth, and a variety of federal and state grants that the town has secured. On the expense side, while not meeting every 
need outlined by the department heads, this budget does begin to address several emerging and previously unmet needs. 
The total in requested expenditures is $49,953,531.76. While we are pleased with our current fiscal standing, we do face 
challenges ahead; especially with regard to a number of our municipal buildings, which we outline in more detail below. 
Notably, our certified free cash this year is $2,666,962, which is substantially higher than in previous years, a temporary 
benefit resulting from appropriations that were not fully expended in FY21 as a result of COVID-related restrictions and 
impacts. Town fiscal policy directs portions of free cash to accounts such as stabilization, the OPEB allocation (a fund to 
pay for future retiree benefits), and the capital plan. 
Accordingly, this year’s recommended capital plan (funded by free cash, tax revenues, and unspent capital appropria-
tions from previous years) is $2,162,849. It is an unprecedented investment of available funds (without incurring any 
additional debt) into the capital infrastructure that supports our town. As of the printing of this warrant, the Finance 
Committee is seeking to answer a few specific questions pertaining to the capital plan. We will have a recommendation 
on the capital plan, Article 6, at Town Meeting on May 7. 
Other notable items: 
 For FY23, the proposed appropriation for education is $23,397,371, 56.2%% of the Town’s total budget, and a 
3.78% increase over last year. This includes our Monty Tech assessment at $1,054,376, which is a 20.4% increase 
for FY23, mainly due to an increase in enrollment from 83 to 94 students. Additionally, the town budget includes 
$1,207,421 in School Retiree Health Insurance. Fortunately, this year the town benefited from an $898,610 increase 
in Chapter 70 aid, which more than offsets the $673,617 increase in the Lunenburg School District budget. 
While our Chapter 70 increase was welcome news, the increase was partly a result of formula changes that recognized 
more low-income students at a higher tier of funding. Our vulnerable populations are of top concern for all of our depart-
ment leaders. 
An area of concern for the Finance Committee are several personnel positions that the School District is funding through 
ESSER 2 and ESSER 3 federal grants. These grants, totaling $1,939,868 will expire in FY24. Many of these positions 
are addressing acute health, academic, and social/emotional needs that have arisen from COVID-19. Over the next two 
years the School Committee will need to determine what positions we need to maintain, and how to fund them., 
 This year’s budget supports an increase of $53,443 for the library budget to support the addition of two part-time 
personnel and additional expenses. This increase will enable the library to extend its operating hours to include open-

SEWER COMMISSION – FY23 
The Sewer Commission bears the responsibility of overseeing the Sewer Enterprise Fund, planning for the future, and 
developing and implementing rules, regulations, and policies. Under the Town’s Charter, responsibility for the day-to-
day operation, care and maintenance of the sanitary sewers are under the supervision of the Town Manager who dele-
gates the responsibility to manage and maintain the operational side of the system to the Department of Public Works’ 
Director. The Town of Lunenburg does not operate a sewer treatment plant. Approximately 87% of Lunenburg sewer is 
discharged to the City of Leominster, and the other 13% is discharged to the City of Fitchburg. 
 
In FY23, the following members served on the Sewer Commission: John Reynolds-Chair, Brett Ramsden-Vice Chair, 
Mike Deveikis-Clerk and Members Dave MacDonald and Mike Nault. The Commission continued to meet virtually via 
Zoom for all meetings.   
 
There are currently 1212 properties connected to the Lunenburg system and another 284 which have access to Town 
Sewer but are not yet connected. During FY23, 6 new accounts connected to Town Sewer. The Commission billed out 
$1,058,041 in quarterly sewer bills and had $33,257 of prior year delinquencies added to the owners’ property tax bills 
for non-payment.   
 
In FY23, Sewer Business Manager Barb Lefebvre announced her retirement after 15 years of service in the Sewer De-
partment and 6 years in the Assessor’s office. The Commission thanks Barb for her dedicated work in running the office, 
budgeting, and giving guidance to the Commissioners throughout the years. Assistant manager, Paula Bertram, took over 
as Sewer Business Manager in March 2023.  
 
During FY23, CTE (Chad Empey) continued to provide excellent service for the Operation & Maintenance of the Sewer 
Stations and completed the 3rd year of their contract.  The Commission approved funds for upgrades to the pump sta-
tions, to replace cranes, block heaters, dialers, seals, and replacement pumps. These upgrades are important to keep the 
stations running smoothly.  A Request for Proposals also went out for a new O&M contract for the pump stations. 
 
DPW Director, Bill Bernard and Paula Bertram, Business Manager, worked with the Commission to carry out the 
Maintenance Plan for the pump stations, setting up jetting for 20% of the lines each year and overseeing the operation 
and maintenance of the pump stations.  The Commission worked on a proposal for possible ARPA funds of $300K for 
upgrades to the pump stations and $200K for repairs to 84 manholes.  Also, a similar proposal was sent for state earmark 
funds.  
A proposed 40B project on Howard Street, seeking expansion of sewer outside of the Sewer Service Area, was discussed 
multiple times during FY23. An extensive list of concerns was sent to the ZBA, stating the estimated costs and impacts 
to the system if the project proceeds. The Commission also met several times with the developer, Kevin O’Brien, and 
maintained that the expansion of the Sewer Service Area requires a vote by residents at a Town Meeting.  
 
The Commission continued the annual permit program for the 16 Food Service Establishments with grease traps and in-
terceptors on Town Sewer.  FY23 Annual Permit renewals will have no fee for establishments in compliance. Those who 
are not in compliance will pay $350 for internal traps and $650 for external trap renewals.  
 
The “Rules & Regulations for Oil & Grease Traps/Interceptors for Food Establishments” continues to be a focus of the 
Commission as Fats, Oils and Grease (FOG) can negatively affect the smooth operation and maintenance costs of the 
sewer system.  While the Grease Trap Regulation focuses on commercial and public disposal, individual homeowners 
can also contribute a significant amount of grease into the system which increases everyone’s costs. Whether on sewer or 
septic, grease should not be put down the drain.

Veterans Services Office Annual Report 2023 The Commonwealth of Massachusetts has the most comprehen-
sive system of assistance offered to Veterans, their families and surviving spouses in all of the United States. 
Every city or town in Massachusetts, in accordance with chapter 115 of the Massachusetts General Laws, must 
have available to its citizens a Veterans’ Services Officer (VSO), either full- or part-time, as determined by the 
community population, in order to serve the needs of its Veterans. Lunenburg has a part-time VSO at the Ritter 
Building at 960 Massachusetts Ave. The Office hours are Thursdays from 8:00 AM thru 1:00 PM.  
 
Veterans, families, or widows who qualify can be provided monthly financial assistance (Chapter 115 Bene-
fits), which are reimbursements for specific living expenses such as heating fuel, medical care, and pharma-
ceutical co-payments. With the VSO’s approval, the Town of Lunenburg provides the financial assistance, but 
then the town is reimbursed by the state’s Department of Veterans’ Services (DVS). It is the function of the 
Veterans’ Services Office to identify qualifying members of the community and assist with enrollment into the 
DVS program. Additionally, certain Veterans and/or widows may also be eligible for federal benefits from the 
Veterans Administration (VA). Only Massachusetts offers both of these services to every community.  
 
During Fiscal Year 2023 (July 2022- June 2023), Lunenburg is serving and average of 10 recipients (Veterans, 
Families, or Widows) per month on Chapter 115. Lunenburg paid out $71,041.50 in benefits, or an average of 
$5,920.13 per recipient per month. Lunenburg was reimbursed $53,281.16 by DVS, which is typically 75% of 
what was given as assistance, the maximum allowable share. There are a couple of services that the Town is 
reimbursed 100%  
 
Some examples of Veterans’ Service Office assistance services include:  
 Enrollment in chapter 115, State Veterans benefits  
 Assistance obtaining military service records or replacement medals/decorations  
 Tuition waivers at Massachusetts state colleges  
 Housing assistance, including Veterans Shelters and Veterans Homes  
 Payments of “Welcome Home” bonuses for deployed Veterans  
 Assistance obtaining certain Massachusetts automobile and motorcycle license plates  
 Employment assistance

The Firefighters and Emergency Medical Technicians of the Lunenburg Fire Department are dedicated to providing the highest qu ality, 
most cost effective fire and emergency medical services to the citizens of the town of Lunenburg.  
 
In 2023, your Fire Department responded to 2033 calls for service, broken down as follows:  
Fires, Alarms & Hazardous Conditions       356              Emergency Medical      1193 
Inspections                 366                Other                             18 
 
This represents a 7% increase in emergency calls for service over 2023, mostly in Emergency Medical calls.  
The Department consists of 10 Career and 30 -35 On Call/Per Diem Firefighters and Emergency Medical Technicians, plus a 
full time Administrative Assistant/EMS Coordinator.  Three Firefighter/EMT or Paramedics are on duty 24/7 at the Station.  
This coverage is provided by a combination of our Career Staff and Per Diem Firefighters.  Staffing the Station with dual 
trained Fire/EMS personnel ensures that we are ready to respond to whatever emergency occurs.  As many calls require 
more personnel than are on duty, we continue to rely heavily on the dedication and commitment of our On Call Firefighters 
and Emergency Medical Technicians. 
After five years of preparation, the Department Ambulances began operating at the Paramedic Level on January 1, 
2023. This allows us to provide advanced lifesaving care at the local level, and no longer rely on other agencies 
for assistance. The Department now has six Full Time and seven On Call/Per diem Firefighter/Paramedics. Para-
medic training is more than two thousand hours and requires anywhere from 9 -18 months of college level class-
room education, clinical time, and field internships to obtain certification. The Department continues to train our 
newly hired full-time staff to this level with Firefighter Jennie Smith completing her training in September. Achiev-
ing Paramedic Level service, along with 24/7 coverage in the Station are the most significant advancements to the 
Department in the last 50 years, 
Lieutenant Christos Lekaditis and Firefighter Carl Gamberdella make up the Department’s Fire Prevention Divi-
sion and in 2023 conducted 366 Inspections. This is in addition to multiple fire drills and public education initia-
tives. By law, we are required to inspect Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Detector installations when a home is sold. 
We are also required to inspect all new oil burners and propane gas installations. Any business holding a liquor 
license also requires a yearly inspection for fire safety issues. In addition, all new construction projects must 
have their plans reviewed for proper fire protection, as well as ongoing inspections during the construction pro-
cess. Other Fire Safety Initiatives undertaken during 2023 include:

Fiscal Year 2024 Capital Plan
 
The Capital Planning Committee met several times during the fall and early winter months to consider capital 
requests that were submitted by department heads.  Following extensive dialogue with department heads and 
revisions to some requests, the committee considered 28 requests and ultimately voted to rank 27 items for 
consideration by the Town Manager, Select Board, and Finance Committee. 
On January 28th, the Town Manager presented her preliminary Capital Plan. Her recommendation included the 
first six items recommended by the Capital Planning Committee, totaling $1,360,500. 
On February 13th, the Capital Planning Committee met to consider the Town Manager’s preliminary capital 
plan and some differences in her ranking of projects. No action was taken, however at the time there were two 
outstanding issues to be addressed before a final capital plan recommendation could be submitted for the war-
rant. 
The first was a request for American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) funds to purchase three vehicles (an ambulance 
and two 6-wheel dump/spreader/plow trucks). On February 21st, the Select Board committed $986,000 of 
ARPA funds for these projects. This action accelerated ordering the vehicles by several months (demand for 
these items and supply chain issues have resulted in extended delays) and eliminated a need to tap the Special 
Purpose Stabilization Account to fund these items. 
The second issue was the availability of free cash, which the Town Manager had reserved to fund the overlay 
account, relating to the high number of property tax abatement requests. On March 28th, the Town Manager 
presented a revised recommendation that eliminated the $986,000 from the Special Purpose Stabilization Ac-
count, added $467,268.98 from free cash, added $356,789 from tax revenues, and reduced the amount from 
unexpended capital by $2,758.98 to her recommendation for the FY24 Capital Plan to fund six additional pro-
jects. The Town Manager’s new recommendation also removed two items from the priority list (TCP Ansul 
Inspection and Public Safety Building Downspouts) after consultation and further assessment by the Facilities 
Director. The following outlines the Town Manager’s final Capital Plan Recommendation with associated 
Capital Planning Committee rankings and scoring derived from the UMass Collins Center for Public Manage-
ment. There are 12 items, totaling $2,025,010.00. As referenced above, $986,000 of that total does not require 
an Annual Town Meeting appropriation or approval.

The Master Plan Steering Committee (MPSC) advises on the development of the Master Plan 
document. The committee also fosters openness and inclusion in the Master Plan process

the community’s priorities and goals. A comprehensive plan takes a long range view of the 
Town’s land use, housing, open spaces, natural and cultural resources, public facilities, eco-
nomic development, and transportation—and tries to shape the direction of the Town for the 
next five to fifteen years. The Town completed its last comprehensive plan in 2002, more than 
twenty years ago. The Lunenburg Planning Board initiated this new comprehensive plan pro-
cess, Plan Lunenburg, to explore and define the Town’s shared vision and create an updated 
framework for future Town goals and policies. In 2023, PlanLunenburg.org was developed to 
be the host site of Lunenburg’s Master Plan process. Two Public Meetings were conducted as 
well as surveys in between. The MPSC worked closely with OverUnder to coordinate venues, 
distribute surveys, and encourage community involvement. The MPSC, OverUnder, RKG, 
Innes Associates, along with the energetic involvement of residents of Lunenburg, have suc-
cessfully kept the process on track with each phase originally outlined: Phase 1 in May 2023 
focusing on discovery, Phase 2 in February 2024 focusing on testing and recommending, and 
Phase 3 in August 2024 leading to a report. All throughout each phase, the MPSC actively 
seeks the engagement of the residents of Lunenburg so that their voices can be heard in the 
Master Plan process.

Town Report 2023 
 
Teaching and learning is the crux of our school system.  We have been working diligently as a district to pro-
vide high-quality materials and instruction to students, provide targeted interventions and challenges to our 
learners to support the learning process. 
Using high-quality curriculum materials which are standards-aligned, inclusive, rigorous, and engaging is a 
priority for the state of Massachusetts and for our district.  We are in our second year of implementation for 
our new English language arts and math curriculums, Into Reading and Into Math, at grades K-5.  Teachers 
have been making adjustments and refinements to practice, having taught all of the modules at least once. We 
are also in our first year of implementation for Amplify, our new middle school language arts curriculum, and 
we are in the process of evaluating and selecting a high-quality math program for our middle school as well. 
Structured common planning time for teachers in grade level continues for grades K-8.  Our district literacy 
coaches meet with teachers during common planning time to support curriculum development and help foster 
student growth.  High school department heads structure department meeting time to facilitate the necessary 
curriculum work needed to move each department forward and improve learning for students.  
===PAGE 123===
123 
 
The Lunenburg Public School District is participating in the Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS) Liter-
acy Academy, the Inclusive Practices Academy (based on the Universal Design for Learning), and the Social 
Emotional and Behavioral Academy (SEB) through the Department of Elementary and Secondary Education 
(DESE).  All three academies provide coaching sessions and technical assistance in and assist our district 
with creating action plans. 
Lunenburg Public Schools uses multiple assessments to evaluate student achievement and growth and to be 
able to provide supports and targeted interventions. Students in grades K-8 completed the fall and winter 
iReady diagnostic assessment in both reading and math, and teachers are participating in data cycles to ana-
lyze data, plan for targeted support, and adjust instruction. Personalized learning platforms through iReady 
provide differentiated instruction, systematic practice, and scaffolded feedback in both reading and math. 
We are also using DIBELS (Dynamic Indicators of Basic Literacy Skills)  along with i-Ready to determine 
how students are performing on the critical skills necessary for reading: phonemic awareness, phonics, fluen-
cy, vocabulary, and comprehension. In math, we use the Forefront Universal Screener for Number Sense.   
Lunenburg Primary and Turkey Hill have implemented a multi-tiered support system (MTSS) that provides 
each school with a lead interventionist and two tutors to support students who are struggling to meet grade-
level expectations in reading and math. In order to meet the needs of all students, a team of educators work 
with our literacy and math specialist to analyze multiple data points so that targeted interventions for support 
and/or enrichment can be provided. Student progress is carefully monitored and adjustments are made as 
necessary. 
For professional development, we have been working with Dr. Kalise Wornum, owner of KW diversity.  Dr.

and safety for our LGBTQ students.  Students in our Gender and Sexuality Alliance (GSA) club at LHS 
worked with district staff during our November PD day to present and answer questions, and we recently had 
Landon Callahan from DESE’s Safe Schools for LGBTQ Students present to our faculty from grades 6-12. 
We have continued with our After School Program at Turkey Hill Elementary School, Lunenburg Middle 
School, and Lunenburg High School.  The program provides targeted support in literacy and math in addition 
to academic support for students’ current classes and with executive functioning skills.   
We continue to review and pursue grant opportunities which are presented by the Department of Elementary 
and Secondary Education and have received the Student Opportunity Act - Evidence-Based Practice Grant,

tions and provide them with the tools they need to create and maintain positive social interaction with their

Lunenburg Middle School 
Town Report 2023 
Lunenburg Middle School provides a rigorous academic curriculum as well as strong programs that support 
students’ social and emotional growth. These programs ensure that each child becomes a self-motivated, crea-
tive, lifelong learner in a safe, stimulating environment that encourages diversity and open honest communica-
tion.   The dedicated and accomplished staff of professionals within the school embraces the philosophy of ed-
ucating the whole child academically, socially, and emotionally. This philosophy is a means by which all stu-
dents become positive community members that possess the skills to be successful in high school and beyond. 
 Lunenburg Middle School is a co-located middle - high school serving grades six through eight. Our effective, 
student centered middle school embraces interdisciplinary teams of teachers that enable teachers to work with 
smaller groups of students. Smaller groups of students allow teachers to learn and engage students in their pre-
ferred learning style, making them more confident and successful. 
Our effective middle school model is student centered and provides opportunities to explore a variety of differ-
ent interests and talents through special areas and enrichment classes and extracurricular activities. One of our 
goals is for students to discover something they are passionate about and want to continue exploring as they 
move through high school and beyond. We will continue to seek out opportunities for students that foster criti-
cal thinking and problem solving skills that prepare them for high school and beyond or where their passion 
may lead them to. 
Our grade eight team of teachers continue to implement a schedule that mirrors the high school schedule. Our 
philosophy is to ease the difficult transition from middle school to the first year of high school by developing 
student’s independent skills they will require to be successful in high school. Grade eight teachers function as 
one inclusive team where students take classes with a variety of different teachers as they would in a high 
school setting. This differs from our two interdisciplinary team of teachers that have been a successful model 
in grades six and seven.     
LMS continues to utilize the i-Ready learning platform to provide an additional learning component in addi-
tion to classroom instruction.  i-Ready is being implemented during our intervention blocks and provides 
teachers with individualized learning paths based upon the students individual learning needs.  Parents will be 
provided fall, winter and spring benchmark assessment reports created by I ready so they too can monitor stu-
dent progress.          
Lunenburg Middle School has benefited from a top notch guidance support team.  Two guidance counselors, a 
school social worker coupled with our shared school psychologist has been able to provide a level of social 
emotional support to our students and their families that we have not been accustomed to. Data supports the 
need for these positions as during the pandemic, the number of students experiencing anxiety and depression 
has significantly increased nationwide and in our own community. Our guidance team has been able to meet 
the needs of our students while preparing them to be available for academic learning.

Lunenburg High School 
Town Report 2023 
Lunenburg High School and its faculty continue to focus on rigorous curriculum, high standards and opportu-
nities for students, both academic and extracurricular, so that every student feels supported and included in our 
community. LHS faculty strives to reflect on their teaching practices, to learn and to change in order to best 
serve all of our students.  
2023-2024 School year 
 Lunenburg High School returned this August with a renewed philosophy of support for our school communi-
ty. We have assembled an outstanding, talented faculty with a shared vison of supporting students academical-
ly, socially and emotionally as they tackle a rigorous and challenging curriculum.  With our renewed sense of 
support, students are able to acquire the skills necessary to earn a high school diploma as well as be successful 
in post-secondary education as well as in the work force.  
One pillar of support that has proven to be successful for students is the LHS Intervention Teacher. This teach-
ing position provides direct instruction to students who were identified by their teachers as to needing addi-
tional support to be successful in the classroom. Students report to the Interventionist during their study skills 
block and receive assistance to the specific class or classes they are struggling in. The data show the students 
participating in the intervention classroom show substantial improvement in the completion of classwork and 
in their overall grades.

We have reorganized our Algebra classes to ensure all students are successful. With the addition of a class-
room teacher we were able to reduce class sizes in Algebra II and allow teachers to provide more personalized, 
individualized instruction while working with smaller groups of students in a traditionally academically de-
manding course. This has been an effective strategy as you can see though our results in the MCAS table be-
low.  
 The addition of faculty and courses at Lunenburg High School have allowed students a more varied options 
for course selection, provided individualized and programmatic support and structure for students to be suc-
cessful.  
MCAS RESULTS 
Lunenburg High School - Spring 2023 MCAS Results (Grade 10) 
 
 
The results indicate the LHS percentage scores for meeting and exceeding expectations are high, higher than 
the state average and in mathematics, over 15 percentage points higher than the state average when compared 
to schools who serve students in similar grade levels. Investing in a sixth math teacher that reduced class sizes 
and provided students with additional individual classroom support was a successful strategy. The percentage 
of grade 10 students scoring in the meeting and exceeding expectations category in mathematics is the highest 
percentage in the district.  
We were also very pleased to learn that 30 students at Lunenburg High School received the state ’s John and 
Abigail Adams Scholarship this year.  The scholarship is available to students whose MCAS performance puts 
them at the top 25 percent of their district.  To be eligible, students must either score Advanced on one exam 
and Proficient on the other, or Advanced on both the English and Math exams.  That is approximately 30 % of 
grade 10 students receiving the award.

250-4.9 WATER SUPPLY PROTECTION DISTRICT 
1. PURPOSE OF DISTRICT 
The purpose of this Water Supply Protection District is to: 
 promote the health, safety, and general welfare of the community by ensuring an adequate quality and quantity of drinking water 
for the Town of Lunenburg. 
 preserve and protect existing and potential sources of drinking water; 
 conserve natural resources in the Town of Lunenburg; and 
 prevent temporary and permanent contamination of the environment.

SCOPE OF AUTHORITY 
The Water Supply Protection District is an overlay district superimposed on the zoning districts.  This overlay dis-
trict shall apply to all new construction, reconstruction, or expansion of existing buildings and new or expanded us-

waste or oil.

in serious irreversible, or incapacitating reversible illness or pose a substantial present or potential hazard to human

Impervious Surface: Material or structure on, above, or below the ground that does not allow precipitation or surface

DISTRICT BOUNDARY DISPUTES 
 
If the location of the Water Supply Protection District in relation to a particular parcel is in doubt, resolution of the 
boundary dispute shall be through a Special Permit application to the Special Permit Granting Authority. Any appli-
cation for a special permit for this purpose shall be accompanied by adequate documentation. 
Burden of proof shall be upon the land owner to demonstrate that the location of the Water Supply Protection Dis-
trict with respect to a particular parcel(s) of land is uncertain. At the request of the land owner, the Town may en-
gage a professional engineer, hydrologist, geologist, or soil scientist to determine more accurately the boundaries of 
the Water Supply Protection District with respect to a particular parcel(s) of land, and may charge the owner for the 
cost of the investigation. Changes to the Water Supply Protection District require town meeting approval. Changes 
to the Zone II boundary requires approval from the MassDEP. 
Where the Zone II boundary line of the Water Supply Protection District divides a lot or parcel, the requirements es-
tablished by this bylaw shall apply to the land 20 feet beyond the Zone II boundary.

(7) Common Driveways 
A common driveway is an access drive to be used for vehicular access to two or more primary dwellings or places of 
business. No person will develop or construct a driveway serving two or more lots or properties without first obtaining a 
Special Permit from the Planning Board in accordance with this Section 250-6.4C(7).  
The Planning Board may issue a Special Permit to allow a common driveway upon finding that the driveway will: 
Promote efficient traffic flow, 
Reduce traffic hazards from numerous individual driveways, 
In the judgment of the Planning Board, such an arrangement will be more advantageous to the neighborhood than indi-
vidual driveways, 
Consolidate access to lots across/near wetland resources, and  
Provide sufficient access to emergency vehicles and responders. 
 
(b) Special Permit Requirements 
The driveway will be located entirely within the lots being served. 
A common driveway will be considered satisfactory only if: 
It has been constructed in accordance with the provisions of the Special Permit 
There is a recorded access easement outlining the right of access and including clear provision for maintenance and snow 
removal; running with the land.  
A common driveway will not be used to provide lot frontage required by this Bylaw or by the Subdivision Control Law.  
Where the access to structures or uses provided by the driveway is substantially different from that which would be pro-
vided through the required lot frontages, the Special Permit will not be issued unless the Planning Board finds 
that the proposed shared driveway and its location are in the public interest.  
Each lot that shares a common driveway will meet the minimum dimensional requirements for the Zoning District in 
which it is located as outlined in this Bylaw. Specifically, each lot will have the required amount of frontage lo-
cated on the street which connects to the common driveway. 
The Planning Board will submit a copy of the Special Permit and approved plans to the Building Commissioner. No 
building permit will be issued until receipt of the Special Permit and plan.  
No occupancy permit shall be issued for any uses or structures utilizing the common driveway until evidence of record-
ing of the required utility, access and maintenance easement has been filed with the Building Commissioner and
