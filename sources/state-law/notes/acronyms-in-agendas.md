# Acronyms on an agenda: what the Attorney General's determinations say

Written by us, 8 October 2026. Not a determination. Only the Attorney General decides whether
the Open Meeting Law was violated, and nothing below says any Lunenburg body did.

**Short answer.** Yes, an acronym on an agenda can be an Open Meeting Law problem. The
Attorney General has found that an acronym the public does not widely understand made a
notice topic insufficiently specific: TRRP (OML 2015-197), B.E.S.S. (OML 2025-153), PMR
(OML 2026-99), and MASC and the policy codes BEDH/BEDH-E on a school committee notice
(OML 2023-83). It has also found acronyms acceptable when they are widely understood
(AFSCME, OML 2015-148) or when the rest of the topic carried the meaning (LBOH, OML
2022-176). The test is the reader, not the body: an acronym "meaningful to the Commission
and individuals familiar with the regular business of the Commission" is not enough
(OML 2024-218, which is about Lunenburg's Historical Commission).

## The standard the acronym cases apply

Every quotation below is verbatim from the AG's letter. Our copy of each letter is listed
at the end.

- **The rule.** Topics must be listed with "sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the
  public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting" (940 CMR 29.03(1)(b), quoted in
  OML 2015-128, 1 Sep 2015).
- **How the AG reads it.** "We generally consider a topic to include sufficient specificity
  when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated
  nature of the public body's discussion." (OML 2015-128; the same test in OML 2014-125 and
  OML 2024-218.)
- **The notice is read against the meeting.** "unless it is clearly insufficient on its
  face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone. ... Rather, we review what was
  discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently specific."
  (OML 2024-218, 14 Nov 2024.)

## What the determinations say about acronyms

| Determination | Body | Acronym | What the AG said |
|---|---|---|---|
| OML 2014-138, 1 Dec 2014 | Cape & Vineyard Electric Coop. | (in minutes) | "While we do not consider CVEC's use of acronyms here to be a violation, we generally discourage public bodies from using acronyms in meeting minutes" |
| OML 2015-148, 7 Oct 2015 | Barnstable School Committee | AFSCME | "\"AFSCME\" is a widely understood abbreviation for the American Federation of State, County & Municipal Employees. ... The use of this abbreviation on the meeting notice did not violate the Open Meeting Law." |
| OML 2015-197, 28 Dec 2015 | Barnstable Town Council Roads Subcommittee | TRRP | "the acronym, \"TRRP,\" which stands for \"Temporary Repair to Private Roads Policy,\" is not widely understood by members of the public who are unfamiliar with the Subcommittee's work. Accordingly, the use of this abbreviation on this meeting notice violated the Open Meeting Law." |
| OML 2018-56, 20 Apr 2018 | Group Insurance Commission | OOP | "Public bodies may not use acronyms in a meeting notice, unless they are commonly understood." |
| OML 2020-152, 23 Nov 2020 | Monomoy Regional School Committee | COA | "Our office discourages the use of abbreviations or acronyms that are not widely understood by members of the public." (a footnote; not the finding) |
| OML 2022-174, 19 Sep 2022 | Medway School Committee | SLA | "the inclusion of an acronym like \"SLA\" on a meeting notice likely does not comply with the Open Meeting Law. ... Although \"SLA\" might be meaningful to Committee members and regular Committee meeting attendees, it is potentially unclear to members of the public unfamiliar with the regular matters before the Committee." (a footnote; not raised in the complaint) |
| OML 2022-176, 19 Sep 2022 | Fall River Board of Health | LBOH | "Whether the use of the acronym \"LBOH\" rendered the notice insufficient is a close call. ... In context, we are satisfied that the topic as listed on the notice was sufficiently specific" -- **no violation** |
| OML 2023-83, 17 May 2023 | Medfield School Committee | MPS, MASC, BEDH, BEDH-E | "We find that the acronym \"MPS\" referring to Medfield Public Schools on a Medfield School Committee meeting notice could be widely understood by members of the public. However, the acronym \"MASC\" standing for Massachusetts Association of School Committees and policy codes \"BEDH\" and \"BEDH-E,\" without context or explanation, were not abbreviations or codes that would be widely understood by members of the public." -- found insufficiently specific |
| OML 2024-218, 14 Nov 2024 | **Lunenburg Historical Commission** | CPA | "although the acronym \"CPA\" may be meaningful to the Commission and individuals familiar with the regular business of the Commission, it is not an acronym widely understood by members of the public and should not be used." (footnote 5; the violation was found on specificity, and the AG did not separately decide the acronym) |
| OML 2025-153, 29 Sep 2025 | Somerset Planning Board | B.E.S.S. | "the notice was not sufficiently specific because \"B.E.S.S.\" is an acronym not widely understood by members of the public, and thus its use here violated the Open Meeting Law." |
| OML 2026-99, 10 Aug 2026 | Medfield Housing Authority | PMR | "Inclusion of an acronym that is not widely understood by the public will render a notice topic insufficiently specific, in violation of the Open Meeting Law." |

**What the table shows.** The wording in the letters gets firmer over time. In 2014 and 2015 the
AG "generally discourage[s]" acronyms. In 2018 it says public bodies "may not use" them. In
2026 an uncommon acronym "will render a notice topic insufficiently specific". The letters
also keep citing one comparison: TRRP (a violation) against FAA (OML 2015-172, "commonly used
and generally understood", which we have not fetched).

**What it does not show.** None of these letters gives a list of acceptable acronyms, a test
for "widely understood" beyond the examples, or a rule about acronyms on a school
committee notice in particular. Each one is decided on its own facts.

## The general specificity cases (for balance)

- **"Old Business" / "New Business" as headings are not violations when specific items are
  listed under them.** "While the use of such headings alone may be insufficient to give the
  public a true idea of what will be discussed, ... here the Board used the phrases as
  headings under which more specific descriptions were listed. This practice does not
  violate the Open Meeting Law." (OML 2015-127, Sandwich, 1 Sep 2015.)
- **A topic with no content is a violation.** For "Finalize pending issues" and "General
  housekeeping": "neither anticipated topic contain enough detail to give a member of the
  public an idea as to what would be discussed. This violated the Open Meeting Law."
  (OML 2015-128, Warren, 1 Sep 2015.)
- **Name the specific matter.** Where the chair knew which permits were coming, the board
  "should take the additional step of listing in the meeting notice the details of those
  specific permits, including the name of the applicant and the location under
  consideration." (OML 2011-11, Freetown, 15 Feb 2011.)
- **A broad topic is enough when the discussion is broad.** "FY 15 Budget discussion": "as
  written the topic was sufficient to inform the public that the Committee would be
  discussing the entire FY 15 school budget." -- **no violation** (OML 2014-125, Lanesborough
  Elementary School Committee, 6 Nov 2014.)
- **Naming the subject is not the same as naming the issue.** In Lunenburg: "The notice
  topics before us here informed the public only that a specific park, cemetery, and statute
  would be discussed; they did not inform the public of the specific issues to be discussed."
  (OML 2024-218.)

## The 7 October 2026 item: our reading, not a determination

The School Committee's revised agenda for 7 October 2026
(`sources/meetings/text/school-committee/2026-10-07-agenda-8058.txt`, publisher URL
https://www.lunenburgma.gov/AgendaCenter/ViewFile/Agenda/_10072026-8058) lists, under
"9. New Business / Action":

> d. COTA Position Change to .5 OT (3 min)

**What the agenda shows.** One line with two abbreviations, COTA and OT, and an unexplained
".5". The agenda does not spell either one out. The Committee's 29 July 2026 minutes do:
"Certified Occupational Therapy Assistant (COTA)"
(`sources/meetings/text/school-committee/2026-07-29-minutes-7930.txt`, line 335). The item is
listed under Action, so a vote was anticipated.

**How the determinations above would read it. This is our reading.**

- *COTA.* It is a staffing title in special education. On the AG's examples it looks more
  like SLA (Medway School Committee) and MASC/BEDH (Medfield School Committee) than like
  AFSCME or FAA. The AG has also said that a body's own familiarity with a term does not
  count: "meaningful to the Commission and individuals familiar with the regular business"
  (OML 2024-218). Spelling COTA out in July's minutes does not put the meaning on the
  October notice.
- *OT and ".5".* OT is more widely known, but it has more than one meaning (occupational
  therapy or therapist; overtime). ".5" means half a full-time position only to a reader who
  already knows the convention.
- *The issue, not only the subject.* The line does say something will change: one position
  becomes another, at half time. That is more than "Marshall Park" or "CPA" said in
  OML 2024-218. The AG's test still asks whether a reasonable member of the public could
  "understand the anticipated nature of the public body's discussion", and this line gives
  that only to a reader who can decode it.
- *What could weigh the other way.* The AG reads a notice against what was discussed. The
  minutes of 7 October are not posted. In OML 2022-176 the context of the topic saved an
  unclear acronym. Whether that could happen here depends on the discussion, and we cannot
  see it.

**What would settle it.** Only an AG determination on this notice. Short of that, the
Committee's own minutes of 7 October 2026 would show what the item actually covered.

**A wording that avoids the question** (offered as an example, not as what the law requires):
"Special education staffing: change the vacant Certified Occupational Therapy Assistant
position to a half-time (0.5 FTE) Occupational Therapist -- vote."

## How the letters were found, and what that search can and cannot tell you

The AG's determination lookup (https://massago.hylandcloud.com/231publicaccess2/OML.html,
linked from the AG's Open Meeting Law page) has a full-text search. It was searched on
8 October 2026 through the portal's own API (reproduce with
`python3 scripts/fetch_oml_determinations.py --search acronym`):

| search text | letters returned |
|---|---:|
| acronym | 66 |
| acronyms | 66 |
| abbreviation | 48 |
| abbreviations | 48 |
| jargon | 1 (OML 2025-127, Ashby: "jargon" on a Town Meeting warrant) |

Together the four acronym searches return 73 distinct letters (72 determinations and 1
declination), dated 2013 to 2026. A hit means the word is in the letter. It does not
mean the letter decided anything about acronyms: some hits are about minutes, some are
footnotes, and some quote a complainant. **Fifteen letters were fetched and read.** The
other 58 were not, so this note does not claim to cover every acronym determination. The
fifteen were picked for: this town (2024-218), school committees (2015-148, 2020-152,
2022-174, 2023-83, 2014-125), violations found on an acronym alone (2015-197, 2025-153,
2026-99), no violation found (2015-148, 2022-176, 2014-125), the earliest statements
(2014-138, 2011-11) and the general specificity standard (2015-127, 2015-128). Search hits
that look relevant but were not fetched: OML 2015-154 and OML 2015-172 (FAA), which the
later letters keep citing, and OML 2016-5 (PILOT), OML 2017-197 and OML 2024-44 (Wayland
School Committee, BEDH).
