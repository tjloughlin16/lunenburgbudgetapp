We find the facts to be as follows. On March 11 the Town issued a request for proposal 
for the purchase and development of Town-owned land located at 59 Water Street (the “RFP”). 
The RFP identified that the Town was seeking proposed development at 59 Water Street that 
would enhance Water Street as an anchor of the Town’s commercial tax base and that 
emphasized mixed use, explaining that “[a] Highly Advantageous rating will be given to 
proposals that emphasize the creation of commercial space and multi-family housing that will 
add to and improve the vibrancy of Water Street and the Village Business district and the Town’s 
property tax base.” (emphasis in original).   
 
The sole response to the RFP came from Williams College. The College proposed to 
develop “a three- to four-story Facilities Services building of approximately 30,000 gross square 
feet and a structured parking facility providing approximately 170 spaces to support institutional 
and public needs.” The College acknowledged that its proposal did not include the creation of 
multifamily housing or commercial space, asserting that “the Site’s challenging development 
conditions around the protected riverfront area, Christmas Brook culvert, and access road to 
serve the College’s central heating plant make this unfeasible.” The proposal further explained 
that in order to advance the objectives of the Town despite the College’s inability to provide 
multifamily housing and commercial space, the College was proposing an “initial purchase price 
of $500,000, which is significantly higher than both the Site’s tax-assessed value of $254,200 
and its appraised value of $350,000,” further supplemented by “a 10-year, $50,000 annual 
contribution to the Mount Greylock Regional School District beginning in fiscal year 2028, . . . 
[bringing] the College’s total financial offer for the Site to $1,000,000.” The proposal also 
asserted that the College’s development would enhance “the vitality of the downtown area by 
strengthening the physical and functional connection between Water Street and Spring Street[,] . 
. . enhance[ing] the pedestrian linkages between these corridors, [and improving] walkability 
between the retail centers.” Finally, the proposal explained that “[p]arking [was] a central 
component of the College’s proposal, and that parking will directly support downtown 
functionality, including businesses on Water Street.” 
 
The Board posted notice for a meeting to be held on May 11 at 7:00 p.m. Included as a 
topic on the meeting notice was an executive session to be held under G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6) 
(“Purpose 6”) to discuss Town property located at 59 Water Street. The meeting was held as 
scheduled. When the Board reached the executive session topic, Board Chair Stephanie Boyd 
read the executive session purpose aloud, declared that holding the discussion in open session 
may have a detrimental effect on the Board’s negotiating position, and announced that the Board 
would not reconvene in open session. Thereafter, Chair Boyd adjourned the Board to executive 
session. The Board did not take a vote to convene in executive session.   
 
In executive session the Board received an update from the Town Manager regarding the 
timeline for the Town’s review of the College’s proposal. After the Town Manager provided this 
information and left the meeting, Chair Boyd explained that she had discussed the propriety of 
that evening’s executive session with legal counsel. The Board then discussed site features such 
as the culvert and the access road; potential for tax revenue if the site were to be used for 
commercial or residential development; approaches it might take in future negotiations for the 
property including swapping the property for an alternative or changing the College’s proposed

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). All meetings of a public body must be conducted in an 
open session unless a closed-door executive session is convened for one of ten permissible 
purposes. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). Prior to convening in executive session, the Open 
Meeting Law requires that the public body take certain procedural steps in open session, 
including that “a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive session and 
the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes.” G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 21(b)(2).  
 
Pursuant to the Open Meeting Law, a public body may meet in executive session to 
“consider the purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property if the chair declares that an 
open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the negotiating position of the public body.” G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21(a)(6) (“Purpose 6”). Purpose 6 is intended to preserve confidentiality in negotiating 
the value of the property to be purchased, exchanged or leased to avoid putting the public body at 
a disadvantage in its negotiations for the property. See District Atty. for the Plymouth Dist. v. 
Selectmen of Middleborough, 395 Mass. 629, 631 (1985); OML 2016-50.4 Executive session 
purposes are narrowly construed. The public body bears the burden of demonstrating 
applicability of the claimed executive session purpose. See District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. 
School Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 566 (2009). Discussions under Purpose 6 must relate 
directly to the purchase, lease, exchange, or value of real property where confidentiality is 
necessary to protect a public body’s negotiating position. See OML 2024-37; OML 2022-119; 
OML 2021-62. The proper application of Purpose 6 is to protect a public body’s negotiating 
position vis-a-vis other parties to a transaction, not to escape the inconvenience of public 
scrutiny or opposition. See OML 2024-37; OML 2023-241; OML 2023-81. 
 
 T he complaint alleges that it was improper for the Board to convene in executive session 
because discussion of “negotiation strategies was ‘premature’” and should have followed an 
evaluation of the College’s proposal. The complaint further asserts that the “RFP itself outline[d] 
a process where the site [would be] awarded first and then negotiation takes place.” The Board 
asserts that its discussions were appropriate for executive session under Purpose 6 where they 
related to the value of the property at 59 Water Street and where having the discussions in open 
session may have had a detrimental effect on the Town’s negotiating position. 
 
We find here that, on the whole, the Board’s discussions in executive session on May 11 
regarding the property at 59 Water Street were appropriate under Purpose 6. We do not read 
Purpose 6 as narrowly as the Complainant urges. See OML 2022-119 (finding discussions of the 
potential exchange or acquisition of certain property and the identification of potential entities to 
approach regarding buying or exchanging real property appropriate for executive session under

Purpose 6); OML 2018-73 (finding discussion of potential acquisition of alternate elementary 
school sites appropriate for Purpose 6); OML 2016-50 (finding consideration of several potential 
sites for a new school appropriate under Purpose 6). We find that the Board did not violate the 
Open Meeting Law when it convened in executive session on May 11 to discuss matters related 
to the sale and value of 59 Water Street. 
 
 Second, the complaint alleges that the Board failed to vote by roll call prior to convening 
in executive session on May 11. The Board acknowledges that it failed to vote by roll call to 
convene in executive session. After reviewing a video recording of the meeting, we find that the 
Board failed to take any vote, whether by voice or roll call, to convene in executive session and 
instead simply adjourned to executive session following the required announcements. The 
executive session convening procedures, including the vote to convene in executive session, are 
intended to create a clear record of what occurred and to reflect the significance of holding a 
discussion behind closed doors. See OML 2015-131; OML 2014-94; OML 2012-10. We find 
that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it failed to vote to convene in executive 
session on May 11. We further note that the minutes of the Board’s May 11 open session state 
that Board member Matt Neely seconded a motion to convene in executive session and that “the 
motion passed unanimously by a roll call vote. 5-0: Beck-aye, Boyd-aye, Budington-aye, Dixon-
aye, Neely-aye.” Meeting minutes must accurately reflect what actually occurred during a 
meeting; therefore, it is improper to include in the minutes of the May 11 open session a motion 
and vote that did not occur. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2021-128; OML 2014-94 (minutes 
inaccurate where they record a roll call vote to convene in executive session when in fact the 
public body held a voice vote); OML 2012-37. We recommend that the Board amend the 
minutes of its May 11 open session meeting to accurately reflect what occurred during the 
meeting. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to vote by roll call to convene in executive session. We find that the Board did not 
otherwise violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged. We order the Board’s immediate and future 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution that similar future violations may be 
considered evidence of an intent to violate the Law. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Board or with
