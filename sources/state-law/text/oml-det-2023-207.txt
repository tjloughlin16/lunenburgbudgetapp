DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened.  
See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for 
any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened 
in an open session, that a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive 
session, that the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and 
the chair has publicly announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of 
the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.

The chair of the public body must announce in open session the purpose for the executive

sion, “stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which 
the executive session was called.”  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b)(3); see also District Attorney 
for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of 
the reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification 
given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the 
public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  This level of detail 
about the executive session topic must also be included in the meeting notice.  See G.L. c. 30A,  
§ 20(b); OML 2016-72.   
 
I. The Fe
 bruary 13, 2023, Meeting Notice Lacked Sufficient Specificity by 
Failing to Include the Specific Union the Board Would Negotiate with in 
Executive Session.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to post notice of a meeting at least 48 
hours i
n advance, including a “listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are required to list topics on a 
notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b); OML 2014-47.  We generally consider a topic sufficiently 
specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the 
anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.   
 
The complaint alleges that the executive session topic listed on the February 13, 2023, 
meetin
g notice lacked sufficient detail where it did not include the specific collective bargaining 
unit with whom the Board would be negotiating.  Here, the meeting notice and the Chair’s 
announcement stated that the Board would convene in executive session under Purpose 3 to 
discuss “Union Negotiations” without identifying any specific collective bargaining unit.  When 
convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 3, a public body must identify the collective 
bargaining unit with whom it will be negotiating, if doing so will not compromise the lawful 
purpose for secrecy.  See OML 2020-129; OML 2016-43.  The Board does not suggest that 
identifying such unit would have compromised the purpose for which the executive session was 
called.  As such, the Board should have identified on the meeting notice the collective bargaining 
unit that was the subject of the executive session, and the Board Chair should have publicly 
identified that unit prior to convening the executive session.  See OML 2020-30; OML 2013-97; 
OML 2012-39; OML 2011-56.  We find that the notice of the February 13, 2023, meeting 
contained insufficient specificity by failing to include this information and therefore the Board 
violated the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2020-143; OML 2019-114; OML 2012-68. 
 
Next, the complaint alleges that the February 13, 2023, meeting notice should have 
iden
tified the specific executive session meeting minutes to be approved.  Notices should 
generally include this detail.  See OML 2020-34; OML 2017-92; OML 2013-199.  Here, the 
February 13, 2023, notice did not identify the specific minutes to be approved, and instead 
included a range of executive session minutes between September 13, 2021, and December 12, 
2022, that the Board would approve.  We understand that the Board reviewed all executive 
session minutes of meetings held between September 2021 and December 2022 and therefore

II. We Find that the Board Complied with the Required Procedures for 
Convening in Executive Session During the Meeting Held on December 
12, 2022, but Violated the Open Meeting Law by Failing to Comply with 
One Procedural Convening Requirement for the Meeting Held on 
February 13, 2023.   
 
The complaint alleges that the Chair did not announce the purpose for the executive 
ses
sion, and that the Board failed to conduct a roll call vote prior to convening in executive 
session on December 12, 2022.  Here, the Board properly convened in open session and 
announced that it would not resume in open session but would adjourn following its executive 
session.  After reviewing a video recording of the December 12, 2022, meeting, we find that the 
Chair’s announcement and the Board’s vote to convene in executive session were not recorded as 
Fall River Government TV ceased the recording just prior to the Board’s announcement and 
vote.  However, the Board states and the minutes of the December 12, 2022, meeting reflect that 
the Chair announced the purpose for the executive session and that the Board took a roll call 
vote.  We note that the procedural requirements for convening in executive session are intended 
to protect the public’s right to know when a public body is discussing matters behind closed 
doors, and the reasons its members believe a private discussion is warranted.  See OML 2014-43; 
OML 2013-195.  We find that the Board complied with the Open Meeting Law’s procedural 
requirements for convening in executive session on December 12, 2022.    
 
The complaint further alleges that the Board failed to conduct a roll call vote prior to 
conveni
ng in executive session on February 13, 2023.  Here, the Board properly convened in 
open session and announced that it would not resume in open session but adjourn following its 
executive session.  However, the Board failed to conduct a roll call vote; instead, after the 
motion was made to convene in executive session, Chair Sahady asked “all in favor” and 
members stated, in unison, “Aye.”  This was a violation of the Open Meeting Law as the law 
specifically requires that the vote of each member to enter executive session be by roll call.  See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 21 (b).  A roll call vote is “the act or an instance of calling off a list of names.”  
Webster’s Third New International Dictionary of the English Language, 683 (unabridged ed. 
1961).  This requirement is intended to create a clear record of what occurred and to reflect the 
significance of holding a discussion behind closed doors.  See OML 2015-131; OML 2014-94; 
OML 2012-10.  Failure to follow required procedures casts doubt on the validity of any action 
taken during an executive session.  See OML 2013-9; OML 2012-68. 
 
We note that the minutes of the February 13, 2023, reflect that the vote to convene in 
exec
utive session was recorded by roll call.  The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body 
create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings setting forth a summary of the discussions 
on each subject and the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the 
record of all votes.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  To be “accurate,” meeting minutes must 
accurately reflect the discussions that took place during a meeting.  See OML 2020-62.  Because 
the minutes must accurately capture the discussions and actions that actually took place, it is 
inappropriate to include information in meeting minutes that was not discussed or did not occur

at a meeting.  S ee OML 2021-128.  We encourage the Board to revise the minutes to reflect the 
vote to convene in executive session as it actually occurred during the February 13, 2023, 
meeting. 
 
III. We Fin
 d that Two of the Topics the Board Discussed in Executive on 
February 13, 2023, were Improper, While the Remaining Two Topics 
Discussed were Proper.  
 
The complaint challenges the propriety of the Board’s February 13, 2023, executive 
session.  Here, the notice of the February 13, 2023, meeting included an executive session to 
discuss “Executive Director Evaluation” and “Executive Team Restructuring” under Purpose 2, 
“Union Negotiations” under Purpose 3, and “HUD Audit Update to Maintain Compliance with 
HUD Action Plan” under Purpose 7. 
 
A public body may convene in executive session to “conduct strategy sessions in 
prepa
ration for negotiations with nonunion personnel,” conduct contract negotiations or 
collective bargaining sessions, or “discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining or 
litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating 
position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2) (“Purpose 2”); 
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).  A public body may enter executive session to conduct 
actual collective bargaining negotiations with either union or nonunion personnel under Purpose 
2; or enter executive session to discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining negotiations 
under Purpose 3.  See OML 2018-19.  
 
Here, the February 13, 2023, meeting notice described two of the executive sessions, 
“Execu
tive Director Evaluation” and “Executive Team Restructuring,” as pertaining to contract  
negotiations under Purpose 2.  Our review of the as-yet-unreleased executive session minutes 
shows that the Board used this executive session to discuss the process of how to conduct an 
evaluation of the Executive Director and the steps to be taken to begin that process.  Under 
“Executive Team Restructuring,” the Board discussed the creation of two new non-union 
positions within the Housing Authority and identified specific individuals who might be 
interested in filling those positions.  Performance evaluations and discussions of an employee’s 
professional competence must occur in open session as they do not fit within any of the ten 
permissible purposes to enter executive session.  See OML 2021-45; OML 2018-38.  Although 
we find no evidence that the Board performed an evaluation of the Executive Director in 
executive session, the discussion about the process by which the evaluation should be conducted 
was not appropriate for executive session.  See OML 2021-88; OML 2021-45; OML 2019-57.  
Moreover, the Board’s discussion of the process for creating and hiring two new non-union 
positions did not fit within the parameters of executive session Purpose 2, or any other executive 
session purpose.  See OML 2021-21; OML 2013-195.  
 
As a result of our finding that these two topics were inappropriate for executive session 
and in vi
olation of the Open Meeting Law, we order the Board, within 30 days of this letter, to 
publicly release the portion of the February 13, 2023, executive session minutes that pertained to 
the topics “Executive Director Evaluation” and “Executive Team Restructuring.”  The Board 
may not redact or withhold that portion of the minutes in any way.  See G.L c. 30A, § 22(f)

(“The minutes of any executive session ... may be withheld from disclosure to the public in their 
entirety ... as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no 
longer; provided, however, that the executive session was held in compliance with section 21”) 
(emphasis added)); OML 2014-42. 
 
We find that the discussions in executive session that related to “Union Negotiations” 
under Purpose 3 were appropriate as the Board discussed its strategy with respect to a collective 
bargaining agreement with the municipal employees’ union.  See OML 2022-229. 
 
Finally, we find that the discussions pertaining to the “HUD Audit Update” were 
appropriate under Purpose 7.  A public body may convene in executive session under Purpose 7 
“to comply with, or act under the authority of, any general or special law or federal grant-in-aid 
requirements.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(7).  We understand that the Housing Authority receives 
federal funding from HUD and that HUD had previously audited the Housing Authority and 
required that it create a corrective action plan to show fiscal responsibility.  The Board’s 
discussions in executive session related to the required corrective action plans and the Housing 
Authority’s compliance with such financial reporting so as to continue to receive federal funding.  
We remind the Board that when a public body enters executive session under Purpose 7, it must 
cite the specific general or special law, or federal grant-in-aid requirement, that requires 
confidentiality or requires the public body to meet behind closed doors.  See Dist. Attorney for 
N. Dist. v. School Committee of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 569 (2009) (explaining that the proper 
use of Purpose 7 to enter executive session requires the enumeration of the enabling law); OML 
2013-144.  
 
Although not specifically raised in the complaints, we note our concern with the Board’s 
exec
utive session held on December 12, 2022.  During that meeting, the Board convened in 
executive session under Purpose 5 to discuss “a Personnel Matter.”  First, “personnel” is not an 
appropriate reason to enter executive session.  While several of the delineated purposes under the 
Open Meeting Law exist to allow bodies to deliberate on personnel related issues, “personnel” 
by itself is not a statutorily authorized purpose to enter executive session.  See OML 2011-9.  
Second, public bodies may convene in executive session under Purpose 5 to “investigate charges 
of criminal misconduct or to consider the filing of criminal complaints.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(5).  
An executive session convened pursuant to Purpose 5 involves the conversations that would 
precede the formal criminal process in court.  See OML 2016-21; OML 2015-99.  During the 
executive session, the Board did not discuss or consider filing criminal complaints but rather 
received an update from the Executive Director of the Housing Authority on disciplinary action 
recently taken against a Housing Authority employee.  Although it was not an appropriate  
discussion under Purpose 5, it may have been an appropriate matter to discuss under Purpose 1 
had the Board notified the subject of the executive session.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1) (public 
body may convene in executive session under Purpose 1 to “discuss the reputation, character, 
physical condition or mental health, rather than professional competence, of an individual, or to 
discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or charges brought against, a public officer, 
employee, staff member or individual” and 
triggers certain rights for the individual who is the 
subject of the discussion).  We remind the Board of its obligations under the law.

IV. The Board Violated the Open Meeting Law by Failing to Timely Approve 
Certain Executive Session Minutes During the February 13, 2023, 
Meeting.  
 
The complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely approve executive session minutes 
for m
eetings held between September 2021 and December 2022.  The Open Meeting Law 
requires public bodies to create and approve meeting minutes, whether for open or executive 
session, in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” means “within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can 
show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 29.11; see OML 2018-48.  Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2018-67; 
OML 2017-133.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f) states, in relevant part, that the minutes of any executive 
session, the notes, recordings or other materials used in the preparation of such minutes may be 
withheld from disclosure to the public in their entirety as long as publication may defeat the 
lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer.  When the purpose for which a valid 
executive session was held has been served, the minutes, preparatory materials and documents of 
the session shall be disclosed.  
 
Alt
hough the Board did not list or otherwise identify the specific executive session 
minutes it planned to approve during the February 13, 2023, meeting, it appears that the Board 
convened in executive session almost every month between September 2021 and December 
2022.
5  As such, because the Board did not approve the majority of its executive session minutes 
until February 13, 2023, more than three meetings or 30 days after the meetings held between 
September 2021 and October 2022, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to approve these minutes in a timely manner.  We find that the Board approved executive 
session minutes of meetings held on November 14, 2022, and December 12, 2022, in a timely 
manner as these minutes were approved within the next three meetings. 
 
We remind the Board that approval of executive session minutes is a separate and 
unrel
ated obligation from the requirement that executive session minutes be periodically 
reviewed to determine whether they should be released to the public.  See OML 2019-115.  This 
initial approval of executive session minutes is to approve the minutes as an accurate record of 
what occurred at a particular executive session and does not mean that the minutes are approved 
for release to the public.  We note that the Open Meeting Law does not specify how meeting 
minutes must be approved.  This means that public bodies may approve meeting minutes in a 
variety of ways.  For example, a public body may delegate authority to a single person, such as 
the chair, to approve the minutes; approve the minutes through a consent agenda; or distribute 
the minutes in advance by email and state that they will be deemed approved as of the date of the 
next meeting unless someone advises the chair they wish to discuss them at the next meeting.  
See AGO FAQ on Meeting Minutes.

V. The Board Failed to Timely Respond to Open Meeting Law Complaints. 
 
Finally, the complaints allege that the Board failed to timely respond to prior Open 
Meet
ing Law complaints filed on February 20, 2023, and March 11, 2023.  The Open Meeting 
Law requires that within 14 days of receipt of a complaint, the public body review the 
complaint’s allegations; take remedial action, if appropriate; and send to the Attorney General a 
copy of the complaint and a description of any remedial action taken.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 
CMR 29.05(5).  Upon the filing of an Open Meeting Law complaint with a public body, the chair 
“shall disseminate copies of the complaint to the members of the public body,” and “the public 
body shall review the complaint’s allegations.”  940 CMR 29.05(3), (5).  Thus, a public body 
must meet to review the complaint and formulate a response, or meet to delegate that authority, 
and respond to the complaint within 14 business days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); OML 2017-69; 
OML 2012-90.   
 
The Board disc
 ussed the two complaints during a meeting held on March 20, 2023, and 
General Counsel Michael Sousa responded to both complaints by separate letters dated April 5, 
2023.  We understand that the complaints were emailed to Chair Sahady’s business email 
address, rather than to the email address for the Board, and were not sent to Attorney Sousa.  
However, the Open Meeting Law and Regulations require the complainant to file a complaint 
with the chair of the public body, who shall disseminate copies of the complaint to the members 
of the public body.  See G.L. c. 30A, §23; 940 CMR 29.05(3).  The Board does not suggest that 
Chair Sahady did not receive the complaints; therefore, where Chair Sahady received the 
complaints on or about February 20, 2023, and March 11, 2023, we find that the Board was on 
notice and had an obligation to respond to the complaints within 14 business days.  As the Board 
did not respond to the complaints until April 5, 2023, we find a violation of the Open Meeting 
Law.   
   
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the O pen Meeting Law by 
failing to list sufficient detail in the February 13, 2023, notice in not identifying the collective 
bargaining unit that was the subject of the executive session; failing to follow certain procedures 
for convening an executive session on February 13, 2023; improperly discussing certain matters 
in executive session during the February 13, 2023, meeting; failing to timely approve certain 
executive session minutes; and failing to respond to Open Meeting Law complaints within 14 
business days.  We order the Board to release that portion of the minutes of the executive session 
meeting held on February 13, 2023, that pertained to the discussions identified as “Executive 
Director Evaluation” and “Executive Team Restructuring” within 30 days of the date of this 
letter.  In addition, we order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s 
requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.
