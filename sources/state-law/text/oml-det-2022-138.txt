The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be noticed and open to the public, unless an executive session is convened.  G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public 
body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c 30A, § 18.  The law 
defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including 
electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its 
jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of other 
procedural meeting [sic] or the distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a 
meeting, provided than no opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  For purposes of the Open 
Meeting Law, a “quorum” is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Committee deliberated outside of a properly posted 
meetin
g when Committee Member Julie Connelly sent an email on September 16, 2021, that 
included her recommendations on a proposed Downtown District Zoning Bylaw.  Although the 
email was sent directly to the Southborough Planning Board, all Committee members, as well as 
the Economic Development Coordinator, Town Administrator, and Select Board, were copied on 
the email.  The email provided Ms. Connelly’s “proposed revisions to the Draft Zoning Bylaw” 
stating what she believed were four “critical” adjustments “to the successful implementation of 
the Bylaw if it should pass” at Town Meeting.  We do not find that the Committee violated the 
Open Meeting Law even though the email was sent to a quorum of Committee members and 
included Ms. Connelly’s opinion on the Downtown District Zoning Bylaw, a topic the 
Committee had been discussing for several years.  Rather, we find that the email simply 
memorialized the opinions that Ms. Connolly had previously shared during a Committee meeting 
held on September 9, 2021, and a Planning Board meeting held on September 13, 2021, and did 
not convey any new opinions.  See OML 2020-48.2  Thus, we find that the September 16, 2021, 
email did not constitute deliberation outside of a properly posted meeting and therefore did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law. 
 
The complaint also suggests that the Committee’s no tices generally lack insufficient 
specificity.  We will not conduct broad audits of a public body and thus decline to review 
generalized allegations about the notice posting practices of the Committee.  See OML 2016-57.  
However, we have reviewed the notice for the Committee meeting held on September 9, 2021, 
and find that the notice included sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the 
issues to be discussed at the meeting, including a discussion of the proposed Downtown District 
Zoning Bylaw.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b) (notice must be printed in a 
legible, easily understandable format and contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well 
as a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed, with the topics
