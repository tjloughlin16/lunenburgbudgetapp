This office received two complaints from Michael Weishan, alleging that the 
Southborough Planning Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The first complaint was filed with the Board on December 16, 2025, and you responded 
on behalf of the Board by letter dated January 7, 2026.  This complaint was filed with our office 
on January 28, 2026.  In it, the complainant alleges that the Board held a non-emergency meeting 
without posting notice 48 hours in advance of the meeting.  The second complaint was filed with 
the Board on January 7, 2026, and you responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated January 
27, 2026.  This complaint was filed with our office on February 10, 2026.  The second complaint 
alleges that at an on-site inspection at 42 Central Street on January 7, 2026, the Board harassed 
the Southborough Historical Society, and deliberated outside a posted meeting. 
 
We appreciate the parties’ patience as we reviewed these complaints.  We find that the 
Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it deliberated at an on-site inspection on January 7, 
2026.  We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it met on December 
4, 2025, without posting notice 48 hours prior to the meeting.1  We decline to review the 
remaining allegations of the complaints.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the 
complaints, the Board’s responses, and the requests for further review.  We also reviewed the 
notice and minutes of the December 4 meeting.  Finally, we viewed a recording of the December 
4 meeting.2

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  In or about 2023, the Southborough Historical Society 
commenced renovations of a historic building located at 42 Central Street, to be used as the 
Southborough History & Arts Center (“the Center”).  In furtherance of the project, the Historical 
Society secured permits and a site plan.  On November 13, 2025, the complainant, on behalf of 
the Historical Society, met with Town Planner Karina Quinn.  In an email later that day, he noted 
that a Board meeting, scheduled for December 1, had been cancelled and asked whether it could 
be “resuscitated,” stating that it was “fairly urgent we discuss a temporary occupancy permit for 
42 Central Street in light of the AGO July 12th 2025 updated Dover Amendment Guidance.”3  
Ms. Quinn responded by confirming that the December 1 meeting was cancelled; she asked 
whether the board of the Historical Society wished to be on the agenda for a meeting to be held 
December 8.  In response, by email dated November 14, the complainant again asked to “revive” 
the December 1 meeting to discuss “very important business” “before the sixth.”  
 
During the last week of November, a flyer was sent to residents of Southborough, 
announcing the Center’s opening.  The flyer advertised a Holiday Arts and Crafts Fair & Open 
House, to be held on Saturday, December 6 from 11:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.  Between the date that 
the flyer was mailed and the December 6 Open House, the complainant had no communication 
with the Board chair. 
 
December 3 Meeting 
 
On Wednesday, December 3, the Town Building Commissioner advised Town Planner 
Quinn that the Historical Society was in discussions with Town Administrator Mark Purple and 
Town Counsel to secure a temporary certificate of occupancy in advance of the Center’s 
December 6 Open House.  Upon learning of these discussions, Town Planner Quinn relayed this 
information to Board Chair Meme Luttrell.  On the evening of Wednesday, December 3, at 
approximately 6:18 p.m., the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on Thursday, 
December 4 at 9:00 a.m.  The sole topic of discussion was listed as “40-42 Central St. – Potential 
Zoning Code Violation.” 
 
The meeting went forward on December 4, as scheduled.  The Chair opened the meeting 
by displaying the flyer and stating that there was an issue “that I thought the Planning Board 
should be aware of.”  She noted that an Open House was being planned even though an 
occupancy permit had not been issued.  The Chair further explained that discussions regarding a 
temporary occupancy permit had come to the Board’s attention the previous day.  She stated that 
Town Administrator Purple and Town Counsel were negotiating an agreement with the 
complainant, bypassing the Board in what the Chair deemed a violation of Town bylaws.  She 
did not know what steps the Board should take in response to these developments. 
 
The Board met for over one hour.  Town Planner Quinn, who also attended the meeting, 
described prior interactions with the complainant about the Center, going back months.

 January 7, 2026, On-Site Inspection 
 
The Board is comprised of five members.  Thus, three members constitute a quorum.  On 
January 7, 2026, three members of the Board visited the Center to conduct an on-site inspection 
requested by the complainant.  The Board did not post notice of the on-site inspection as a 
meeting.  While on site, the Board Chair opined about a deed restriction on the subject property 
and made statements about other aspects of the renovation project, including alleged code 
violations. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it met on December 4 
without posting 48 hours’ notice. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that “[e]xcept in an emergency, in addition to any notice 
otherwise required by law, a public body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours 
prior to such meeting excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
“In an emergency, a public body shall post notice as soon as reasonably possible prior to the 
meeting.”  Id.  The Open Meeting Law defines an emergency as “a sudden, generally unexpected 
occurrence or set of circumstances demanding immediate action.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18; 940 CMR 
29.02.  The burden of justifying the need for an emergency meeting falls on the public body.  See 
OML 2026-55; OML 2025-201; OML 2020-123.5

For months, if not years, prior to the December 6 Open House, the Board was party to (or 
at least aware of) disagreements with the Historical Society about the latter’s obligations under 
permits and a site plan issued relative to the renovation of the Center.  It appears that many of 
these disagreements arose out of differing interpretations of the Dover Amendment.  
Additionally, it is almost certain that the Board knew of the December 6 Open House before 
December 3, the date they posted notice of the December 4 meeting.  A flier was mailed out the 
week before the meeting.  As early as November 24, a column about the Center was posted on a 
local blog under the headline “Southborough History & Arts Center Grand Opening – December 
6.”6  That article included a picture of the flier.   
 
Although the Board knew that the Historical Society had scheduled the Open House 
without first securing a certificate of occupancy, it was the report of negotiations between Town 
Administrator Purple, Town Counsel and the Historical Society that prompted the December 4 
meeting.7  Because the Board learned of those negotiations on December 3, and because the 
Board was concerned that an agreement might be reached in the immediate future without its 
input, we find that the Board met its burden in justifying the need for an emergency meeting.  
Member DeMuria may well have been correct when she stated that the emergency meeting 
would not have been required had the Board not cancelled its December 1 meeting.  However, it 
was within the Board’s discretion to cancel the December 1 meeting, even if that later resulted in 
an unforeseen emergency.  Therefore, although a close question, we find that the Board did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law when it met on December 4 without 48 hours’ notice. 
 
The complainant notes that when the Board first learned of the Town Administrator’s 
negotiations with the Historical Society, three days remained before the Open House.  He argues 
that the Board could have scheduled a meeting for December 5, thereby providing for 48 hours’ 
notice of the meeting.  The Board, however, was reacting not to the December 6 Open House in 
isolation, but rather to negotiations pursued by Town Administrator Purple in anticipation of the 
Open House.  The Board was reasonably concerned that those negotiations might have 
concluded without the Board’s involvement had the Board delayed its meeting until the evening 
of December 5. 
 
II. The Board violated the Open Meeting Law when a quorum attended an on-site 
inspection on January 7, 2026, and deliberated. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an 
open session, unless an executive session is convened.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  The Open 
Meeting Law defines “meeting” as “a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter 
within the body’s jurisdiction; provided however, ‘meeting’ shall not include: (a) an on-site 
inspection of a project or program, so long as the members do not deliberate ...”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
18.  Subject to exceptions that are not relevant here, “deliberation” is defined as “an oral or 
written communication through any medium, including electronic mail, between or among a

quorum of a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction.”  Id.  For the purposes of 
the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” is a simple majority of the member of the public body.  Id.  
Expression of an opinion on matters within a body’s jurisdiction to a quorum of a public body is 
a deliberation, even if no other public body member responds.  See OML 2026-108; OML 2017-
44; OML 2016-104.  
 
To conduct an on-site inspection in compliance with the Open Meeting Law, a public 
body has three options.  It may: (1) post notice of the inspection as a meeting, provided the 
property is accessible to everyone, including members of the public and individuals with 
disabilities; (2) visit the property as individuals or in groups that constitute less than a quorum so 
as to avoid deliberation among a quorum; or (3) visit the property as a quorum, but refrain from 
asking any questions of the property owner or otherwise discussing any matters within the public 
body’s jurisdiction until the public body convenes in a properly noticed meeting.  See OML 
2025-169; OML 2018-57; OML 2017-44.  
 
Here, a quorum of the Board visited the Center on January 7, 2026.  The Chair made 
several remarks during the on-site inspection about the renovation project and alleged violations 
by the Southborough Historical Society.  There is no evidence that either of the two other Board 
members in attendance responded to those comments.  The lack of response notwithstanding, the 
Chair’s comments constituted deliberation, as they were communications on matters within the 
Board’s jurisdiction in the presence of a quorum of the Board.  Therefore, we find that the Board 
violated the Open Meeting Law at the site inspection. 
 
III. We decline to review the remaining allegation of the complaints. 
 
The complainant also alleges that the Board harassed the Historical Society during the 
January 7 on-site inspection.  We decline to review this allegation.  The Division of Open 
Government’s review concerns compliance with the Open Meeting Law.  Although our office 
encourages professionalism and civility during meetings of public bodies, an allegation of 
unprofessional conduct or incivility does not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  
See OML Declination 8-4-25 (Cohasset Affordable Housing Trust); OML 2019-166; OML 
2018-91.  As this allegation, even if true, could not be construed as a violation of the Open 
Meeting Law, we decline to review it.  See OML Declination 8-10-26 (North Brookfield Board 
of Selectmen); Declination 4-3-25 (Middlefield Conservation Commission); OML Declination 7-
2-18 (Edgartown Planning Board). 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it deliberated outside a meeting at the on-site inspection on January 7, 2026.  We order the 
Board to create minutes of the January 7, 2026, on-site inspection within 30 days of receipt of 
this letter.  We also order immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and 
caution that similar violations in the future may be considered evidence of intent to violate the 
Law.  940 CMR 29.02.
