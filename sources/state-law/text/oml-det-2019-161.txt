DISCUSSION
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberation and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The law requires that all meetings of a public body be 
conducted in an open session, with some exceptions. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). A public 
body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the 
Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that a majority of 
members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each member is 
recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly announced whether 
the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.3
I. The Committee Followed the Proper Procedures when it Convened in Executive
Session on December 6. 2018; December 20. 2018; and January 17. 2019.
The first, second and sixth complaints allege that the Committee failed to follow the 
proper procedures prior to entering executive session on December 6, 2018; December 20, 2018; 
and January 17, 2019. Prior to entering into executive session, a public body must convene in 
open session, the chair must state the purpose for the executive session, stating all subjects that 
may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called, 
and a majority of the members of the public body must vote to enter into executive session. See 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(b) (l)-(3). Here, on December 6, 2018; December 20, 2018; and January 17, 
2019, the Committee opened each meeting and then either unanimously voted or by majority 
vote entered executive session. A Committee member stated the specific purposes for each 
executive session and announced that the Committee would reconvene in open session at the 
conclusion of each executive session. We find that the Committee completed the appropriate 
procedural steps before convening in executive session, including announcing the purposes for 
the executive session and taking a roll call vote. In this respect, the Committee complied with 
the Open Meeting Law’s procedural requirements for entering executive session.
In addition, the mastheads of the Committee’s December 6, 2018, and January 17, 2019, 
meeting notices listed the meeting start time as 6:00 P.M., and under that, an executive session 
was listed as the first topic at 6:00 P.M., with the Call to Order and Pledge of Allegiance at 7:00 
P.M. followed by additional topics each with a specified time. The masthead of the notice for 
the Committee’s December 20, 2018, meeting listed the meeting start time as 6:30 P.M., and 
under that, an executive session was listed as the first topic at 6:30 P.M., with the Call to Order 
and Pledge of Allegiance at 7:00 P.M. followed by additional topics each with a specified time. 
When the only business to be discussed during an open session are the procedural requirements 
for entering executive session, we have written that public bodies must list “Open Session” on 
the meeting notice, as this is the only means by which members of the public are informed that a 
public body will, in fact, hold an open meeting that they are permitted to attend. See OML 2019- 
80; OML 2013-9; OML 2012-68. Without such notice, members of the public are essentially

denied the opportunity to attend and learn the reasons for the executive session. See OML 2013- 
195. However, where additional open session topics are included in a meeting notice, we have 
not required that a public body list “Open Session” as a distinct topic. See OML 2019-80; OML 
2015-157; OML 2015-110; OML 2015-105. Here, it was clear that an open session meeting 
would take place as the full notices for December 6, 2018; December 20, 2018; and January 17, 
2019, all provided context placing the executive session as a topic for discussion during an 
otherwise open meeting. We therefore find that the Committee was not required to list “Open 
Session” in its meeting notices and did not violate the Open Meeting Law by omitting this 
statement.
II. The Committee Did Not Provide Sufficient Notice Regarding the Executive
Session Topics for its Meetings Held on December 6. 2018; December 20, 2018;
and January 17. 2019. However, the Committee Met in Executive Session for a
Proper Purpose on December 6, 2018. and January 17. 2019, but Improperly Met
in Executive Session on December 20, 2018.
Public bodies may enter a closed, executive session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a). Executive session topics must be 
described, both in the meeting notice and in an announcement during open session, in as much 
detail as possible without compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called. 
See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm, of 
Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for convening in 
executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification given the public that a 
[public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the 
reason for doing so was proper or improper”).
The first, second and fifth complaints allege that the executive session topics listed on the 
December 6, 2018; December 20, 2018; and January 17, 2019, notices are “defective” and that 
the Committee improperly met in executive session on those three dates. Specifically, the 
complaints allege that the notices did not specify that the Committee was discussing a grievance 
hearing and failed to identify the litigation matter to be discussed. The complaints further allege 
that collective bargaining can only be conducted with non-union personnel and that “potential” 
litigation is not an appropriate discussion for executive session.
Executive session Purpose 2 allows a public body to enter executive session to “conduct 
strategy sessions in preparation for negotiations with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective 
bargaining sessions or contract negotiations with nonunion personnel.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2). 
Executive session Purpose 3 allows a public body to enter executive session to “discuss strategy 
with respect to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental 
effect on the bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.” G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21(a)(3). Grievance matters may be discussed under Purpose 2 or Purpose 3 because 
they are part of the collective bargaining process. See Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 73 (1978) (“[cjollective bargaining sessions encompass not only the 
negotiations leading to the agreement, but also the resolution of grievances pursuant to the 
collective bargaining agreement”). When convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 2 
or Purpose 3, a public body must identify the collective bargaining unit with whom it will be

negotiating, if doing so will not compromise the lawful purpose for secrecy. See OML 2018- 
139; OML 2016-43; OML 2015-87.
Here, both the December 6, 2018, and January 17, 2019, meeting notices indicated that 
the Committee would be entering executive session for a grievance hearing under collective 
bargaining and strategy with respect to potential litigation, and identified both matters as 
pertaining to an individual from Unit B. The December 20, 2018, meeting notice indicated that 
the Committee would be entering executive session to discuss strategy with respect to potential 
litigation with a union member. Together, executive session Purposes 2 and 3 allow a public 
body both to discuss collective bargaining strategy and to bargain directly with a union in 
executive session. See Board of Selectmen of Marion v. Labor Relations Commission, 7 Mass. 
App. Ct. 360 (1979); OML 2013-84. Unlike the language in Purpose 2, Purpose 3 does not limit 
strategy discussions to the preparatory stage, rather it broadly allows a body to go into executive 
session “to discuss strategy” with respect to collective bargaining or litigation any time an open 
meeting may have a detrimental effect on the body’s bargaining or litigating position and the 
chair so declares. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3).
We find that the notices of the December 6, 2018, and January 17, 2019, meetings were 
not sufficiently detailed. The notices for each meeting did not identify the individual with whom 
the Committee would be discussing a grievance matter, only referring to the individual as a Unit 
B member. A public body generally must specifically identify the individual or the employment 
position that is the subject of the grievance matter, see OML 2011-15, although we have stated 
that a public body may withhold the identity of an employee out of concern for the individual’s 
privacy. See OML 2015-180. The Committee does not argue that it did not include the name of 
the employee in order to protect the identity of that employee and the employee’s privacy 
interests. As such, we find that both the December 6, 2018, and January 17, 2019, notices were 
insufficiently detailed with respect to the executive session Purpose 2 topics. The notices for the 
December 6, 2018, and January 17, 2019, meetings with respect to the executive session Purpose 
3 topics were likewise insufficiently detailed.
Based on our in camera review of the minutes of the December 6, 2018, and January 17, 
2019, meetings, we find that the discussions that occurred under Purpose 3 were a continuation 
of the grievance matter and therefore related to collective bargaining, not litigation as stated on 
the meeting notices. Nonetheless, we find that the Committee’s discussions in executive session 
during the December 6, 2018, and January 17, 2019, meetings were proper under Purposes 2 and 
3 because the discussions that occurred during these two executive session meetings related to a 
grievance matter.
Finally, we find that the notice of the December 20, 2018, meeting was insufficiently 
detailed and also that the discussions that occurred during that meeting were not appropriate for 
executive session under Purpose 3. Although we recognize that the complainant had filed a civil 
lawsuit, that lawsuit was filed against the Town Clerk and not the Committee. We further 
recognize that the complainant filed several complaints with various state and federal agencies 
but the actual discussions in executive session on December 20, 2018, did not pertain to any of 
those matters. A public body’s discussions with its counsel do not automatically fall under 
Purpose 3 or any other executive session purpose. See Plymouth Dist. Attv v. Selectmen of

Middleborough, 395 Mass. 629 (1985); OML 2012-55. Rather, Purpose 3 offers the narrow 
opportunity to discuss strategy with respect to litigation that is pending or clearly and imminently 
threatened or otherwise demonstrably likely. See Doherty v. School Committee of Boston, 386 
Mass. 643, 648 (1982); Perryman v. School Committee of Boston. 17 Mass. App. Ct. 346, 352 
(1983); OML 2017-87; OML 2012-05. To invoke Purpose 3, a public body must have a 
litigating position to protect. See OML 2018-110; 2012-55; OML 2011-47. Here, we find that 
the Committee did not have a litigating position to protect because the Committee was not a 
party to the civil litigation matter and the Committee did not have decision making power or 
authority over the conduct of the litigation since it was filed against the Town Clerk. Therefore, 
the Committee’s December 20, 2018, executive session discussion was not an appropriate 
Purpose 3 discussion of litigation strategy. Moreover, although not relied on by the Committee, 
we note that the discussions during the December 20, 2018, meeting did not pertain to the 
pending grievance matter, and therefore would not have been appropriate as a discussion of a 
grievance matter under Purpose 2 or Purpose 3 either.
We find that the Committee improperly met in executive session on December 20, 2018. 
As a result, the Committee may not continue to withhold the minutes of the December 20, 2018, 
executive session under the Open Meeting Law. See G.L c. 30A, § 22(1) (minutes of an 
executive session may be withheld from disclosure to the public as long as publication may 
defeat the lawful purposes of an executive session, provided that the executive session was held 
in compliance with the Open Meeting Law)', OML 2014-42; OML 2014-30; OML 2014-17.
III. The Committee’s January 3, 2019. and January 17, 2019. Meeting Notices were
Sufficiently Detailed.
The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a public body shall post 
notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays 
and legal holidays.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). The notice must be printed in a legible, easily 
understandable format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed. Ick The list of topics 
shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific 
when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated 
nature of the public body’s discussion. See OML 2015-35.
The third complaint alleges that the Committee discussed a topic during its January 3, 
2019, meeting that was not listed on the notice. Specifically, the complainant alleges that Town 
Solicitor Joseph Callanan “made personal attacks” against him during the January 3, 2019, 
meeting. The notice for the January 3, 2019, meeting specifically identified discussion of the 
complainant’s fifth Open Meeting Law complaint against the Committee. While advising the 
Committee of the fifth Open Meeting Law complaint, Attorney Callanan mentioned that the 
complainant had filed hundreds of letters, emails and public records requests with the Town, as 
well as numerous complaints with various state and federal agencies.
The Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to anticipate the course of 
deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only that it identify the discussion topic with

sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting. 
See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38. 
We find, here, that the January 3, 2019, meeting notice sufficiently stated the topic to be 
discussed as it identified an Open Meeting Law complaint and specifically listed the 
complainant’s name as the individual who filed that complaint. Accordingly, we find no 
violation with respect to this allegation and note that the complainant should have clearly been 
on notice that he would likely be discussed. The Open Meeting Law does not require public 
bodies to list all tangential topics related to a discussion. See OML 2015-73. Moreover, except 
when meeting in executive session pursuant to G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1), the Open Meeting Law 
does not require public bodies to provide additional notice of meetings to specific individuals for 
topics discussed during a meeting. See OML 2013-41; OML 2011-41. Thus, the Committee did 
not violate the Open Meeting Law by failing to provide the complainant with individual notice.
The seventh complaint alleges that the “Consent Agenda” topic on the January 17, 2019, 
meeting notice lacked sufficient detail because it simply listed payment of bills without further 
identifying information. We have stated that a topic listed under “Consent Agenda,” which does 
not require the Committee’s discussion but is listed instead as a perfunctory administrative task, 
as it was here, does not need to include the specific bills. See OML 2018-22. However, 
whenever possible, the Committee should make an effort to include descriptive categories, such 
as identifying groups of related invoices or bills payable. See OML 2013-55.
IV. We Find No Evidence that the Committee’s December 6, 2018 Meeting was
Inaccessible.
The first complaint alleges that the December 6, 2018 was not accessible because the 
microphones were not turned on and therefore the statements made by the Chair to convene in 
executive session could not be heard. Meetings must be open and accessible to the public, 
including the open session portion of any meeting preceding an executive session. G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 20(a), 21(b)(1). Access to a meeting must include the opportunity to be physically present, as 
well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the members of the public body. See OML 
2015-38; OML 2014-3; OML 2013-189; OML 2012-66. This portion of the December 6, 2018 
meeting was not recorded and the minutes of the December 6, 2018 meeting reflect that the 
Committee announced the purpose for executive session and took a roll call vote. A public body 
is not required to make an audio or video recording of its own meeting. See OML 2014-82; 
OML2013-40. We find that we cannot arrive at a formal conclusion of whether the meeting was 
truly accessible where the complainant alleges that statements to convene in executive session 
were not audible, but the minutes reflect that such statements were made and there is no 
indication that either the complainant or any other members of the public who were present 
informed the Committee of difficulty hearing. Accordingly, we are constrained to find that the 
Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2018-50. We note that the 
procedural requirements for convening in executive session are intended to protect the public’s 
right to know when a public body is discussing matters behind closed doors, and the reasons its 
members believe a private discussion is warranted. See OML 2014-43; OML 2013-195. 
Therefore, we recommend that, if the Committee typically uses microphones during its meeting, 
it do so during this portion of the meeting as well so as not to cast doubt on the validity of any 
action taken during an executive session.

V. The Committee Did Not Violate the Open Meeting Law by Restricting the
Complainant from Attending the December 6, 2018, Executive Session Meeting.
The fourth complaint alleges that the Committee did not allow the complainant, as 
attorney for Kevin Mackin, to attend the December 6, 2018, executive session meeting. 
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public body 
within 30 days of the alleged violation. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b). Any complaint concerning your 
attendance at the December 6, 2018 meeting should have been filed with the Committee within 
30 days, or by January 5, 2019. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b). Because the complaint was filed with the 
Committee on January 9, 2019, we find that the complaint is untimely and we decline to review 
it. See OML Declination 2-1-17 (West Bridgewater Board of Water Commissioners); OML 
Declination 11-21-16 (Middleton Planning Board); OML Declination 12-15-14 (Ware 
Conservation Board).
Regardless, we find no violation of the Open Meeting Law. Members of the public have 
the right to attend any open session meeting of a public body, as well as to see and hear what is 
being discussed. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a); OML 2015-38; OML 2014-3. Although all executive 
sessions must be preceded by an open session so that the public has an opportunity to attend and 
learn the reasons for the executive session, the executive session meeting itself is entirely closed 
to the public, offering no ability to witness the proceedings. See G.L. c. 30A, § 18. Only if a 
public body meets in executive session under Purpose 1 does the individual to be discussed 
during such an executive session have the right to be present during deliberations, to have 
counsel present, and to speak on his own behalf. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1). Here, the 
Committee met in executive session under Purpose 2 and Purpose 3 on December 6, 2018. As 
such, whether or not the complainant was permitted to participate in the executive session was a 
matter at the discretion of the Committee and its decision to restrict the complainant’s attendance 
at the December 6, 2018, executive session meeting was not a violation of the Open Meeting 
Law. See OML 2013-50.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting 
Law by posting insufficiently detailed notices for its December 6, 2018; December 20, 2018; and 
January 17, 2019, meetings and meeting in executive session for an improper purpose on 
December 20, 2018. We order immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, 
and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the 
law. Additionally, we order the Committee to publicly release its December 20, 2018, executive 
session meeting minutes.4
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions
