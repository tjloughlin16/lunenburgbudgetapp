This office received a complaint from Christopher Kelleher on December 18, 2024, a 
second complaint on January 6, 2025, and a third complaint on January 23, 2025, alleging that 
the Brookfield Conservation Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The first complaint was originally filed with the Commission on or about 
September 27, 2024, and the Commission responded to the complaint by email dated October 8, 
2024.  The second complaint was originally filed with the Commission on or about October 11, 
2024, and the Commission responded to the complaint by email dated October 24, 2024.  The 
third complaint was originally filed with the Commission on or about December 3, 2024, and the 
Commission responded to the complaint by email dated January 23, 2025.  All three complaints 
allege that the Commission failed to timely respond to Open Meeting Law complaints.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law in 
the way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaints, the 
Commission’s responses to the complaints, and the complaints filed with our office requesting 
further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notices and minutes of Commission meetings held 
on August 28, September 19, and December 18, 2024.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that within 14 business days of receipt of a complaint, 
the public body review the complaint’s allegations; take remedial action, if appropriate; and send 
to the Attorney General a copy of the complaint and a description of any remedial action taken.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  A public body must meet to review the complaint and

formulate a response, or meet to delegate that authority, and respond to the complaint within 14 
business days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); OML 2022-43; OML 2017-69; OML 2012-90.1   
 
The complaints allege that the Commission failed to timely respond to eight previous 
Open Meeting Law complaints, which complaints were filed on or about August 16, August 21, 
August 29 (two complaints), September 5, October 25 (two complaints), and November 1, 2024.  
The Commission explains that it reviewed the August 16 and August 21, 2024, complaints 
during a meeting held on August 28, 2024, and then reviewed the two August 29, 2024, 
complaints and September 5, 2024, complaint during a meeting held on September 19, 2024. 
However, the Commission did not respond to any of these five complaints until September 30, 
2024 - between 17 and 30 business days after the complaints were filed.  Even though the 
Commission reviewed the five complaints within 14 business days, it must also respond to the 
complaints within that timeframe, unless it secures an extension from the Director of the 
Division of Open Government.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  Because the 
Commission failed to respond to the first five Open Meeting Law complaints within 14 business 
days, we find that it violated the Open Meeting Law.    
 
In addition, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law when it 
reviewed the two October 25, 2024, complaints during a meeting held on December 18, 2024, 
but did not respond to these two complaints until January 6, 2025 - 48 business days after the 
complaints were filed.  We acknowledge that the Commission timely requested an extension 
until November 21, 2024, to respond to these two October complaints, as it planned to discuss 
the complaints during a meeting scheduled for November 20, 2024.  However, the November 20, 
2024, meeting was cancelled, and the Commission did not request a further extension.  As such, 
we find that the Commission failed to review and respond to these two complaints within the 
timeframe required by the Open Meeting Law. 
 
Moreover, we are unable to find any evidence that the Commission reviewed the 
Novem
ber 1, 2024, complaint during a meeting prior to the Chair’s response on January 6, 2025.  
Proper procedure requires that a public body review a complaint before authorizing another 
individual, such as the chair, to respond on its behalf.  See OML 2017-132; OML 2012- 95; 
OML 2011-6.  We note that once a public body has reviewed a complaint during a meeting, its 
decision to simply refer the complaint, rather than discuss its substance, is the public body’s 
prerogative.  See OML 2019-40; OML 2017-96.  We find that the Commission violated the 
Open Meeting Law when it did not first meet to review the November 1, 2024, complaint before 
the chair responded.  See OML 2021-185; OML 2013-173.  
 
To the extent the complainant asserts that he requested, and was not provided with, 
minutes, we find that this allegation was not clearly raised in the complaints and thus we do not 
review its merits.  Here, the complainant states that the Commission “withheld from the public” 
the minutes of the meeting held on June 11, 2024.  It is not clear that the complainant requested 
the minutes from the Commission; rather, the complaint suggests that the Commission 
“withheld” the minutes by approving them during a meeting held on August 15, 2024, but not 
posting them to the municipal website until September 4, 2024.  The Open Law Meeting does

Finally, with respect to the complainant’s concerns that the Commission engaged in 
“egr
egious and unprofessional” conduct by mocking him and refusing to allow him to speak 
during meetings, we decline to find a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  The Open Meeting 
Law does not require that a public body allow public participation in its meetings, but rather 
provides that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body without permission of the 
chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”  G.L. c. 30A § 20(f); see also 
OML 2021-27; OML 2020-161; OML 2017-189; OML 2014-23; OML 2012-78.  If public 
participation will be permitted, the chair may determine the method for receiving public 
comment, who may speak or otherwise participate during a meeting, and may set limitations on 
participation.  See OML 2020-114; OML 2017-189.  We do, however, encourage public bodies 
to allow for as much public participation as time and circumstances permit.  See OML 2015-12; 
OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59.  Nonetheless, even assuming this allegation were true, it would 
not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  Moreover, an allegation of unprofessional 
conduct or incivility towards members of the public does not constitute a violation of the Open 
Meeting Law.  See OML Declination 6-14-16 (Sterling Board of Selectmen); OML Declination 
1-25-16 (Nahant Housing Authority Board of Commissioners).  Nevertheless, our office 
encourages civility during meetings of public bodies.  
 
For t
he reasons stated above, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to timely respond to Open Meeting Law complaints.  We order the Commission’s 
immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements and caution the Commission that 
similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law and may result 
in a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation. 
 
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Commission.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
