RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney D’Ortenzio: 
 
This office received a complaint from Alfonso and Maureen Longobardi on April 30, 
2025, alleging that the Middlefield Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on April 2, 2025, and 
you responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated April 18, 2025.  Specifically, the 
complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely respond to a request for minutes of a meeting 
held on March 19, 2025. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s 
response to the complaint, the complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the 
minutes of the March 19, 2025, meeting.  In addition, we communicated with you on August 19 
and September 8, 2025. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The minutes of an 
open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon 
request by any person within 10 days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  If minutes do not yet exist at the 
time of a request, the public body is still required to respond to the request within 10 days with

an explanation of whether the minutes do or do not exist in either approved or draft form.  See 
OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; OML 2015-173.1 
 
On March 21, 2025, the complainants sent an email to Mark Doane, the Board’s 
Administrative Assistant, requesting a copy of the minutes of the Board meeting held on March 
19, 2025.  Mr. Doane responded two hours later stating, “I will when I have completed them.”   
 
Requests for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law or the Open 
Mee
ting Law, depending on how the request is made, and the requirements for responding differ. 
A request for minutes that is directed to a clerk or other municipal employee as Records Access 
Officer will likely fall under the Public Records Law and requires a response in 10 business 
days.  See G.L. c. 66, § 10; OML 2019-126; OML 2019-68.  A request for meeting minutes 
made directly to the public body falls under the Open Meeting Law and requires a response 
within 10 calendar days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2); OML 2019-125.  Here, we find that the 
request fell under the Open Meeting Law where the request was sent to the Board’s 
administrative assistant and was thus effectively sent to the Board.  See OML 2019-145 (finding 
that public body failed to timely respond to a request for meeting minutes that was sent to body’s 
administrative assistant); OML 2018-126 (same).  
 
Because we find that the request fell under the Open Meeting, the Board was required to  
res
pond within 10 calendar days.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (g); OML 2019-163.  Here, Mr. 
Doane responded to the March 21, 2025, request for minutes within 10 days explaining that he 
would provide the minutes “when I have completed them.”  Based on our communications with 
legal counsel, we understand that Mr. Doane takes shorthand notes for each meeting which are 
unlikely to be understood by anyone but him.  He then uses the shorthand notes to create draft 
minutes.  At the time of the complainant’s request for the March 19, 2025, meeting minutes, only 
his shorthand notes existed.  Although we have found that in some circumstances handwritten 
notes can be considered draft minutes, we have found that notes in shorthand are not draft 
minutes.  See OML 2018-117; OML 2014-95; OML 2013-131.  As such, we find that draft 
minutes of the March 19, 2025, minutes did not yet exist at the time of the request and therefore 
the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when Mr. Doane responded within two hours of 
receiving the request without providing copies of minutes.  Although we do not find a violation, 
we encourage the Board to provide a more unequivocal explanation of whether minutes do or do 
not exist in either approved or draft form.  See OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; OML 2015-173.  
We understand that the complainants have since received the minutes of the March 19, 2025, 
meeting.  
 
With respect to the allegations in the complaint that the Board does not allow the public 
to subm
it questions during meetings, we do not find a violation.  The Open Meeting Law states 
that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body without permission of the chair, and 
all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(g).  The Law does not 
require that a public body allow public participation; rather, the Law gives the chair of the public 
body discretion to decide whether to permit public comment during a meeting, and if public 
comment will be permitted, to set restrictions on public comment.  See OML 2020-9; OML

 reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting
