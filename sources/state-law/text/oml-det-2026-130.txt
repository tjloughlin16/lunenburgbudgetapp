 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Waugh: 
 
This office received a complaint from Dawn Byers on January 27, 2026, alleging that the 
Whitman-Hanson School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on November 18, 2025, 
and you responded on behalf of the Committee by letter dated December 9, 2025.  In her 
complaint, Ms. Byers alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law (1) by failing 
to review executive session minutes in response to a request for same; and (2) by failing to 
review the minutes of executive sessions at reasonable intervals to determine whether continued 
non-disclosure was warranted. 
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter.  Following our 
review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to review 
executive session minutes at reasonable intervals.  We decline to review the remaining 
allegation.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Committee’s 
response, and the request for further review filed with our office. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Committee failed to review minutes of executive sessions at reasonable 
intervals. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body 
===PAGE 2===

“create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 22(a).  The minutes of an executive session may be withheld from disclosure to the public 
in their entirety as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, 
but no longer.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).  A public body or its chair or designee must, at reasonable 
intervals, review the minutes of executive sessions to determine if continued nondisclosure of 
those minutes is warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1).  At the meeting following each periodic 
review, the public body must announce which executive session minutes will be released and 
which will continue to be withheld, and such announcement must be included in the minutes.  Id.   
A public body’s obligation to review executive session minutes for possible release is ongoing.  
If a public body reviews executive session minutes and determines that the purpose for the 
executive session remains such that the minutes should continue to be withheld from the public, 
it must continue to review those same executive session minutes at reasonable intervals until it 
determines that the reason for secrecy has expired.  See OML 2026-91; OML 2022-155; OML 
2021-129.0F
1  Although “reasonable interval” is not defined by the Law, our office has found a 
quarterly review or a review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2025-63; OML 
2020-101; OML 2017-104. 
 
Prior to the filing of the complaint, the Committee last reviewed executive session 
minutes on May 7, 2025.  As of the date that the complaint was filed with the Committee, 
November 18, 2025, more than six months had passed since the last review.  Because more than 
six months had passed, we find that the Committee failed to review executive session minutes at 
reasonable intervals, and that in failing to conduct this periodic review, the Committee violated 
the Open Meeting Law. 
 
Having found a violation, we must determine whether the Committee’s violation was, as 
the complainant urges, an intentional one.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is 
an “act or omission by a public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open 
Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An intentional violation may be found where the public body 
acted with deliberate ignorance of the Law’s requirement or has previously been advised that 
certain conduct violates the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  The complainant argues that the violation 
was intentional because she could not locate any executive session minutes on the School 
District website, which she cites as evidence of a “longstanding pattern of avoiding statutory 
obligations.”  We note that the Open Meeting Law does not require the posting online of open 
session or executive session minutes.  Moreover, even if the Committee had repeatedly failed to 
conduct a periodic review of executive session minutes, there is no evidence before us that it has 
been warned of this violation or that it did so in knowing violation of the Law.  For these 
reasons, we decline to find the violation intentional. 
 
II. We decline to review the remaining allegation of the complaint. 
 
The complaint also alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to review executive session minutes in response to her request for same.  A request for meeting 
minutes may fall under the Public Records Law or the Open Meeting Law, depending on how

When filing the complaint with our office for review, the complainant also alleged that 
notices of the Committee’s meetings were insufficiently specific.  A public body must post 
notice of every meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal 
holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  Id.  The list of topics must have 
sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting. 
940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We decline to review the complainant’s allegations regarding the 
sufficiency of meeting notices because those allegations were not raised in the original complaint 
first filed with the Committee.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(7); OML 2024-204; 
OML 2019-76; OML 2012-92. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to review executive session minutes at reasonable intervals.  We order the 
Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law.  Additionally, we 
order the Committee to review for possible release all unreleased executive session minutes that 
have not been reviewed within the last six months.
