 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Vasudevan: 
 
This office received a complaint from Doreen Taylor on January 6, 2022, alleging that 
the Seekonk School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on December 14, 2021, and 
you responded, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated January 4, 2022.1 In her complaint, 
Ms. Taylor alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by i) failing to properly 
post notice of the meeting to be held on December 6; ii) removing from this notice an 
opportunity for public comment; and (iii) failing to post meeting minutes on the Committee’s 
website.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law 
in the ways alleged. We resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 940 CMR 
29.07(1) after reviewing the original complaint, the Committee’s response to the complaint, the 
complainant’s request for further review, and the original and revised notices of the Committee 
meeting held on December 6, as well as the minutes of the December 6 meeting.  
 
Except in the case of an emergency, the Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies 
post notice of every meeting “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, 
Sundays and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). “Notice shall be printed in a legible, easily 
understandable format and shall contain the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of 
topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” Id.   
 
On Wednesday, December 1 around 3:00 P.M., the Committee posted notice of a meeting 
to be held on Monday, December 6 at 7:00 P.M. The notice listed ten items. Two of the listed 
items were “Public Comments,” one at the beginning of the meeting and the second at the end of

the meeting. The next day, on December 2 at 12:30 P.M., the Committee posted an amended 
notice for the December 6 meeting. This amended notice included nine items as the Committee 
removed the “Public Comments” that had been listed for the beginning of the meeting.  
 
The complaint alleges that notice for the Committee meeting held on December 6 was not 
properly posted. For local public bodies, notice shall be filed with the municipal clerk and posted 
in a manner conspicuously visible to the public at all hours in or on the municipal building in 
which the clerk’s office is located. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(c). A municipality may designate a website 
as an alternative notice posting location for all public bodies within the municipality. 940 CMR 
29.03(2)(b). The Town of Seekonk has not adopted its website as the official method of posting 
notice. Rather, the official method of posting notice for Seekonk public bodies is a town bulletin 
board located at Town Hall. Although the Open Meeting Law and the Attorney General’s 
regulations do not prohibit a public body from posting meeting notices in multiple locations, a 
municipality may only have one official notice posting location. See 940 CMR 29.03(2)(c); 
OML 2021-94; OML 2021-63; OML 2020-20. We find that the notice for the December 6 
meeting was properly posted in compliance with the Open Meeting Law where the Town Clerk 
posted both the original and amended notices more than 48 hours in advance in the official notice 
posting location, i.e. the bulletin board at Town Hall. There is no requirement that a meeting 
notice also be posted to either the Town or Committee website.   
  
The complaint further alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
amending the notice of the December 6 meeting to remove one of the public comment periods.2  
Here, the Committee removed one of the two Public Comment periods listed on the notice for 
the December 6 meeting. Although a public body may not add anticipated topics to a notice 
within 48 hours of a meeting, the law does not prohibit a public body from removing, 
postponing, or declining to discuss a topic listed on a meeting notice. See OML 2015-174; OML 
2012-23. Moreover, while the Open Meeting Law requires that the chair list on the meeting 
notice all topics anticipated for discussion 48 hours before the meeting, a public comment period 
is not itself a topic for the public body’s discussion that needs to be listed on the meeting notice. 
See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); OML 2022-45. Nor does the law require that a public body allow 
public participation at all, but rather gives the chair of the public body discretion to decide 
whether to permit public comment during a meeting. See OML 2017-189; OML 2014-23; OML 
2012-78; OML 2020-77. Nevertheless, the Committee allowed public participation towards the 
end of its meeting after it discussed the open session topics and heard from three individuals, 
including the complainant. As such, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.    
 
 Finally, with respect to the complaint’s allegation that the Committee failed to post 
meeting minutes to the Committee’s website, the Open Law Meeting does not require that public 
bodies post their minutes to a website or elsewhere, but only that minutes be created, maintained,

Meeting Law. We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.
