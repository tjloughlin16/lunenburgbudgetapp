RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints 
 
Dear Attorney Weinberger: 
 
This office received two complaints from Tanya Gailus on January 31, 2025, alleging 
that the Minuteman Advisory Group on Interlocal Communication (the “Group”) violated the 
Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The first complaint was originally filed with the 
Group on or about December 26, 2024, and the second complaint was originally filed with the 
Group on or about January 3, 2025.  You responded to the complaints, on behalf of the Group, 
by separate emails on January 2 and January 3, 2025, respectively.  The complaints allege that 
the Group failed to post notice of meetings held in November and December 2024.   
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed these matters.  Following 
our review, we find that the Group violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to post notice for a 
meeting held on December 19, 2024.  We find that the Group did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original 
complaints, the Group’s responses to the complaints, the complaints filed with our office 
requesting further review, the supplemental material attached to the requests for review, and the 
Group’s response, dated November 18, 2025, to this office’s request for additional information.  
In addition, we reviewed the notices of Group meetings held on September 3, 2024, November 5, 
2024, December 3, 2024, and December 19, 2024.  Finally, we communicated with you between 
October 28, 2025, and February 3, 2026, and with the complainant on November 21, 2025.

By way of background, the Metropolitan Area Planning Council (MAPC) is the regional 
planning organization for 101 communities in the greater Boston area.  The Group is one of eight 
subregional committees that represent the MAPC communities of Acton, Bedford, Bolton, 
Boxborough, Carlisle, Concord, Hudson, Lexington, Lincoln, Littleton, Maynard, Stow, and 
Sudbury.  
 
Initially, the Group responded to the complaints stating that it was not a public body.  The 
Open Meeting Law defines a “public body” as any “multiple-member board, commission, 
committee or subcommittee within the executive or legislative branch or within any county, 
district, city, region or town, however created, elected, appointed or otherwise constituted, 
established to serve a public purpose.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  Generally, to determine whether a 
multiple-member entity is a public body subject to the Open Meeting Law, there are three factors 
we consider: 1) the entity must be “within” government and not excluded from the definition of 
“public body;” 2) the entity must be a “body” empowered to act collectively; and 3) the entity 
must serve a “public purpose.”  See OML 2017-118; OML 2016-102; OML 2012-20; OML 
2011-42; OML 2010-1.1  After discussions with our office and consideration of the three factors, 
the Group now acknowledges that it is a public body subject to the Open Meeting Law.   
 
Because the Group acknowledges that it is a public body, we must then determine 
whether it violated the Open Meeting Law.  Specifically, the complaints allege that the Group 
failed to post notice of meetings held in “early November 2024,” on December 3, 2024, and 
December 19, 2024.  The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every 
meeting “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal 
holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Every notice must be printed in a legible, easily understandable 
format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a listing of topics that 
the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are 
required to list topics on a notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of 
the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally consider a topic 
sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand 
the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2019-163; OML 2015-35; 
OML 2013-39.

along the Greenway . . . .”3  Following our review, we are unable to formally find that the Group 
held a meeting in early November for which it failed to post notice.  When a public body does 
not actually hold a meeting, it cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with regard to the 
sufficiency and posting of its meeting notice.  See OML 2022-102.  Accordingly, we are 
constrained to find that the Group did not violate the Open Meeting Law in this regard.  See 
OML 2018-50.   
 
Nonetheless, we take seriously this allegation raised by the complainant and remind the 
Group that the Open Meeting Law defines “meeting,” in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a 
public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The 
Open Meeting Law’s definition of “meeting” excludes “attendance by a quorum of a public body 
at a public or private gathering, including a conference or training program or a media, social or 
other event, so long as the members do not deliberate.”  The Law defines “deliberation” as “an 
oral or written communication through any medium, including electronic mail, between or 
among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction; provided, 
however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of other procedural meeting [sic] or 
the distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided than no 
opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” 
is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id.    
 
Second, with respect to the Group meeting held on December 3, 2024, the complainant 
states that when she checked the MAPC website on December 14, 2024, only the date of the 
December 3, 2024, meeting was posted.  The Open Meeting Law requires that notices contain 
the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a listing of topics that the chair reasonably 
anticipates will be discussed.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  For regional public bodies, notice shall be 
filed and posted in each city or town within the region in the manner prescribed for local public 
bodies in that city or town.  See 940 CMR 29.03(3)(a).  As an alternative, the regional public 
body may, by majority vote, adopt the regional public body’s website as its official notice 
posting method.  See 940 CMR 29.03(3)(b).  MAPC has adopted its website, 
http://www.mapc.org/calendar/, as the official method of posting notice and the Group posts its 
notices to this website.   
 
We note that a notice is currently posted on MAPC’s website for a December 3, 2024, 
Group meeting, and that notice includes the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a list 
of topics.

for its December 3, 2024, meeting.  We do, however, find that the Group violated the Open 
Meeting Law by failing to record the date and time that the notice was posted.  The Open 
Meeting Law requires that “the date and time that the notice is posted shall be conspicuously 
recorded thereon or therewith.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(d); see OML 2011-45.  The date and time of 
posting may be listed either on the meeting notice itself or on the website next to the link to the 
meeting notice.  See OML 2019-102; OML 2018-53.  Recording the date and time enables the 
public to observe that public bodies are complying with the Open Meeting Law’s notice 
requirements and further provides this office with evidence of compliance with the law.  See 
OML 2013-44.  As evidenced here, the failure to record the date and time of posting impeded 
our and the public’s ability to determine whether the notice was timely posted and underscores 
the importance of this requirement.   
 
Finally, with respect to the meeting held on December 19, 2024, the Group acknowledges 
that it failed to post notice of this meeting.  Here, the Group posted only the date of the 
December 19, 2024, meeting to the MAPC website and did not include the time or place of the 
meeting or a list of topics that the Group planned to discuss during the meeting.  We therefore 
find that the Group violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to post notice of the meeting held 
on December 19, 2024.  
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Group violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to record the date and time that the notice was posted for the meeting held on December 
3, 2024, and by failing to post notice of the meeting held on December 19, 2024.  We order 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and caution that future similar 
violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
 
In addition, we order the Group, within 60 days of the date of this letter, to create 
minutes of the meeting held on December 19, 2024, and to certify to our office that it has done 
so.  The Group must create the minutes to the best of its ability, through whatever means are 
available, including documents, and the notes and recollections of the attendees.  See, e.g., OML 
2025-27 (ordering Monson American Rescue Plan Act Committee to create and approve minutes 
for meetings held over a period of approximately three years).  To the extent that the Group has 
not created or approved minutes of the meeting held on December 3, 2024, we also order the 
Group to do so within 60 days of the date of this letter. 
 
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Group or with 
our office.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
