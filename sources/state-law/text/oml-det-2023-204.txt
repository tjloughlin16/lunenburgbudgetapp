RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Randazzo: 
 
This office received a complaint from Nicole delRosario on June 2, 2023, alleging that 
the Manchester-By-The-Sea Parks and Recreation Committee (the “Committee”) violated the 
Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the 
Committee on or about March 28, 2023, and you responded, on behalf of the Committee, by 
letter dated April 20, 2023.  The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to timely respond to 
a request for meeting minutes, and created insufficient minutes of meetings held on October 19, 
2022, November 16, 2022, and December 14, 2022.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by not 
responding to a request for minutes in a timely manner.  We find that the Committee did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law in the other way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaint, the Committee’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review, and the minutes of the Committee meetings held on 
October 19, 2022, November 16, 2022, December 14, 2022, and March 15, 2023.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The minutes of an 
open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon 
request by any person within 10 days.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  If such minutes do not yet exist at 
the time of a request, the public body is still required to respond to the request within 10 days

On March 1, 2023, the complainant requested copies of minutes of the Committee 
mee
ting held on February 8, 2023.  The requested minutes were not provided to the complainant 
until after the minutes were approved during the Committee meeting held on March 15, 2023.  
The Committee acknowledges that draft minutes should have been provided to the complainant 
within 10 days of the request.  Because the Committee failed to provide a copy of the draft 
minutes of the February 8, 2023, meeting within 10 days of the request, we find that it violated 
the Open Meeting Law.  We order no further remedial action as the Committee has since 
provided a copy of the minutes to the complainant and posted the minutes to its website.   
 
With respect to the complainant’s concern that the Committee did not respond to her 
request for a video recording of the February 8, 2023, meeting, the Open Meeting Law does not 
govern a public body’s response to a request for records other than meeting minutes.  See G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 22(c), (e); OML Declination 8-19-13 (Southwick Board of Selectmen).  Therefore, 
failure to timely respond to requests for records other than minutes concerns a potential violation 
of the Public Records Law, rather than the Open Meeting Law. 
 
The complaint also alleges that the minutes of meetings held on October 19, 2022, 
Novem
ber 16, 2022, and December 14, 2022, are insufficient.  The Open Meeting Law requires 
that a public body “create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive 
sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a summary of the 
discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the 
decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for 
substantial compliance with the accuracy requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.  By 
substantial compliance, we mean that the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so 
that a member of the public who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a 
clear understanding of what occurred.  See OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a 
summary of the discussion on each topic, a transcript is not required, and the minutes do not 
need to include every remark or opinion presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55.   
 
Specifically, the complaint alleges that the minutes of the October, November and 
December meetings included “no documentation of votes, no documentation of requests I made” 
and failed to “document repeated complaints of noise nuisance from pickle ball and player 
harassment by me and others.”  We have reviewed the minutes of the meetings held on October 
19, 2022, November 16, 2022, and December 14, 2022, and note that the minutes of these three 
meetings include the date, time and place of the meeting, the members present and absent, and a 
summary of the discussion on each topic, as well as the decisions made and actions taken.

 We also note that the minutes of the meetings held on November 16, 2022, and 
Dec
ember 14, 2022, indicate that previous minutes were reviewed and approved, with no record 
of any vote.  The Open Meeting Law does not govern the method for approving minutes.  As 
such, public bodies may choose the method for approving their minutes, including approval by 
the public body chair alone, by majority vote of the public body, or by consent of the body 
(approved unless there is an objection).  We remind the Committee that if members actually 
voted to approve the minutes, a record of that vote must be included in the minutes, even if that 
vote was unanimous.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). 
 
Finally, we review the sufficiency of the October 19, 2022, November 16, 2022, and 
December 14, 2022, meeting minutes with respect to the comments made by the complainant 
during the three meetings.  Recordings of these three meetings are unavailable and thus we are 
unable to independently ascertain what exactly was said by the complainant during each meeting.  
Nevertheless, a public body is not required to capture every comment made during a meeting in 
order to substantially comply with the accuracy requirement of the Open Meeting Law.  See 
OML 2021-4; OML 2019-136.  As such, we find that the minutes capture a concise summary of 
the complainant’s comments even though they do not capture her exact statements.  See OML 
2017-104; OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55 (while minutes must include a summary of the 
discussion on each topic, a transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include 
every remark or opinion presented).   
 
We take this opportunity to advise the Committee of the Attorney General’s 
rec
ommendations as to best practices for drafting meeting minutes.  In particular, when 
summarizing discussions, we encourage public bodies to include dissenting or minority opinions 
whenever possible.  Where significant debate on a matter occurs and it is clear that one or more 
members of the body and/or the public disagree with the decision ultimately taken by the public 
body, public bodies should note the opposing viewpoints in the meeting minutes.  Although it is 
not necessary to record the comments of every speaker, and we acknowledge the difficulty of 
doing so where there are many speakers whose remarks may even overlap, if a particular 
individual speaks at some length or is the only one to offer an argument for or against a proposal, 
that person and his or her comments should be identified in the minutes.  See OML 2014-108; 
OML 2013-47; OML 2012-29.   
 
For t
he reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to timely respond to a request for meeting minutes.  We order immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
