your response, and the complaint filed with our office, requesting further review. We also

FACTS 
We find the facts as follows. On April 26, 2016, the Committee approved certain 
executive session minutes concerning discussions it held in 2015 about negotiations with 
paraprofessionals.2 Those minutes summarized the paraprofessional negotiation 
discussions as "Due to the nature of this topic and being employed as a paraprofessional in 
the SPS, the Recording Secretary has excused herself from the meeting at this time." The 
complainant, who is a Committee member, disagreed with the sufficiency of the minutes 
and thus filed these complaints. In response, the Committee agreed with the complainant 
that the minutes were incomplete due to the fact that the Committee's recording secretary 
had to leave the executive session discussion because she was a member of the 
paraprofessionals collective bargaining unit. The Committee amended the minutes to 
include specific information concerning the discussions based on Committee Chair Katie 
Pina-Enokian's notes from the meetings. 
With regard to the accuracy of the Committee's July 28, 2015 executive session 
minutes, the Committee amended the section which began, "Mr. Soares asked for 
clarification on the issue" by adding that Attorney Emerson had replied to Mr. Soares' 
request for clarification during the meeting. The Committee also added the December 28, 
2014 meeting minutes as a document used during that meeting. 
DISCUSSION 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body "create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, 
the members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of 
documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken 
at each meeting, including the record of all votes." G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). These meeting 
minutes must be reviewed and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). 
Although the Open Meeting Law does not define "timely manner," we recommend that 
minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting, whenever possible. See OML 
2014-15; OML 2012-91.3 For bodies that meet weekly or bi-monthly, we have concluded 
that a delay of two to three months in approving minutes does not comply with the law. 
See OML 2016-62. 
The complainant alleges that the minutes from executive sessions held July 28, 
2015, August 25, 2015, September 8, 2015, and September 22, 2015 failed to include 
sufficient detail and were not approved in a timely manner. The minutes, as originally 
approved seven months or more later, failed to include any detail about the discussion 
concerning the paraprofessionals due to the fact that the minutes-taker, who was a member

find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to include any detail in 
its executive session minutes about those discussions. 
The complainant also alleges that the Committee's July 28, 2015 executive session 
minutes failed to include a list of documents used during the meeting and failed to contain 
sufficient detail about the complainant's request for clarification on a topic. In response, 
the Committee added clarification to the minutes concerning the complainant's request and 
added a "List of Documents/Exhibits" section at the end of the minutes. Accordingly, the 
Committee violated the law by failing to include sufficient detail in those minutes and by 
failing include the requisite list of documents used during that meeting. Additionally, by 
waiting at least seven months, the Committee failed to approve all four sets of minutes in a 
timely manner. See OML 2016-122; OML 2016-57. Even if the Board believed that the 
executive session purpose warranted continued non-disclosure, the Board was obligated to 
approve final minutes in a timely manner. See OML 2012-91. We therefore find that the 
Committee violated the law. 
We commend the Committee for promptly revising the minutes in response to these 
complaints. Because of the Committee's prompt remedial action, we decline to find that 
the violation was intentional.4 See OML 2016-148. Where, as occurred here, a public 
body takes corrective action in response to a complaint, we strongly encourage 
complainants to consider whether filing a complaint with our office is necessary. See 
OML 2016-37. 
CONCLUSION 
We find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to include 
sufficient detail in its executive session minutes and by failing to timely approve executive 
session minutes. We order the Committee's immediate and future compliance with the 
law's requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be considered 
evidence of intent to violate the law. 
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.
