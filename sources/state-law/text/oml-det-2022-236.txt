This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on October 26, 2022, alleging that 
the Rehoboth Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about October 6, 2022, and you 
responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by a letter dated October 25, 2022. The 
complaint alleges that the Board: (1) failed to timely approve the minutes of meetings held on 
August 8, 2022, and August 22, 2022; (2) failed to list on the notice for the October 3, 2022, 
meeting, candidates to be considered for approval or appointment; (3) and failed to list on the 
notice for the October 3, 2022, meeting, the date for the Rehoboth People Have Heart event.   
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to timely approve meeting minutes and by posting an insufficiently specific notice for its October 
3, 2022, meeting by failing to list the names of candidates to be considered for approval or 
appointment. We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in other ways 
alleged. In reaching this determination we reviewed the original Open Meeting Law complaint, 
the Board’s response, the request for further review, as well as the October 3, 2022, meeting 
notice, and minutes of the meetings that took place on August 8, 2022, and August 22, 2022.
 
Additionally, we communicated over the telephone with Board counsel1 on December 7, 2022. 2

I. The Board Failed to Timely Approve Meeting Minutes. 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting minutes must be reviewed 
and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). The Open Meeting Law itself does not 
define “timely manner.” However, the Attorney General's Open Meeting Law regulations 
provide that “timely manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 
days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.” 940 
C.M.R. 29.11(2); OML 2018-48.

The Board was required to approve the minutes of the meetings held on August 8 and 
August 22 within 30 days, as the Board met at least three times within the 30 days following 
each meeting, respectively, so that was the later time period for these minutes. See 940 C.M.R. 
29.11(2); OML 2018-152. In its response, the Board represented that it approved those minutes 
during its October 3 meeting. Board counsel also acknowledged during a telephone conversation 
that the October 3 meeting minutes have not been drafted, and there are meeting minutes from 
September, October, and November that remain outstanding. Because the Board did not approve 
the August 8 and August 22 meeting minutes until October 3, 56 days after its August 8 meeting, 
and 42 days after its August 22 meeting, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to approve these minutes in a timely manner.  
As we previously reminded the Board, the responsibility for complying with the Open 
Meet
ing Law rests with public bodies and members of such bodies, not with individual public 
employees, such as administrative staff. See OML 2018-105. We understand the demands on a 
public body with limited staff; however, the Board is still obligated to timely approve minutes. 
See OML 2018-111; OML 2017-115. If staff resources are not available to create minutes for the 
Board to approve, then Board members must take on this responsibility to ensure compliance 
with the law. See OML 2017-115. 
Next, we must determine whether the violations are intentional, as the complainant urges. 
See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c). An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body or a 
member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].” 940 CMR 29.02. An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
Law’s requirements or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open 
Meeting Law. Id. We have previously found that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in 
this regard. On December 8, 2021, our office found that the Board violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to approve minutes of several meetings in a timely manner. See OML 2021-181. 
On March 30 and April 13, we again found that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely approve other meeting minutes. See OML 2022-63; OML 2022-83. However, 
we did not find an intentional violation in those instances because the minutes at issue in our 
March 30 and April 13 determinations were already untimely by the time we issued our

determination OML 2021-181, and therefore, the Board did not have the benefit of our guidance.

The Board had the benefit of three determinations, two of which were issued this year, 
from our office on this issue by the time the August 8 and August 22 meetings were held. In our 
December 8, 2021, determination, we specifically cautioned the Board that “a similar violation in 
the future may be considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law.” OML 2021-
181. In our March 30 and April 13 determinations, we cautioned the same, and indicated that 
additional violations “may result in a recommendation of a civil penalty.” OML 2022-63; OML 
2022-83. In response to the latest complaint, the Board failed to show good cause for the delay in 
approving meeting minutes. See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2022-175. Therefore, we find that the 
Board’s failure to approve the August 8 and August 22 meeting minutes in a timely manner were 
intentional violations of the Open Meeting Law. Although we find an intentional violation and 
are very concerned by the Board’s failure to prioritize its obligation to timely approve meeting 
minutes, we decline to impose a civil penalty on the Board at this time. We recognize the 
challenges the Board has faced in securing reliable administrative support, yet again we 
emphasize that responsibility for creating and approving meeting minutes rests with the Board 
members themselves and not administrative staff. See OML 2018-105. We caution that a future 
finding of an intentional violation for failure to timely approve meeting minutes may result in a 
civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation. 
II. Notice for the October 3 Meeting was Insuf ficiently Specific Where It Failed to 
Identify Individuals for Approval and Appointment. 
The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to post notice 48 hours in advance of a 
meeting and include a “listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at 
the meeting.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Public bodies are required to list topics in a meeting notice 
with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). We generally consider a topic sufficiently detailed when a 
reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of 
the public body’s discussion. See OML 2015-35. The Open Meeting Law does not require a 
public body to anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only that it 
identify the discussion topic with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the 
issues to be discussed at the meeting. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(1)(b); OML 
2017-186; OML 2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38. The Open Meeting Law also does not 
require a public body to list each and every detail about a topic in order for the listing of topics to 
be sufficiently specific. See OML 2018-60. However, we have consistently stated that the names 
of interviewees and applicants for appointment which are known 48 hours in advance of a 
meeting should be included on a meeting notice. See OML 2019-24; OML 2016-150; OML 
2013-163. 
The Board posted notice for its October 3 meeting and included the following topics: 
“Ac
tion 1: Vote to Approve New Hire-Police Dispatch” and “Action 7: Vote to Appoint Board 
of Health Member.” It did not include the names of the candidates for approval or appointment. 
In its response, the Board stated that it voted to appoint Gary Zimmer to the Board of Health and 
approve Lindsay Strese as police dispatcher at this meeting. The Board has not claimed that the 
approval or appointment of the aforementioned individuals was not reasonably anticipated and 
concedes that the failure to list the names provided the public with insufficient notice. The Board

III. The Board’s No tice Was Sufficiently Specific as to the One-Day Liquor License 
for Rehoboth People Have Heart.  
The October 3 meeting notice also included the topic “Action 8: Vote to Approve One 
Day Liquor License for Rehoboth People Have a Heart, 61 Reed Street.” The complainant 
alleges that topic was insufficiently specific because it failed to list the date of the event. As 
discussed above, a topic is sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could 
read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion. See OML 
2015-35; see also OML 2019-76 (finding that the agenda item "Easter Egg Roll" was sufficiently 
specific to put the public on notice that the Commission was going to discuss an Easter egg 
event). Although listing additional detail may have been helpful to the public, we are satisfied 
that the topic as listed was sufficient to reasonably advise the public of the issue to be discussed 
at the meeting. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); see also OML 2013-152. As such, we do not find a 
violation of the Open Meeting Law in this instance. 
CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely approve minutes of its August 8 and August 22 meetings, and by posting an 
insufficiently specific notice for its October 3 meeting because it failed to list the names of 
candidates for approval and appointment. We find that the violation regarding timely approval of 
minutes was intentional for the reasons explained above, and we caution the Board that a 
determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may result in a civil penalty of up 
to $1000 per intentional violation. We order the Board to create and approve all outstanding 
meeting minutes within ninety (90) days of the Board’s receipt of this letter, and to certify to our 
office that it has done so. We also order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the 
Open Meeting Law.
