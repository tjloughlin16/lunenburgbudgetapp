RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Bouchard: 
 
This office received a complaint from Sandra Ann Peret on August 11, 2023, alleging 
that the Chicopee School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on June 1, 2023, and you 
responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated June 23, 2023, following 
an extension of time granted by this office.  The complaint alleges that the Chair of the 
Committee failed to state at the beginning of the executive session held on May 17, 2023, that no 
other person was present in his remote location. 
 
We resolve this matte r by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review, and the notices of the Board meetings held on May 17, 
2023, and June 21, 2023, as well as video recordings of these two meetings.1  Following our 
review, we find that the Chair violated the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.   
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
del
iberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of

Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened.  
See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for 
any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened 
in an open session, that a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive 
session, that the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and 
the chair has publicly announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of 
the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.

Here, the Committee held a meeting on May 17, 2023, at 7 P.M.  The Chair of the 
Committee, Mayor John Vieau, participated in the meeting remotely.  After discussing the open 
session topics, the Committee convened in executive session at approximately 8 P.M.  At 
approximately 8:54 P.M., while the Committee was still in executive session, Mayor Vieau 
conversed with an individual not visible to the other Committee members.  When asked whether 
someone else was present in his location, Mayor Vieau acknowledged that someone was present 
and asked whether that person needed to leave.  Mayor Vieau was advised that the person should 
leave.    
 
The Open Meeting Law regulations require that when a public body member participates 
rem
otely in an executive session, such member must “state at the start of any such session that no 
other person is present and/or able to hear the discussion at the remote location, unless presence 
of that person is approved by a simple majority vote of the public body.”  940 CMR 29.10 (7)(d).  
The Committee acknowledges that such a statement was not made during the executive session 
held on May 17, 2023.  As such, we find an individual violation of the Open Meeting Law by 
Mayor Vieau for failing to state at the beginning of the executive session that no other person 
was present in his remote location.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c) (Attorney General has authority to 
determine “whether the public body, 1 or more of the members, or both, are responsible” for a 
violation of the Open Meeting Law); see also OML 2020-136; OML 2011-37.  We commend the 
Committee’s commitment to ensure future compliance with the Law. 
 
We ord
er Mayor Vieau’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, 
remind the Committee of its obligation to comply with the law, and we caution that similar 
future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
