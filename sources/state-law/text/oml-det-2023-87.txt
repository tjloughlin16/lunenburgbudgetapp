FACTS 
 
On August 11 at 10:55 A.M., the Committee posted notice of a virtual meeting to be held 
on August 15 at 7:00 P.M.  The notice stated: “Possible quorum of the Finance Committee at the 
Select Board meeting Monday August 15, 2022” but did not include on the notice any specific 
topics for discussion.  On August 11 at 4:03 P.M., the Board posted notice of a virtual meeting to 
be held on August 15 and included the following topics: “Discussion: RE: Fall Town Meeting 
RE: $ Needs et al;” “Discussion: RE: Goal Setting RE: FY 24 & Beyond;” “Discussion: RE: 
Staff & Volunteer Appreciation Luncheon Update.”   
 
Also, on August 11 at 10:56 A.M., the Committee posted a separate notice of a meeting 
to be held on August 16 at 7:00 P.M.  The notice listed 18 topics, including “RFT’s,” “Possible 
fall STM discussion” and Accountant update.

The August 15 meeting of the Board was held as planned and a quorum of the Committee 
was present at this virtual meeting, although each Committee member accessed the meeting 
separately.  The Board discussed various items for a possible fall Special Town Meeting 
including three bylaw changes and several budgeting issues.  Specifically, the Interim Town 
Administrator advised the Board that “unanticipated expenses” had arisen requiring additional 
monies above what had been previously budgeted for in this fiscal year, including increasing the 
salary range for a new Town Administrator by $30,000, an additional $50,000 for improvements 
to Liberty Field, an additional $30,000 to purchase assessing software, and an additional $4,200 
to local cable access for broadcasting meetings, as well as several other expenses submitted by 
the Treasurer, library, and police and fire departments. 
 
Committee Chair Maria Neyland spoke four times during the August 15 meeting.  First, 
she asked the Interim Town Administrator whether the Town needs an additional $30,000 more 
for the purchase of software for the Assessing Department that was previously approved at the 
June 2021 Town Meeting.  Ten minutes later, Chair Neyland provided some history with respect 
to warrant articles explaining that if a specific amount of money was approved at town meeting 
any additional money requested would need to go back to town meeting for approval.  She 
explained that any requests for reserve fund transfers would be discussed by the Committee at its 
meeting the following night.  When Chair Neyland spoke again, she asked the Interim Town 
Administrator whether cable funds would cover the additional costs for broadcasting meetings.  
The last comment made by Chair Neyland was that the Town had budgeted for a fall Special 
Town Meeting.  No other member of the Committee spoke during the meeting.  
 
The August 16 meeting of the Committee was held as planned.  During the meeting, the 
Committee discussed the budget issues that the Interim Town Administrator had brought before 
the Board at the August 15 meeting the previous evening.  The Committee discussed whether 
certain expenses could be paid through reserve fund transfers, such as the $30,000 additional 
funds for a new Town Administrator, or needed to go back to town meeting for approval, such as 
the $50,000 for improvements to Liberty Field.  The Committee agreed that some expenses did

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
beration and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that “a public body shall 
post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, 
Sundays and legal holidays.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as 
“a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. 
c. 30A, § 18.  The Open Meeting Law excludes from the definition of “meeting,” however, the 
“attendance by a quorum of a public body at a meeting of another public body that has complied 
with the notice requirements of the open meeting law, so long as the visiting members 
communicate only by open participation in the meetings on those matters under discussion by the 
host body and do not deliberate.”  Id.   
 
“Deliberation” is defined as “an oral or written communication through any medium, 
incl
uding electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business 
within its jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of 
a meeting agenda, scheduling information or other procedural meeting [sic] or the distribution of 
reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided that no opinion of a member 
is expressed.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The Open Meeting Law does not carve out an exception to the 
definition of “deliberation” for discussions that do not result in a decision or vote.  Any 
communication among a quorum of a public body on matters that are pending or will come 
before it and within the public body’s jurisdiction is considered deliberation.  See OML 2015-3; 
OML 2014-108; OML 2013-136; Boelter v. Board of Selectmen of Wayland, 479 Mass. 233, 
243 (2018).  Moreover, the expression of an opinion of one public body member on matters 
within the body’s jurisdiction to a quorum of a public body is a deliberation, even if no other 
public body member responds.  See OML 2016-104; OML 2015-33.   
 
The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to post notice of a joint meeting held on 
August
 15 with the Board and, because a quorum of Committee members was present at this 
meeting, the Committee deliberated outside of a properly posted meeting when the Committee 
Chair spoke and asked questions.  The Committee timely posted notice of “a possible quorum” at 
the Board’s August 15 meeting.  The notice included the date, time and place of the meeting but 
did not list any topics that the chair reasonably anticipated the Committee would discuss.  The 
Open Meeting Law requires that every notice include “the date, time and place of such meeting 
and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  
G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are required to list topics on a notice with “sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 
CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We find that the Committee posted notice, but did not include any topics, and 
therefore we review the complaint as regarding the sufficiency of the notice rather than the 
failure to post any notice.  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly 
insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2015-109; 
OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if

the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the public aware of the discussion topics.  See OML 
2015-101; OML 2015-21.   
 
 Here, although the Committee admits that a quorum of members was present at the 
Boar
d meeting held on August 15, we find that the Committee did not discuss any topics during 
the meeting.  Because the Committee members did not deliberate, no meeting occurred for 
purposes of the Open Meeting Law and the notice therefore cannot be insufficient.  A quorum of 
a public body may attend the meeting of another public body without posting notice so long as 
the visiting public body members listen and participate as members of the audience and do not 
deliberate.  See OML 2020-18; OML 2012-6.  When determining whether a quorum of a public 
body has deliberated while attending the meeting of another public body, we consider whether 
the public body members in attendance appeared to direct their remarks primarily toward their 
fellow public body members in attendance, or whether the remarks were directed primarily at the 
public and the public body whose meeting was being attended.  See OML 2021-171; OML 2020-
93; OML 2017-111.  In addition, certain administrative communications are permissible as long 
as “no opinion of a member is expressed.”  See G.L. c. 30A, § 18; OML 2013-4; OML 2013-5.  
During the August 15 meeting, Committee Chair Neyland posed several questions to the Interim 
Town Administrator with respect to certain expenses being discussed by the Board.  We find that 
the communications by Chair Neyland were directed to the Interim Town Administrator and not 
the Committee members.  Moreover, the questions were meant to collect factual information and 
not to convey an opinion.  See OML 2021-112; OML 2013-130.  Finally, we note that no other 
Committee member spoke during the meeting.  We conclude that the Committee did not 
deliberate, and therefore did not hold a meeting subject to the Open Meeting Law.  Where no 
“meeting” occurred, the posted notice could not be insufficient.  
 
Though we do not find a violation of the Open Meeting Law here, we take this 
opportuni
ty to remind the Committee that two or more public bodies may hold a joint meeting.  
However, each public body participating in the meeting must provide notice pursuant to G. L. c. 
30A, § 20.  The public bodies must provide independent notice of their meetings, or if posting a 
single notice, must clearly state that each public body will be meeting.  See OML 2021-99.  If 
two or more public bodies plan to hold a joint meeting, for purpose of clarity, all bodies involved 
should note in their meeting notices that this will be a joint meeting with the other bodies.  See 
OML 2012-6; OML 2011-16.  Any notice of a joint meeting must meet all the requirements of 
the Open Meeting Law and include the public bodies’ names, the date, time and location of the 
meeting, and all the topics that the public bodies anticipate discussing.  See OML 2013-36.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.
