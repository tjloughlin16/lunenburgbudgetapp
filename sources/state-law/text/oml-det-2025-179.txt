This office received a complaint from Donald Stampfler on August 21, 2025,

that the Georgetown Selectboard (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about June 26, and you 
responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated July 15, 2025.  The complaint alleges that the 
Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to provide the public reasonable time to join the 
Board’s June 23 meeting when it revised the notice, on the day of the meeting, to include a Zoom

Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  In 
reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaint, the Board’s 
response, the request for further review, the original and amended notices of the June 23 
meeting, as well as the recording of the meeting.  
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts to be as follows.  On June 18, the Board posted notice of a meeting to 
be held on June 23 at 6:30 PM.  The notice listed the location of the meeting as the third-floor 
meeting room at the Georgetown Town Hall.  The notice was amended on June 23 at 2:00 PM to 
add a Zoom link, Zoom meeting ID and passcode.  No other changes were made to the notice.

The meeting took place as scheduled on June 23 and was open and accessible for the public to

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the s
 ecrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  
The notice must contain “the date, time and place of the meeting and a listing of topics that the 
chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” Id.  At the core of the Open 
Meeting Law is the requirement that meetings of a public body be open and accessible to the 
public. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except when meeting in executive session, “all meetings of a public 
body shall be open to the public.”). 
 
Relev
ant here, Section 20 of Chapter 20 of the Acts of 2021, An Act Relative to 
Extending Certain COVID-19 Measures Adopted During the State of Emergency (“Section 20”), 
temporarily
2 suspended the requirements under the Open Meeting Law and the Attorney 
General’s Open Meeting Law regulations that a quorum of a public body, including the chair, be 
physically present at the meeting location.  Section 20 allows all members of a public body to 
participate in a meeting remotely, provided that the public body provide “adequate, alternative 
means” of public access, such as through telephone, internet or satellite enabled audio or video 
conferencing or any other technology that enables the public to clearly follow the proceedings of 
the public body while those activities are occurring. See Chapter 20, § 20(b); OML 2021-135.  
Section 20 also allows public bodies to provide the public with “adequate, alternative” access to 
meetings instead of providing in-person access.  Notably, Section 20 does not require public 
bodies to provide “adequate, alternative” access if in-person access to a meeting is provided. 
 
Notice which does not clearly identify the location of a meeting does not conform to the 
Open M
eeting Law. See OML 2024-39; OML 2022-120; OML 2022-65; OML 2016-38.  
Similarly, a notice that does not originally identify the location of a meeting, and is only 
amended to include a meeting location less than 48 hours prior to a meeting, does not conform to 
the Open Meeting Law. See 2022-65.  A public body is permitted to change its meeting location, 
even less than 48 hours prior to a meeting, but only if it makes reasonable efforts to notify the 
public of a new location. See OML 2020-102; OML 2020-95; OML 2017-152. 
 
 Here, the original notice listed the meeting location as the third-floor meeting room in the 
Georgetown Town Hall.  The notice was amended less than 24 hours prior to the meeting to add 
a Zoom access option.  The meeting continued to be accessible for the public to attend in person 
in the third-floor meeting room in the Georgetown Town Hall, as originally stated on the meeting 
notice, as well as via Zoom.  The addition of a Zoom access option, when the original posted 
access method continued to be available, did not violate the Open Meeting Law.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Board or with
