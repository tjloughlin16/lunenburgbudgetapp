 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney O’Keefe: 
 
This office received a complaint from Stephen Gillooly on July 13, 2023, alleging that 
the Falmouth Conservation Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Commission on May 18, 
2023, and you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Commission, by letter dated June 8, 
2023.
1 The complaint alleges that the Commission failed to allow members of the public to 
participate during a Commission meeting held on May 17.  
 
We resolve this matter by informal action, in ac cordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Commission’s response to the complaint, the 
complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the notice and video recording of 
the Commission meeting held on May 17.
2 We find that the Commission did not violate the 
Open Meeting Law in the way alleged. 
 
The Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body allow public participation in 
its
 meetings, but rather provides that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body 
without permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.” G.L. 
c. 30A § 20(f). As such, the law gives the chair of the public body discretion to decide who may 
speak or otherwise participate during a meeting. See OML 2017-189; OML 2014-23; OML 
2012-78.3

The complaint alleges that the Commission did not acknowledge or read written 
comments submitted or allow several members of the public to provide verbal comments during 
the May 17 meeting. During the meeting, the Board held several public hearings, one of which 
was a request to reconfigure a dwelling and driveway at 9 Sycamore Street. The Board heard 
from the engineering firm who explained the new layout of the house and driveway and Board 
staff mentioned that they had received many letters of concern regarding the project. The Board 
then heard from one member of the public who had raised her hand. Seeing no other member of 
the public raise their hand to comment, the Board closed the hearing and took the matter under 
advisement. 
 
Because the Open Meeting Law gives the Chair discr
 etion to decide whether to permit 
public comment during a meeting, the Commission did not violate the Open Meeting Law by not 
acknowledging, reading, or allowing public comments in any form. See OML 2021-84. 
Moreover, it is not a violation of the law for a public body to discuss or vote on a topic without 
first taking comments from the public. See OML 2022-109; OML 2019-2. We do, however, 
encourage public bodies to allow for as much public participation as time and circumstances 
permit. See OML 2019-120; OML 2015-12; OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59.  
 
Although the Open Meeting Law does not require that members of the public be 
perm
itted to speak or otherwise actively participate during a meeting, numerous laws in the 
Commonwealth require that a public hearing be held before the government takes certain action.4  
The specific procedures for notice and conduct of the public hearing are governed by the law 
requiring the hearing and such law may require opportunity for public comment. However, the 
Division of Open Government’s review concerns compliance only with the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The Open Meeting Law does not govern the requirements for conducting 
a public hearing. Therefore, we decline to review, and offer no opinion on, whether any actions 
taken by the Commission could be a violation of any public hearing law or regulation.  See OML 
2020-114; OML 2014-31; OML Declination 1-25-18 (Rowley Planning Board); OML 
Declination 1-29-15 (Holbrook Planning Board).

For the reasons stated above, we find that the Commission did not violate the Open
