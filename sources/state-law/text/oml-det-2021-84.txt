DISCUSSION 
 
 The complainant alleges in the first complaint that she was silenced and not allowed to 
speak during the Commission’s August 25, 2020, meeting.  The Open Meeting Law states that 
“[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body without permission of the chair, and all 
persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”  G.L. c. 30A § 20(f).  The law does not 
require that a public body allow public participation, but rather gives the chair of the public body 
discretion to decide whether to permit public comment during a meeting.  See OML 2017-189; 
OML 2014-23; OML 2012-78.5  Because it is within the Chair’s discretion to limit public 
participation during a meeting, she did not violate the Open Meeting Law by restricting the 
complainant from speaking even though the complainant is a member of the Commission.  We 
 
bodies are complying with the Open Meeting Law’s notice requirements and further provides this office with 
evidence of compliance with the law.  See OML 2013-44.

do, however, encourage public bodies to allow for as much public participation as time and 
circumstances permit.  See OML 2019-120; OML 2015-12; OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59. 
  
The second and third complaints allege that the Commission improperly convened in 
executive session under Purpose 1 on June 16, 2020, and September 30, 2019, respectively, and 
failed to provide certain procedural rights as required under Purpose 1.  We decline to review the 
second complaint because we find it to be untimely.  Complaints alleging violations of the Open 
Meeting Law must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation.  G.L. c. 
30A, § 23(b).  If the alleged violation could not reasonably have been known at the time it 
occurred, then the complaint must be filed within 30 days of the date it should reasonably have 
been discovered.  940 CMR 29.05(3).  When an alleged violation occurs during an open meeting, 
the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable at the time it occurs.  See OML 2014-85; OML 
2012-52.  Here, the complainant was a member of the Commission at the time of the June 16, 
2020, meeting and was present at that meeting.  Further, we note that the Commission did not 
convene in executive session on June 16, 2020; the entire meeting was held in open session.  
Complaints alleging violations that take place during an open session can reasonably be 
discovered at the time they occur.  See OML 2012-52; OML Declination 9-30-13 (Boston 
Zoning Board of Appeal).  Accordingly, any complaint relating to the propriety of the June 16, 
2020, meeting or whether the Commission failed to provide the complainant with certain 
procedural rights as required under Purpose 1 should have been filed within 30 days of the June 
16, 2020, meeting.  Because the complaint was filed on September 24, 2020, over three months 
later, we find that these allegations are untimely and we decline to review them.  See OML 2017-
34; OML Declination 8-25-2014 (Barnstable Fire District Prudential Committee).  Moreover, we 
note that the Commission did not convene in executive session on June 16, 2020, and did not 
discuss the complainant but rather deferred the matter.  When a public body does not actually 
deliberate on a noticed topic, the public body cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with respect 
to that topic, even if the notice would have been insufficiently detailed had the public body 
proceeded with its deliberation or the public body failed to provide the Purpose 1 procedural 
rights.  See OML 2021-37; OML 2015-63; OML 2014-81.  
   
 With respect to the third complaint which involves the Commission meeting held on 
September 30, 2019, the complainant states that she did not discover the alleged violation until 
August 25, 2020.  At the time of the September 30, 2019, meeting, the complainant was not yet a 
member of the Commission and this meeting occurred in executive session.  If the alleged 
violation could not reasonably have been known at the time it occurred, then the complaint must 
be filed within 30 days of the date it should reasonably have been discovered.  940 CMR 
29.05(3).  We find this complaint to be timely filed where it was filed on September 24, 2020, 
which was within 30 days of the discovery of the alleged violation on August 25, 2020. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an 
open session, with some exceptions.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  A public body may enter an 
executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law 
provided that it has first convened in an open session, that a majority of members of the body 
have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and 
entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly announced whether the open session will 
===PAGE 5===
5 
 
reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 
2014-94.   
 
One permissible reason for a public body to convene in executive session is to “discuss 
the reputation, character, physical condition or mental health ... of an individual, or to discuss the 
discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or  charges brought against, a public officer, employee, 
staff member or individual.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1) (“Purpose 1”).  When a public body plans 
to convene in executive session pursuant to Purpose 1, the individual who is the subject of 
discussion has the right to “be notified in writing by the public body at least 48 hours prior to the 
proposed executive session”  Id.  That individual also has the right to be present during executive 
session deliberations which involve that individual; to have counsel present for the purpose of 
advising the individual and not for the purpose of active participation; to speak on her own 
behalf; and to cause an independent record to be created of said executive session by audio-
recording or transcription, at the individual's expense.  Id.   
 
Here, the Committee duly posted notice of the September 30, 2019, meeting 48 hours’ 
prior.  We find that the notice provided a sufficient description of the executive session purpose 
where the notice, as well as the announcement preceding the executive session, included the 
specific statutory citation to Purpose 1 and identified the individuals to be discussed during that 
executive session.  See OML 2016-95.  Moreover, we find that the Commission sent an email to 
the Executive Committee members who were the subject of the Purpose 1 discussion on 
September 25, 2019, and therefore properly notified the individuals who were the subject of the 
executive session discussions.  See OML 2013-70.  Finally, after a review of the executive 
session minutes, we find that the discussions that occurred during the September 30, 2019, 
executive session were appropriate under Purpose 1 as the discussions pertained to complaints 
filed by Commission staff about the executive officers of the Commission.6  
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons explained herein, we find that the Commission did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.  We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  
This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or 
the Commission.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any 
questions regarding this letter.
