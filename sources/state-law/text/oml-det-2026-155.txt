DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at 
the meeting.  Id.  The listing of topics shall have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally 
consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read 
the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2023-
114; OML 2022-239.4  A public body need not list every detail about a topic to comply with the 
Open Meeting Law, as the law does not require a meeting notice to list all tangential issues 
related to a particular topic.  See OML 2024-109; OML 2023-114; OML 2019-134. 
 
Notice that fails altogether to identify a property or project that is anticipated to be 
discussed or the nature of the proposal is not sufficiently specific.  See OML 2024-67; 2024-43.  
Where a public body knows specific roads or addresses that will be discussed, such information 
must be listed on the notice.  See OML 2024-255; OML 2023-209.  Likewise, the nature of a 
project or permit must be included in the notice to satisfy the requirements of the Open Meeting 
Law.  See, e.g., OML 2026-42 (finding that notice topic “200 Quannapowitt Parkway: Grading 
Request” was sufficiently specific to notify the public of a discussion of grading of a 
construction site onto adjacent town-owned property).  A meeting notice that includes a property 
address may be found to run afoul of the Open Meeting Law if it is otherwise insufficiently 
specific.  See, e.g., OML 2023-238 (finding notice topic “10 Pleasant Street Change Order” 
insufficiently specific to inform public of anticipated vote to approve change order to demolition 
project at 10 Pleasant Street to cover cost of removing asbestos); OML 2023-182 (finding notice

Here, the notice of the January 21 meeting identified the street of the property that is the 
subject of the complaint, Paula Lane, under the heading of a discussion regarding “finishing 
construction of, and acceptance of, uncompleted/ unaccepted subdivision streets.”  The 
complainant maintains that a vote to release Lot 14 Paula Lane was improper where the only 
reference to Paula Lane in the notice concerned a “Discussion/Status Report on finishing 
construction of, and acceptance of, uncompleted/ unaccepted subdivision streets.”  As noted 
above, Member Thomas Chojnacki raised this same concern during the January 21 meeting.  It is 
true that the public might have benefitted from more specific notice of the discussion of the lot 
release.  Nonetheless, reference to “finishing construction of, and acceptance of, 
uncompleted/unaccepted subdivision streets” imbued the notice with sufficient specificity to 
satisfy the Open Meeting Law.  A member of the public, upon a reasonable reading of the notice 
of the January 21 meeting, could understand that the Board might discuss various topics related 
to the completion of the referenced subdivision, including release of lots from a restrictive 
covenant.  We therefore find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law, as alleged.   
 
 The complaint also notes that Member Richard Clark “tried to release the money from 
Steven Watroba’s surety held by the Town for an active subdivision, which was also not on the 
agenda.”  The bond also was raised during the meeting in the context of the “Discussion/Status 
Report on finishing construction of, and acceptance of, uncompleted/ unaccepted subdivision 
streets.”  However, that discussion did not proceed because the Chair recused himself and the 
complainant, who was the Vice Chair, was not present at the meeting.  When reviewing a 
meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a 
meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2025-101; 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we 
review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently 
detailed to make the public aware of the discussion topics.  See OML 2026-139; OML 2015-101; 
OML 2015-21. Assuming, arguendo, that the bond would have to be listed explicitly in the 
notice for the Board to discuss it, the Board did not actually discuss it.  Therefore, it was not 
required to be listed on the notice. 
 
To the extent that the complaint alleges that the notice should have explicitly referenced a 
possible vote to release Lot 14, we note that the Open Meeting Law does not require that a notice 
separately indicate the possibility of a vote.  See OML 2022-239; OML 2015-66.  It is 
reasonably foreseeable that a public body may take action and hold a vote following discussion 
of a noticed topic, even when the notice lists only a “discussion.”  See OML 2021-153; OML 
2019-36; OML 2017-48.   
Finally, to the extent that the complaint alleges that it was improper to release Lot 14 
without a request to do so, that allegation falls outside the scope of the Open Meeting Law.  The 
Division of Open Government’s review concerns compliance with the Open Meeting Law.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law by discussing a topic not included on the notice of its January 21 meeting, to wit, the release 
of Lot 14 on Paula Lane.
