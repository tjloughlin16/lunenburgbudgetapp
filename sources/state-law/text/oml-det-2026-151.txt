FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  On N ovember 25, the Board posted notice of a meeting to 
be held on December 2.  The notice included the following topic: 
 
Executive Session Exemption #6- To consider the purchase, exchange, lease or value of 
real property if the chair declares that an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on 
the negotiating position of the public body. 
 
 The December 2 meeting went forward as scheduled.  The first order of business was the 
executive session.  The chair invited a motion to enter executive session, tracking the language 
of the written notice and declaring that an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the 
negotiating position of the public body.   
 
The minutes of the December 2 executive session have not been released to the public.  
As we reviewed them in camera, we do not summarize them here.   
 
DISCUSSION 
   
I. The Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it invoked Purpose 6 to 
convene in executive session on December 2. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  All meetings of a public body must be conducted in an 
open session unless a closed-door executive session is convened for one of ten permissible 
purposes.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  When meeting in executive session, a public body may 
only address subjects related to the narrow executive session purpose the public body cited when 
entering executive session.  See Dist. Att’y for N.W. Dist. v. Selectmen of Sunderland, 11 Mass. 
App. Ct. 663, 666 (1981).  Pursuant to the Open Meeting Law, a public body may meet in 
executive session “[t]o consider the purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property if the 
chair declares that an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the negotiating position of 
the public body.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6) (“Purpose 6”).   
 
Executive session purposes are narrowly construed.  The public body bears the burden of 
demonstrating applicability of the claimed executive session purpose.  See District Attorney for 
the N. Dist. v. School Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 566 (2009).  Purpose 6, narrowly 
construed, permits a public body to meet in executive session to protect its negotiating position

when seeking to purchase, exchange, or lease real estate, or to discuss the value of real estate 
where it has a negotiating position relative to that property.  See Allen v. Board of Selectmen of 
Belmont, 58 Mass. App. Ct. 715, 719-721 (2003); OML 2019-143; OML 2016-50. 3  Discussions 
under Purpose 6 must relate directly to the purchase, lease, exchange, or value of real property 
where confidentiality is necessary to protect a public body’s negotiating position.  See OML 
2022-119; OML 2021-62.  The proper application of Purpose 6 is to protect a public body’s 
negotiating position vis-a-vis other parties to a transaction, not to escape the inconvenience of 
public scrutiny or opposition.  See OML 2026-142; OML 2023-241; OML 2023-81; OML 2020-
158.  General discussions regarding real property or the use of real property, where there is no 
negotiating position to protect, do not fall within Purpose 6.  See OML 2026-109; OML 2023-
81; OML 2012-98 (“Potential uses for land and whether or not to go ahead with a project are not 
proper discussions under Purpose 6.”).  Similarly, discussions regarding the imposition of 
conservation restrictions, where there is no negotiating position to protect and the discussion 
does not relate to the purchase, exchange, lease or value of property, do not fall within the 
executive session purpose.  See OML 2019-59.  See also OML 2020-63 (finding Purpose 6 
inappropriate where discussion was about project funding and procedure, not about purchase or 
value of locus for project); OML 2025-95 (finding that discussion of grant and sensitivity to 
property owners’ desire for secrecy did not fall within the narrow permissible scope of Purpose 
6).   
 
Und
er limited circumstances, we have found it permissible to meet in executive session 
under Purpose 6 in the absence of contemporaneous negotiations seeking to consummate a 
specific transaction.  For example, it was permissible to meet in executive session under Purpose 
6 to identify potential entities to approach regarding the purchase or exchange of real property.  
See OML 2022-119.  See also OML 2018-73 (finding no violation where public body discussed 
potential acquisition of alternate elementary school sites in executive session under Purpose 6).  
Here, based upon our in ca
 mera review of the minutes of the December 2 executive session, we 
find that Purpose 6 was applicable to the discussion for which the Board convened in executive 
session.  Therefore, the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it convened in 
executive session on December 2.   
 
Although we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it convened 
in executive session on December 2 under Purpose 6, we note that part of the discussion fell 
outside the stated purpose of the executive session.  When meeting in executive session, a public 
body may only address subjects related to the narrow executive session purpose the public body 
cited when entering executive session.  See Dist. Att’y for N.W. Dist. V. Selectmen of 
Sunderland, 11 Mass. App. Ct. 663, 666 (1981).  The minutes of the December 2 executive 
session reflect that the Board discussed mooring permits.  The minutes provide no further detail 
regarding this discussion.4  A discussion of permitting detached from the purchase, lease, 
exchange or value of property falls outside the Purpose 6.  We acknowledge that managing the

Finally, we address the Board’s statement, in its response to the Open Meeting Law 
complaint, that at the conclusion of the December 2 executive session, the Board voted to table 
its discussion, and that as a result, the lawful purpose of the executive session remains ongoing.  
We caution the Board that when it conducts its review of these executive session minutes for 
possible release, the fact that the discussion was “tabled” does not provide a basis to withhold the 
minutes indefinitely.  The mere possibility that at some point in the future the Town may initiate 
negotiations to purchase or lease property, without more, is insufficient to justify continued 
withholding of the executive session minutes.  To the extent that the Board is not considering the 
purchase, exchange, lease, or value of the subject property, the need for secrecy regarding those 
discussions likely has ceased and the minutes should not be withheld or redacted with respect to 
those discussions.  See OML 2022-119; OML 2021-175. 
 
II. Notice of the December 2 executive session was not sufficiently specific. 
 
A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours in advance, not 
including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Notices must include “a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.  Id.  The 
listing of topics shall have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be 
sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand 
the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2023-114; OML 2022-239.   
 
When noticing an executive session pursuant to Purpose 6, a public body must identify 
the property to be discussed if doing so would not compromise the purpose for which the 
executive session was called.  See OML 2025-102; OML 2019-101; OML 2012-52.  Even if an 
address is not listed, the public body must disclose some descriptive information about the real 
property to be discussed unless disclosure of this information would compromise the purpose of 
the executive session.  See OML 2024-158; OML 2024-67; OML 2018-73.  Notice that fails 
altogether to identify a property or project that is anticipated to be discussed or the nature of a 
proposal is not sufficiently specific.  See OML 2024-67; 2024-43.  See also OML 2024-11 
(finding notice topic “Temporary Housing Fire Department Renovation - Chief Harris and 
Director Larivee” insufficiently described discussion of temporary relocation of fire department 
to the so-called House of Art and subsequent demolition of the House of Art); OML 2023-100 
(finding notice topic “Authorize ARPA Expenditure” insufficiently described discussion of using 
ARPA funding to demolish Old Town Hall building). 
 
The notice of the December 2 executive session did not identify the property to be 
discussed or the nature of the discussion.  Indeed, the notice contained no information about the 
anticipated executive session discussion other than the Purpose 6 statutory language.  Although 
we generally defer to a public body’s assessment of whether identifying a specific property in the 
meeting notice would compromise the purpose of the executive session, the public body must be

able to demonstrate a reasonable basis for that claim if challenged.  See OML 2023-79; OML 
2021-142; OML 2021-56; OML 2020-158; OML 2018-139.   
 
In response to the Open Meeting Law complaint, the Board argues that it complied with 
the Law when the chair announced that a discussion of the topic in open session would have a 
detrimental effect on the Board’s negotiating position.  However, that announcement informs our 
analysis of whether the Board entered executive session for a proper purpose;
5 the announcement 
did not satisfy the requirement of sufficiency of notice.  The Board also cites a prior 
determination in which we found that a public body did not violate the Open Meeting Law where 
it did not identify in a meeting notice the property to be discussed in executive session.  See 
OML 2016-94.  There, however, the public body met its burden by demonstrating a reasonable 
basis for withholding the identity of the property.  We found in OML 2016-94 that the public 
body was justifiably concerned that identifying the property would generate public interest in it 
and compromise the town’s negotiating position.  Here, the complainant challenged the Board’s 
omission of the property identification in the notice of the December 2 executive session.  Upon 
being challenged, the Board was required to demonstrate a reasonable basis for the omission.  
Rather than making the required showing, the Board simply relied on the statutory language.  In 
doing so, it failed to meet its burden.  Therefore, we find that the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it discussed a topic during its December 2 executive session that was not listed with 
sufficient specificity in the notice.  We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with 
the Open Meeting Law, and caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.  We also order the Board to make an announcement during an open 
session, identifying the property that was the subject of the December 2 executive session, such 
announcement to be made within 30 days of this letter. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any questions.
