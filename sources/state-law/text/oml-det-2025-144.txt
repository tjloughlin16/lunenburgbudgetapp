RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint  
 
Dear Attorney Castro:  
 
This office received a complaint from Stephen Morgan on May 10, 2025, alleging that 
the Ashland Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint originally was filed with the Board on April 18, 2025, and you responded on 
behalf of the Board by letter dated May 8, 2025.  The complaint alleges that the Board violated 
the Open Meeting Law by failing to include in the minutes of its January 17, 2024, executive 
session a list of documents used at that meeting.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way 
alleged.  We also find the violation intentional.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the 
complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further 
review.  We also reviewed minutes of the January 17, 2024, executive session, a public records 
request the complainant directed to the Board, and the Board’s response thereto.  Finally, I 
communicated with you by telephone and email and with the complainant by email. 
 
DISCUSSION 
   
The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to “create and maintain accurate minutes 
of al
l meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members 
present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other 
exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including 
the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  A document is “used” at a meeting when it is, at a

minimum, physically present, verbally identified, and its contents are discussed by members of 
the public body during an open session.  See OML 2020-155; OML 2019-57; OML 2012-42.1    
 
On January 17, 2024, the Board convened in executive session, pursuant to G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 21(a)(2), to conduct contract negotiations with the Town Manager.  Minutes of that executive 
session, released on April 17, 2025, note that “Brandi Kinsman provided the board with her 
thoughts and simplified Michaels’s [sic] requests in a three-scenario fact sheet. . . .”  The minutes 
also reflect a discussion of the three scenarios.  On April 18, 2025, the complainant sent a public 
records request to the Town Clerk of the Town of Ashland, asking that they “provide or post a 
copy of all documents used in the executive session meeting of 01-17-2024.”  In response to the 
public records request, the Clerk produced a one page spreadsheet outlining three compensation 
“scenarios” for the Town Manager.  The response to the public records request confirms that the 
Board used the three-scenario fact sheet referenced in the minutes at its executive session on 
January 17, 2024.
2  By failing to list the document in the minutes of the meeting, the Board 
violated the Open Meeting Law.3   
 
The Board notes that the one page spreadsheet was attached to the minutes post
 ed online, 
even if it was not listed in the minutes.  However, attaching documents to the minutes does not 
satisfy the Law’s clear mandate that “minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions,” 
include “a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The 
Open Meeting Law does not require that documents used during a meeting and referenced in 
meeting minutes be appended to or stored with the minutes.  See OML 2024-25; OML 2021-174; 
OML 2017-43.  We also remind the Board that it is not sufficient to merely reference documents 
used at the meeting in the body of the minutes; the minutes must include a list of the documents 
used.  See OML 2024-152; OML 2023-238; OML 2022-189.  
 
Having found a violation, we must determine whether the violation was intentional, as 
the complainant urges.  See G.L. c. 3 0A,  § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission 
by a public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 
CMR 29.02.  An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate 
ignorance of the law’s requirement or has previously been advised or warned that certain conduct 
violates the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  Over the past five years, our office has issued six 
determinations finding the Board in violation of the Open Meeting Law.  Of interest for purposes 
of prior warning is OML 2023-13, in which we found that the Board failed to include within 
executive session minutes a list of documents used, the same violation we find here.  Because the 
Board was found in violation of the same provision of the Open Meeting Law less than three 
years ago, we find this violation intentional.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
faili
ng to include a list of documents in the minutes of its January 17, 2024, executive session.  
We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law’s 
requirements.  We further order the Board to amend the minutes of its January 17, 2024, 
executive session to include a list of all documents used at the meeting, and to certify to 
this office within sixty (60) days of the date of this letter that it has done so.  See 940 CMR 
29.07(4).  Because the Board referenced the spreadsheet within the minutes—albeit not in a 
separate list of documents—and also attached the spreadsheet to the subject executive session 
minutes, which are available on the town website, we do not recommend assessment of a civil 
penalty.
