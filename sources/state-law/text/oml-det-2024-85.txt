On  February 1, 2024, this office received a complaint from Craig Ptaszenski, alleging 
that the New Bedford City Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Council on January 12.
1  You responded 
on behalf of the Council by letter dated February 1.  The complainant alleges that the Council 
deliberated outside a posted meeting on or about December 28, 2023, thereby violating the Open 
Meeting Law. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged by the complainant.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the complaint, the 
response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  We also reviewed 
the invitation to the gathering at issue and viewed recordings of Council members provided by 
the complainant.   
 
FACTS 
  
We find the facts as follows.  On or about November 28, 2023, City Council President 
Linda M. Morad sent an email to city councilors entitled “2023 Holiday Party details.”  The 
email stated in part: “As has been the tradition of the City Council for many years as Council 
President I would like to invite you and your guest to join me at our annual City Council Holiday 
gathering. . . . This year the City Council Holiday gathering will be on Thursday, December 28

at Cotali Mar Restaurante, located at 1178 Acushnet Avenue.”  The email included the time of 
the event and a description of the food.  Ms. Morad concluded the email: “I look forward to

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law is designed to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. Of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public unless an executive session is 
convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a 
deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body's jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 18.  The Law defines a “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any 
medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public 
business within its jurisdiction,” but explicitly excludes “attendance by a quorum of a public 
body at a public or private gathering, including a conference or training program or a media, 
social or other event, so long as the members do not deliberate.”  Id; OML 2021-161; OML 2016 
- 134.2  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” is a simple majority of the 
members of a public body.  Id. 
 
Based on the available evidence in this case, we do not find that deliberation took place. 
See G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  A quorum of the Council attended the 2023 Holiday Party.  However, the 
Council has represented, and we find, that there was no deliberation during the 2023 Holiday 
Party.  Therefore, the party did not constitute a meeting under the Open Meeting Law.  Without 
providing opposing facts, the complainant questions the veracity of the Council’s representation.  
Absent sufficient evidence to the contrary, we credit a public body's account of the facts. See 
OML 2023-121; OML 2021-161; OML 2017-130; OML 2017-93; OML 2016-176; see also 
LaPointe v. License Board of Worcester, 389 Mass. 454 (1983) (“There is every presumption in 
favor of the honesty and sufficiency of the motives actuating public officers in actions ostensibly 
taken for the general welfare.”).  For these reasons, we find that the Council did not violate the 
Open Meeting Law at its 2023 Holiday Party.

CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Me eting
