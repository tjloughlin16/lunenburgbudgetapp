 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Lydon: 
 
This office received a complaint from Stephen Morgan on June 8, 2023, alleging that the 
Ashland Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about May 17, 2023, and you responded 
to the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated June 8, 2023.  The complaint alleges that 
the Board failed to timely approve seven sets of minutes of meetings held between October 12, 
2022, and May 3, 2023.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting by failing to 
timely approve five sets of meeting minutes.  We find that the Board’s failure to timely approve 
minutes was an intentional violation of the Open Meeting Law.  In reaching this determination, 
we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint 
filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice and open 
session minutes of the Board meetings held on May 17, 2023, and June 7, 2023, as well as a 
video recording of the May 17, 2023, meeting.

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Minutes must be reviewed and 
approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” means “within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can 
show good cause for further delay.”  See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.
2  Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2018-67;

The complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely approve seven sets of minutes for 
meetings held between October 12, 2022, and May 3, 2023.  We find that two sets of minutes 
were approved in a timely manner but that the remaining five sets of minutes were not approved 
in a timely manner.  Specifically, the minutes of the meetings held on April 26, 2023, and May 3, 
2023, were approved in a timely manner where both sets of minutes were approved within the 
next three Board meetings.  See OML 2023-4.  Therefore, the Board did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law with respect to these two sets of minutes.  However, we find that the minutes of the 
meetings held on October 12, 2022, March 8, 2023, March 15, 2023, March 29, 2023, and April 
12, 2023, were not approved in a timely manner where the minutes were not approved within the 
later of the next three meetings or 30 days following each meeting.  See OML 2021-155; see also 
OML 2023-129 (finding a public body’s joint meeting with another town public body was a 
meeting of the public body for the purpose of calculating timely approval of meeting minutes).

As such, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to approve these five 
sets of minutes in a timely manner. 
 
We must determine whether this violation was, as the complainant urges, an intentional 
one.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body 
or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting 
Law.  Id.  The Board was advised by the Attorney General last year, on March 15, 2022, of its 
obligation to review and approve minutes in a timely manner and that timely manner means 
within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later.  See OML 
2022-43.  Furthermore, we specifically advised the Board that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  Because the Board had previously been advised 
that the failure to approve minutes within the next three meetings or 30 days, whichever is 
longer, violates the Open Meeting Law, we find that this violation is intentional.  See G.L. c. 
30A, § 18; 940 CMR 29.02; OML 2013-63.  However, we decline to recommend the imposition 
of a civil penalty in this instance where the minutes at issue were approved before the complaint 
was filed with our office for review.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c); OML 2022-184; OML 2018-108; 
OML 2015-122.  
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board’s failure to timely approve minutes 
was an intentional violation of the law.  We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance 
with the law’s requirements and caution the Board that a future finding of an intentional 
violation of the Open Meeting Law may result in a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation.  In 
addition, we order all members of the Board to attend a webinar training on the Open Meeting 
Law conducted by our office within the next 90 days. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
