This office received a complaint from Ann Sheffield on November 4, 2022, alleging that 
the Milton School Building Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. 
c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on September 30, 
2022, and you responded, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated October 27, 2022, 
following an extension of time granted by this office.  The complaint alleges that the Committee 
failed to timely respond to a request for executive session minutes and entered executive session 
for an improper purpose.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law in the 
ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Committee’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review.  We also reviewed the open and executive session minutes of the Committee meetings 
held on November 30, 2020, January 4, 2021, January 25, 2021, March 1, 2021, March 29, 2021, 
and May 24, 2021; the open session minutes of the Committee meeting held on July 28, 2021; 
and the minutes of the meeting of the Warrant Committee held on March 8, 2023. 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  In February of 2019, the residents of Milton voted to create 
the Committee for the purpose of planning the construction, alteration, renovation, remodeling, 
and furnishing of school buildings.  The Committee began meeting in June of 2019.

During an open session meeting held on November 25, 2019, the Committee 
unanimously voted “to plan the construction of a free-standing elementary school building to 
address the Town’s long-term school space needs.”  Beginning in late January of 2020 through 
May of 2021, the Committee met in executive session pursuant to Purpose 6 to discuss potential 
locations for a new elementary school.  Some of the properties discussed in executive session 
were owned by the town while others were owned by religious or other private entities.   
 
During an open session meeting held on July 28, 2021, the Committee announced its 
recommendation that a new elementary school be built on six acres of town-owned land off Gile 
Road.  Although the potential site was designated as conservation land, the Committee explained 
that the town could designate and swap another piece of land as conservation in order to build a 
new school on the Gile Road location. 
 
On August 28, 2022, the complainant requested from the Committee executive session 
minutes of meetings held between November 2020 and June 2021.  The Committee did not 
respond to the request.  On September 30, 2022, having received no further response from the 
Committee, the complainant filed an Open Meeting Law complaint regarding the Committee’s 
failure to respond to her request for executive session minutes.  On October 27, 2022, the 
Committee provided to the complainant copies of executive session minutes of meetings held on 
November 30, 2020, January 4, 2021, January 25, 2021, March 1, 2021, March 29, 2021, and 
May 24, 2021. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Committee Failed to Timely Respond to a Request for Executive 
Session Meeting Minutes. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to timely respond to a request for 
exec
utive session meeting minutes.  The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create 
and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(a).  A public body must respond to a request for executive session minutes within 10 days.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  If the public body or a designee has determined, prior to the request, 
that certain executive session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester 
directly, notifying him or her of the availability of those minutes.  See OML 2013-105; OML 
2013-99.1  And if, at the time of a request, the public body has not conducted a review of the 
minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure is warranted, the body must perform such 
a review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no later than its next meeting or within 30 days, 
whichever occurs first.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  In such circumstances, the body must still 
respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her that it is conducting this review.  
See OML 2022-123; OML 2015-86; OML 2013-99.  We encourage public bodies to also include 
specific information about when the minutes will be reviewed.  See OML 2015-130.     
 
Here, the complainant requested executive session minutes on August 28, 2022, but the 
Committee did not respond to the request until October 27, 2022, two months later.  As such, we

find that the Committee viola ted the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to a request for

We take this opportunity to remind the Committee th at, even in the absence of a specific 
request, public bodies have an obligation to review the minutes of executive sessions at 
reasonable intervals to determine if continued non-disclosure of minutes is warranted, and to 
announce that determination at the next meeting following its review.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(g)(1); OML 2019-35; OML 2015-94; OML 2013-56.  Although the Open Meeting Law does 
not define “reasonable intervals,” we have found that quarterly reviews or reviews every six 
months satisfy the law.  See OML 2017-104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180.  
 
II. We Find that the Committee Discussed Matters in Executive Session that 
Did Not Fall Within the Stated Executive Session Purpose . 
 
The complaint challenges the propriety of the Committee’s discussions in executive 
session pertaining to potential locations for a new elementary school during meetings held 
between November 30, 2020, and May 24, 2021.
3  The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to 
eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding deliberations and decisions on which public policy is 
based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open 
Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an open session, unless 
a closed-door executive session is convened for one of ten permissible purposes set forth in the 
Open Meeting Law.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).   
 
 One permissible reason for a public body to convene in executive session is “to consider 
th
e purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property if the chair declares that an open meeting 
may have a detrimental effect on the negotiating position of the public body.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
21(a)(6) (“Purpose 6”).  Purpose 6, narrowly construed, permits a public body to meet in 
executive session to protect its negotiating position when seeking to purchase, exchange, or lease 
real estate, or to discuss the value of real estate where it has a negotiating position relative to that 
property.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(6); Allen v. Committee of Selectmen of Belmont, 58 Mass. 
App. Ct. 715, 719-721 (2003); OML 2019-143; OML 2016-50.   
 
Foll
owing our review of the executive session minutes, we find that certain of the 
discussions by the Committee during the six executive session meetings held between November 
30, 2020, and May 24, 2021, were appropriate under Purpose 6 but substantial portions of the 
discussions were not appropriate under Purpose 6.  Purpose 6 is intended to preserve

confidentiality in negotiating the value of the property to be purchased, exchanged or leased to 
avoid put
ting the public body at a disadvantage in its negotiations for the property.  See District  
Atty. for the Plymouth Dist. v. Committee of Selectmen of Middleborough, 395 Mass. 629, 633 
n.5 (1985); OML 2019-10; OML 2016-50.  As such, discussions pertaining to the potential 
exchange or acquisition of real property owned by private or religious entities and the 
identification of the potential entities to approach regarding buying or exchanging real property 
were appropriate for executive session under Purpose 6.  See OML 2022-119; OML 2018-73 
(finding no violation where the public body discussed the potential acquisition of alternate 
elementary school sites in executive session under Purpose 6).   
 
However, general discussions regarding real property or the use of real property, where 
ther
e is no negotiating position to protect, do not fall within executive session Purpose 6.  See 
OML 2019-108; OML 2019-59.  Hence, discussions pertaining to the potential use or suitability 
of town-owned property were not appropriate for executive session under Purpose 6 as the 
Committee did not have a negotiating position to protect.  See OML 2012-98.  We find that the 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by having such discussions in executive session.  We 
remind the Committee that the proper application of Purpose 6 is to protect a public body’s 
negotiating position vis-à-vis other parties to a transaction and not to escape the inconvenience of 
public scrutiny or opposition.  See OML 2022-119; OML 2020-158.   
 
Although not specifically raised in the complaint, we note our concern with the 
suff
iciency of the executive session topics listed on the notices for the six meetings held between 
November 30, 2020, and May 24, 2021.  To convene in executive session, a public body must 
identify in the notice the executive session purpose it intends to invoke to justify the executive 
session and provide as much detail as possible to inform the public of the nature of the executive 
session discussion without compromising the confidentiality of the executive session.  See OML 
2016-21; see also District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 
567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary 
... because that is the only notification given the public that a [public body] would conduct 
business in private, and the only way the public would know if the reason for doing so was 
proper or improper”).   
 
The notice of the Committee meeting held on November 30, 2020, simply listed 
“Exe
cutive Session” and did not identify or describe the executive session purpose nor the topic 
to be discussed.  While a public body is not required to provide the specific statutory citation of 
the executive session it plans to convene under, the public body must at least provide a sufficient 
description of the executive session purpose.  See OML 2016-95.   
 
The notices of the Committee meetings held on January 4, 2021, January 25, 2021, 
Mar
ch 1, 2021, March 29, 2021, and May 24, 2021, listed an executive session “To consider the 
purchase, exchange, lease or value of real property if the chair declares that an open meeting may 
have a detrimental effect on the negotiating position of the public body; Property: 0 Randolph 
Ave, Milton MA 02186.”  When noticing an executive session pursuant to Purpose 6, a public 
body must identify the property to be discussed if doing so would not compromise the purpose 
for which the executive session was called.  See OML 2013-97; OML 2012-52; OML 2010-2.  
While the Committee discussed the property located on Randolph Avenue, it discussed several

other properties during each of these executive session meetings and therefore should have also 
identified those other properties on the notice, if doing so would not have compromised the 
Committee’s negotiating purpose.  We remind the Committee of its obligations under the law. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
We find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely respond 
to a request for executive session minutes and by improperly discussing the use or suitability of 
town-owned property in executive session under Purpose 6.  We order immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  We order no further remedial action as the 
Committee has since reviewed and released the requested executive session minutes and 
provided them to the complainant.  
   
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This
