 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints 
 
Dear Attorney Hall: 
 
This office received a complaint from Laura Ortiz on December 2, 2023, a second 
complaint on December 31, 2023, and a third complaint on March 14, 2024, alleging that the 
Lowell School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaints were originally filed with the Committee between August 11, 2023, and 
November 30, 2023, and you responded to all three complaints, on behalf of the Committee, by 
separate letters dated October 6, 2023, October 30, 2023, and January 19, 2024, following 
extensions of time granted by this office.  The complaints allege that the Committee failed to 
post sufficiently specific meeting notices, failed to create sufficient meeting minutes, and failed 
to properly respond to an Open Meeting Law complaint.  In addition, the complaints raise certain 
allegations that, even if true, would not constitute violations of the Open Meeting Law and that 
we decline to review, as discussed below. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting notices for meetings held on July 19, 2023, July 26, 2023, and September 20, 2023, that 
included insufficient specificity; creating insufficient minutes of a meeting held on October 18, 
2023; and failing to properly respond to an Open Meeting Law complaint dated October 4, 2023.  
We decline to review allegations pertaining to meetings that were held on May 17, 2023; June 8, 
2023; June 21, 2023; June 24, 2023; June 28, 2023;  August 11, 2023; September 1, 2023; 
September 6, 2023; September 18, 2023; and September 29, 2023, for the reasons discussed 
below.

DISCUSSION 
 
I. Several Allegations Raised in the Complaints Do Not Constitute 
Violations of the Open Meeting Law. 
  
The complaints allege that the Committee did not allow members of the public to speak 
during meetings.  The Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body allow public 
participation, but rather gives the chair of the public body discretion to decide whether to permit 
public comment during a meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A § 20(g) (“[n]o person shall address a 
meeting of a public body without permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of 
the chair, be silent.”); see also OML 2020-77; OML 2017-189.
1  If public participation will be 
permitted, the chair may determine the method for receiving public comment, who may speak or 
otherwise participate during a meeting, and may set limitations on participation.
2  See OML 
2020-114; OML 2017-189.  We do, however, encourage public bodies to allow for as much 
public participation as time and circumstances permit.  See OML 2019-120; OML 2015-12; 
OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59.  Nonetheless, even assuming the allegation raised in your 
complaints was true, it would not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.   
 
One of the complaints alleges that the Committee improperly discussed certain executive 
ses
sion matters in open session.  The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public 
body be conducted in an open session, unless a closed-door executive session is convened for 
one of the ten permissible purposes enumerated in the law.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  While 
the Open Meeting Law allows public bodies to discuss certain matters, such as contract 
negotiations and collective bargaining, behind closed doors if the body believes confidentiality is 
required, see G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a)(2), (3) (public body may convene in executive session to 
discuss “conduct collective bargaining sessions or contract negotiations” or “discuss strategy 
with respect to collective bargaining if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the 
bargaining position of the public body and the chair so declares”), the law does not require that 
public body members keep topics discussed in executive session confidential nor does it impose 
penalties for members’ failure to do so.  We therefore find that, even if taken as true, the 
allegation that the Committee discussed an executive session matter in open session while the 
purpose for the executive session may still have been ongoing does not constitute a violation of 
the Open Meeting Law.  We express no opinion as to whether other laws may require public 
body members to maintain the confidentiality of such sessions.  See OML 2014-91; OML 2011-
29.

Finally, we understand the complaints to allege that meeting topics requested by 
Comm
ittee members are not included on notices.  The Open Meeting Law does not require the 
chair to add topics to a meeting notice that are requested by fellow members of the public body.  
The Open Meeting Law requires that the notice for every meeting include, among other things, 
“a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 20(b).  Therefore, even if true, an allegation that the Chair or another individual, such as 
the Committee’s administrative assistant, declined to include notice topics that were requested by 
members does not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2023-11; OML 
Declination 4-2-24 (Massachusetts State Rehabilitation Council’s State Plan Committee).  
However, we do encourage public bodies to be responsive to issues raised by members of the 
public body.  
 
II. We Decl
 ine to Review Allegations that are Untimely or Allegations 
Pertaining to Meetings that Did Not Occur.  
 
The complaints allege that executive session topics listed on the notices of the Committee 
meetings held on May 17, 2023; June 8, 2023; June 21, 2023; June 24, 2023; June 28, 2023;  
August 11, 2023; September 1, 2023; September 6, 2023; September 18, 2023; and September 
29, 2023, lacked sufficient specificity where they either did not identify the name of the 
nonunion personnel with whom the Committee would be negotiating or did not identify the name 
of the individual who filed Open Meeting Law complaints that the Committee would be 
discussing.  
   
First, we decline to review allegations with respect to the sufficiency of the May 17, 
2023; June 8, 2023; June 21, 2023; June 24, 2023; and June 28, 2023, meeting notices.  
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public body 
wit
hin 30 days of the alleged violation, or if the alleged violation could not have been known at 
the time it occurred, then within 30 days from when the alleged violation reasonably could have 
been discovered.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(4).  When an alleged violation occurs 
during an open meeting, the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable at the time it occurs.  
See OML 2014-85; OML 2012-52.   
 
The Committee posted notice for meetings held on May 17, 2023, June 8, 2023, and June 
21, 2023, June 24, 2023, a
nd June 28, 2023.  Any violation concerning the notices and 
discussions that occurred during these meetings could have been discovered at the time each 
meeting occurred, and any member of the public could have attended the open session portion of 
the meetings and learned the purpose for the executive session.  See OML Declination 12-18-20 
(Pembroke Board of Health); OML Declination 3-3-14 (Sterling Board of Selectmen).  Because 
the complaints were filed more than 30 days after each alleged violation was reasonably 
discoverable, we find the allegations with respect to these five meetings to be untimely and 
decline to review them.  See OML Declination 4-7-22 (Mendon Board of Assessors); OML 
Declination 9-27-21 (Ashland Select Board); OML Declination 2-19-21 (Everett City Council).  
 
Sec
ond, we decline to review allegations with respect to the sufficiency of the August 11, 
2023; September 1, 2023; September 18, 2023; and September 29, 2023, meeting notices.  The 
Committee did not hold meetings on any of these dates.  In addition, although the Committee

held a meeting on September 6, 2023, the Committee did not convene in executive session 
during t
hat meeting.  Because the Committee did not convene in executive session on August 11, 
2023; September 1, 2023; September 6, 2023; September 18, 2023; or September 29, 2023, we 
find no violation with respect to the sufficiency of the executive session topics listed on the 
notices.  When a public body does not actually deliberate on a noticed topic, the public body 
cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with respect to that topic, even if it would have been 
insufficiently specific had the public body proceeded with its deliberation.  See OML 2019-61; 
OML 2015-63; OML 2014-81.  
 
III. Notices for Certain Executive Sessions Lacked Sufficient Specificity. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
noticed and open to the public, unless an executive session is convened.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-
(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that 
a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each 
member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly 
announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.   
 
The chair of the public body must announce in open session the purpose for the executive 
ses
sion, “stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which 
the executive session was called.”  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b)(3); see also District Attorney 
for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of 
the reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification 
given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the 
public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  This level of detail 
about the executive session topic must also be included in the meeting notice.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); OML 2016-72.  
 
 The notices for Committee meetings held on July 19, 2023, and July 26, 2023, both 
incl
uded an executive session under Purpose 2 or Purpose 3.  Executive Session Purpose 2 
allows a public body to enter executive session to “conduct strategy sessions in preparation for 
negotiations with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective bargaining sessions or contract 
negotiations with nonunion personnel.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2).  Executive session Purpose 3 
allows a public body to enter executive session to “discuss strategy with respect to collective 
bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or 
litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3).  
Grievance matters may also be discussed under Purpose 2 or Purpose 3 because they are part of 
the collective bargaining process.  See Ghiglione v. School Committee of Southbridge, 376 
Mass. 70, 73 (1978) (“[c]ollective bargaining sessions encompass not only the negotiations 
leading to the agreement, but also the resolution of grievances pursuant to the collective 
bargaining agreement”).  When convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 2 or Purpose 
3, a public body must identify the nonunion personnel or collective bargaining unit with whom it 
===PAGE 5===
5 
 
will be negotiating , if doing so will not compromise the lawful purpose for secrecy.  See OML 
2018-139; OML 2016-43; OML 2015-87.   
 
Here, the executive session topics listed on the July 19, 2023, and July 26, 2023, meeting 
notic
es did not include the names of the nonunion personnel with whom the Board would be 
negotiating; rather, the notices identified the positions that would be discussed, i.e. “Chief 
Academic Officer” and “Interim Chief of Schools Officer.”  We have previously stated that 
public bodies must identify in their meeting notice the name or position of the specific nonunion 
personnel with whom they will be negotiating.  See OML 2020-67; OML 2015-63; OML 2013-
97.  Because the July 19, 2023, notice included the position - “Chief Academic Officer” - of the 
specific nonunion personnel with whom the Committee would be negotiating in executive 
session, we find that the notice included sufficient specificity.  However, the title of “Interim 
Chief of Schools Officer,” which was identified on both the July 19, 2023, and July 26, 2023, 
meeting notices suggests a temporary position, and thus does not reasonably notify the public 
that the individual then serving in that role was the same person under consideration for 
appointment.  See OML 2024-83.  The Board does not suggest that inclusion of the name of the 
“Interim Chief of Schools Officer” on the notices would have compromised the purpose for the 
executive session.  See OML 2016-90; OML 2011-11.  Therefore, we find that the Board 
violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to identify on both the July 19, 2023, and July 26, 
2023, meeting notices the name of the specific nonunion personnel under consideration for 
“Interim Chief of Schools Officer.”   
 
The Committee also met in executive session on September 20, 2023, and November 15, 
2023, and both m
eeting notices described the executive session discussions as falling under 
Purpose 3, identifying the “litigation” matter as an Open Meeting Law complaint.  Executive 
session Purpose 3 allows a public body “to discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining 
or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the bargaining or litigating 
position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).  
Although some administrative adjudicatory matters before an agency may be appropriate for a 
discussion of litigation strategy under Purpose 3, see OML 2015-13 (finding that Massachusetts 
Commission Against Discrimination and Architectural Access Board adjudicatory hearing 
procedures closely resemble traditional litigation such that matters before these entities are 
considered pending litigation), Open Meeting Law complaints result in an administrative review 
process conducted by our office, rather than a proceeding before a judge or an adjudicatory 
hearing officer, and are therefore not considered litigation for which strategy would need to be 
protected.  Accordingly, the Committee should not have relied on Purpose 3 for the discussions 
during the September 20, 2023, or November 15, 2023, meetings.  See OML 2019-57.   
 
However, we have consistently said that a public body may convene in executive session 
purs
uant to G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1) to review Open Meeting Law complaints under Purpose 1.  
See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1) (“Purpose 1” allows a public body “[t]o discuss the reputation, 
character, physical condition or mental health, rather than professional competence, of an 
individual, or to discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or charges brought against, 
a public officer, employee, staff member or individual”); OML 2013-82; OML 2012-119; OML 
2011-6.  Notwithstanding that the Committee’s meeting notices erroneously cited to Purpose 3 
rather than Purpose 1, because the notices specified that the Committee would discuss Open

Meeting Law complaints and that is in fact what the Committee discussed during the executive 
sessions, we find that the notices included sufficient information from which the public could

A meeting notice should, however, generally include the name of the person who filed

Law by failing to identify the name of the person who filed the Open Meeting Law complaint on 
the notice of the meeting held on September 20, 2023.   
 
IV. Minutes of the October 18, 2023, Meeting are not Sufficiently Detailed. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minut
es of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.  By substantial compliance, we mean that the 
minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not 
attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See 
OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a 
transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion 
presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55.   
 
The third complaint alleges that the minutes of the October 18, 2023, meeting are 
insufficient as they fail to accurately summarize comments made by members of the public 
during the meeting.  During the meeting, several individuals spoke on various topics throughout 
the meeting, but the minutes simply state that a specifically named individual “registered and 
spoke on this agenda item.”  A public body is not required to capture every comment made 
during a meeting in order to substantially comply with the accuracy requirement of the Open 
Meeting Law.  See OML 2021-4; OML 2019-136.  Here, however, the minutes do not include 
any summary of the public comments at all.  As such, we find that the Board created insufficient 
minutes and violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to include any summary of the 
statements.  See OML 2023-226 and OML 2023-56 (minutes were insufficient where they did 
not summarize public comments at all).   
 
We t
ake this opportunity to advise the Board of the Attorney General’s recommendations 
as to best practices for drafting meeting minutes.  In particular, when summarizing discussions, 
we encourage public bodies to include dissenting or minority opinions whenever possible.  
Where significant debate on a matter occurs and it is clear that one or more members of the body 
and/or the public disagree with the decision ultimately taken by the public body, public bodies

should note the opposing viewpoints in the meeting minutes.  See OML 2014-108; OML 2013-
47; OML 2012-29.   
 
V. The Committee Vi olated the Open Meeting Law by Failing to Review an Open 
Meeting Law Complaint. 
 
The third complaint alleges that the Board failed to review a previous Open Meeting Law 
compl
aint filed by the complainant.  The Open Meeting Law requires that within 14 days of 
receipt of a complaint, the public body review the complaint’s allegations; take remedial action, 
if appropriate; and send to the Attorney General a copy of the complaint and a description of any 
remedial action taken.  G.L.  c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  Upon the filing of an Open 
Meeting Law complaint with a public body, the chair “shall disseminate copies of the complaint 
to the members of the public body,” and “the public body shall review the complaint’s 
allegations.”  940 CMR 29.05(3), (5).  Thus, a public body must meet to review the complaint 
and formulate a response, or meet to delegate that authority, and respond to the complaint within 
14 business days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); OML 2017-69; OML 2012-90.   
 
Here, a complaint was filed with the Committee on or about October 4, 2023, and 
Att
orney Hall responded to the complaint on October 30, 2023.  However, the Committee did not 
first review the complaint and authorize Attorney Hall to respond.  The Committee explains that 
Attorney Hall thought the complaint was simply a continuation of the first complaint filed by the 
complainant in August 2023 and therefore did not warrant an additional response.  We 
understand the Committee’s confusion where the allegations raised in both the first and second 
complaints were similar.  However, the October complaint was filed using the Attorney 
General’s Open Meeting Law complaint form and included different dates of alleged violations.  
Therefore, proper procedure required that the Committee review the complaint before an 
individual, such as the chair or legal counsel, responded on its behalf.  See OML 2017-132; 
OML 2012- 95; OML 2011-6.  Once a public body has reviewed a complaint during a meeting, 
its decision to simply refer the complaint, rather than discuss its substance, is the public body’s 
prerogative.  See OML 2019-40; OML 2017-96.  In addition, forwarding a complaint to legal 
counsel prior to a meeting does not violate the law as long as the public body reviews the 
complaint and any draft response during a meeting, approves the draft response during that 
meeting, and then either decides to send the response itself or authorizes legal counsel to send 
the response.  See OML 2018-134.  We are constrained to find that the Committee violated the 
Open Meeting Law when it did not first meet to review the complaint before legal counsel 
responded.  See OML 2021-185; OML 2013-173.  
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by posting notices for meetings held on July 19, 2023, July 26, 2023, and September 20, 2023, 
that included insufficient specificity; creating insufficient minutes of a meeting held on October 
18, 2023; and failing to properly respond to the Open Meeting Law complaint dated October 4, 
2023.  We order the Committee to revise and approve the minutes of the October 18, 2023, 
meeting within thirty days of the date of this letter in accordance with the guidance provided 
above.  In addition, we order immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and
