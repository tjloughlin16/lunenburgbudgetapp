FACTS
We find the facts as follows. On December 15, 2015, the Rochester Board of 
Selectmen created the Committee of six members to formulate ideas about constructing an 
annex building at Town Hall. Since its creation until the filing of this complaint, the 
Committee has met twelve times. On October 28, 2016, the complainant submitted a request 
to the Committee for certain records, including all of its meeting minutes.
The Committee responded to the complainant’s request on January 19, 2017, 
providing him with the records Chair Cutler was able to locate at Town Hall. Although the 
Committee posted notice of most of its meetings, it did not draft minutes for most of those 
meetings because the Committee was initially informed that it was not a public body subject 
to the Open Meeting Law.4 However, the Committee did not post notice (or at least have any 
record of it) for the only two meetings for which it had drafted minutes, held on February 18, 
2016 and September 8, 2016. The Committee also compiled typed notes for a meeting held 
on May 7, 2015.5 Since the filing of this complaint, the Committee has pledged to comply 
with the Open Meeting Law’s requirements going forward.
DISCUSSION
The Open Meeting Law defines a “public body,” in relevant part, as “a multiple 
member board, commission, committee or subcommittee within the executive or legislative 
branch or within any county, district, city, region or town, however created, elected, appointed 
or otherwise constituted, established to serve a public purpose[.]” G.L. c. 30A, § 18.
Because there was some discussion between the parties about whether the Committee 
is a public body subject to the Open Meeting Law, we will outline our standard analysis to 
confirm that it is indeed a public body. To determine whether a multiple-member entity is a 
public body subject to the Open Meeting Law, there are three factors we consider:
1. The entity must be “within” government and not excluded from the definition
of “public body;”
2. The entity must be a “body” empowered to act collectively; and
3. The entity must serve a “public purpose.”

The Board of Selectmen created the Committee, thus it is an entity “within” 
government. See OML 2016-33.6 The Committee has six members who are empowered to 
formulate a collective recommendation for Town Meeting and the Board of Selectmen. 
Finally, the Committee serves a public purpose as it is tasked with formulating and presenting 
a proposal on the cost and function of adding an annex building to Town Hall at Town 
Meeting. See OML 2016-102; OML 2013-56. The Committee therefore satisfies all three 
factors and is a public body subject to the Open Meeting Law.
Because the Committee is a public body subject to the Open Meeting Law, it violated 
the law by failing to timely respond to the complainant’s request for meeting minutes. The 
Open Meeting Law requires that open session minutes “if they exist and whether approved or 
in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 days.” Id. The 
Committee did not respond to the complainant’s October 29, 2016 request until almost three 
months later, and only after the filing of this complaint. Although most of the requested 
minutes have not yet been drafted, and thus could not have been provided to the complainant, 
two of the twelve sets of requested minutes did exist. Therefore, those minutes, along with an 
explanation that the other minutes did not exist, should have been provided to the complainant 
within 10 days of his request. See OML 2016-71, OML 2015-173 (when minutes do not 
exist, the body must respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying the requestor that 
requested minutes do not exist in either approved or draft form). The Committee’s delayed 
response to the complainant’s request is a violation of the law.
Furthermore, although not alleged in the complaint, we note that the Committee 
failed to post notice of some of its meetings and failed to create and approve most of its 
minutes in a timely manner. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(c), 22(a)-(c); OML 2017-36; OML 
2016-17. The Committee posted notice of 10 of the 12 meetings that it held leading up to this 
complaint, and only created minutes for two of those meetings (the two for which it failed to 
post notice). We recommend that the Committee promptly create and approve the missing 
meeting minutes. Additionally, because notice was not posted for its February 18, 2016 and 
September 8, 2016 meetings, we recommend that the Committee convene a properly posted 
meeting for the purpose of reconsidering any topics discussed and any actions taken during 
those meetings. See OML 2017-30; OML 2016-148.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to properly respond to a request for meeting minutes. Accordingly, we order 
the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we 
caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or
