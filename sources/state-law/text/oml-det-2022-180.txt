The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the  
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghigli one v. School Committee of  
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement  
that meetings of public bodies be open and accessible to the public.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except  
when meeting in executive session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”).  
Generally, access to a meeting must include the opportunity to be physically present as well as to 
see and hear what is being discussed by the members of the public body.  See OML 2020-100; 
OML 2018-75; OML 2016-54.4 
 
On March 10, 2020, Governor Charles Baker declared a state of emergency in response 
to the
 public health emergency created by the outbreak of the 2019 novel Coronavirus (“COVID-
19”).  See Exec. Order No. 591 (Mar. 10, 2020).  Governor Baker issued an executive order 
temporarily suspending certain provisions of the Open Meeting Law during the state of 
emergency.  See Order Suspending Certain Provisions of the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, § 
20 (Mar. 12, 2020) (the “Executive Order”).5  Relevant here, the Executive Order temporarily 
suspended the requirements under the Open Meeting Law and the Attorney General’s Open 
Meeting Law regulations that a quorum of a public body, including the chair, be physically 
present at the meeting location and allows all members of a public body to participate in a 
meeting remotely, provided that the public body provide “adequate, alternative means” of public 
access, such as through telephone, internet or satellite enabled audio or video conferencing or 
any other technology that enables the public to clearly follow the proceedings of the public body 
while those activities are occurring.  See Executive Order at § 1; Chapter 20, § 20(b).  All other 
provisions of the law and regulations regarding remote participation remain in effect, including 
the requirement that “[a]ll votes taken during any meeting in which a member participates 
remotely must be by roll call vote.”  940 CMR 29.10(7)(c).

meeting.  The April 30, 2021, meeting was held as planned.  However, the Commission 
experienced technical difficulties at the beginning of the meeting and the Zoom link listed on the 
notice did not work.  Notwithstanding these technical difficulties, the Commission proceeded 
with the meeting after providing a new Zoom link to Commission members.  The meeting lasted 
less than fifteen minutes and the Commission only approved the payroll warrant. 
 
We find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law when, despite learning that 
technical problems prevented the meeting from being streamed live via the Zoom link listed on 
the notice, the Commission proceeded with the meeting.  When the Commission became aware 
that, due to technical problems, the meeting would not be streamed using the original Zoom link, 
it should have adjourned the meeting and rescheduled the meeting for a future date.  See OML 
2021-63; OML 2021-17; OML 2020-107.  Although a public body is permitted to change its 
meeting location, even less than 48 hours prior to a meeting, it must make reasonable efforts to 
notify the public of the new location, such as publicizing the access information through list 
serves, automated email notifications, social media platforms and online messaging boards, as 
well as provide rapid response to any email or phone inquiries from the public regarding access 
to the meeting.  See OML 2022-39; OML 2020-102.  Here, the Commission did not provide any 
meaningful notice to the public of the change in the meeting location and therefore should not 
have proceeded with the meeting.   
 
We commend the Commission for posting a full recording of the meeting to the Town 
website afterwards but emphasize that such action does not replace the obligation to provide 
access to a meeting in real time.  Although the Executive Order includes an exception to the 
requirement that a public body provide real-time access to its proceedings, this exception only 
applies where a public body is unable to provide real-time access to its meetings due to both 
economic hardship and despite best efforts.  See Executive Order at § 1; Chapter 20, § 20(b) 
(public body may post on its website as soon as practicable after the meeting a full and complete 
transcript, recording, or other comprehensive record but only where a public body is unable to 
provide real-time access to its meetings due to both economic hardship and despite best efforts).  
Here, the Committee only asserts that it was unable to provide real-time access despite best 
efforts.  Because the public was entitled to real-time access to the deliberations, once the 
Commission became aware of the technical difficulties and inability to provide access via the 
original Zoom link, it should have adjourned the meeting until a future date.  See OML 2021-
166; OML 2021-63; OML 2021-17.  
 
Finally, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
conduct r
oll call votes during the April 30, 2021, meeting.  The Attorney General’s Regulations 
require that, “all votes taken during any meeting in which a member participates remotely must 
be by roll call vote,” even unanimous votes.  940 CMR 29.10(7)(c); see OML 2016-132; OML 
2015-145.  Because Commission members participated remotely during this meeting, all votes 
taken during the meeting should have been conducted by roll call vote. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law when it did not provide real-time access to its April 30, 2021, meeting via the Zoom access 
information listed on the notice, and by failing to conduct roll call votes during the April 30, 
2021, meeting.  We order the Commission’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s
