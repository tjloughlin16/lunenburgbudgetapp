Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law as alleged  
in the August 2 Complaint when the notice of its July 31 meeting included the wrong date of 
minutes that it anticipated reviewing and approving at that meeting.  We decline to review the 
allegations of the remaining complaints for the reasons set forth below.  We also decline to refer 
these complaints to mediation, as requested by the Board.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaints, the Board’s responses, and the requests for further 
review. We also reviewed the notice and minutes of the Board’s July 31 meeting and minutes of 
the Board’s June 20 and June 26 meetings.  
 
DISCUSSION 
 
I. Notice of the Jul y 31 meeting was not sufficiently specific. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at 
the meeting.”  Id.  The list of topics must have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider 
a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic 
and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2023-3; OML 
2019-134; OML 2015- 35.2  Where the chair anticipates reviewing and/or approving specific 
meeting minutes during a meeting, the notice for that meeting must identify the minutes to be 
reviewed or approved.  See OML 2023-124; OML 2020-34; OML 2017-92.   
 
Notice of the July 31 meeting included the following topic: “Review and approve 6/24/24 
meeting minutes.”  The Board had not met on June 24; the minutes to be reviewed on July 31 
were those of the June 26 meeting.  We accept the Board’s representation that the incorrect date 
is attributable to typographical error.
3  Nonetheless, as the notice failed to accurately list the date 
of the minutes to be reviewed and approved, we are constrained to find a violation of the Open 
Meeting Law.  See OML 2024-109 (acknowledging that “there may be circumstances where a 
seemingly minor typographical error on a meeting notice may significantly impact how a 
reasonable member of the public understands the nature of the topic to be discussed.”) 
 
II. We decli
 ne to review the remaining complaints as they are not sufficiently specific. 
 
At least 30 days prior to the filing of a complaint with the attorney general, the 
complainant shall file a written complaint with the public body, setting forth the circumstances 
which constitute the alleged violation and giving the body an opportunity to remedy the alleged 
violation.  G.L. c. 30A § 23(b).  Allegations of Open Meeting Law violations must be made with 
a degree of specificity, as our office will not conduct broad audits of public bodies based on

date of the meetings at issue.  See OML 2024-25; OML Declination 3-23-22 (Masconomet

 The July 15 Complaints and the July 24 complaint allege that meeting minutes were 
insufficient or inaccurate in a variety of ways.  However, none of these complaints identify 
which meeting minutes are alleged to be insufficient.  In the July 15 Complaints and the July 24 
Complaint, the complainant lists the dates of the alleged violations as June 21 and June 27.4  The 
Board did not meet on June 21 or June 27.  When seeking review by our office, Mr. Kelleher 
explained that the dates of June 21 and June 27 were his estimates of when the Board approved 
minutes that did not comply with the Open Meeting Law.  Mr. Kelleher explained in his requests 
for review that the First July 15 Complaint concerns minutes of the Board’s April 24 meeting; 
the Second July 15 Complaint concerns minutes of the Board’s March 27 meeting; and the July 
24 Compliant concerns minutes of the Board’s May 22 meeting.  These meeting dates were not 
identified in the complaints, however.  Therefore, we find that the allegations of the July 15 
Complaints and the July 24 Complaint are not sufficiently specific for our office to investigate 
these complaints.  To the extent that the complainant clarified his allegations when he requested 
review, that clarification came too late, as we will not review allegations outside of the formal 
Open Meeting Law complaints.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(1); OML 2024-167; 
OML 2022-179, OML 2021-67.   
 
Moreover, we note that at least some of the allegations in these remaining complaints 
appear to be untimely.  Allegations regarding the accuracy of meeting minutes generally must be 
filed within 30 days of the date the minutes were approved.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b).  See OML 
2017-194; OML 2015-26.  Finally, we would decline to review some of the allegations regarding 
the content of the minutes in any event—including that the Board’s chair used the minutes “to 
put a spin on what happened,” mock the complainant, and omit context to make the complainant 
“appear unstable”—as such allegations, even if true, would not constitute violations of the Open 
Meeting Law.  Although we strongly encourage civility by and among public body members, 
and we have explained that public bodies should avoid editorializing in their meeting minutes, 
the Open Meeting Law does not mandate that public bodies adopt a particular tone when 
conducting business.  See OML 2023-146; OML 2022-11; OML 2015-199; OML Declination 6-
28-16 (Kingston Board of Selectmen). 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting notice of its July 31 meeting that contained a typographical error, rendering one of the 
notice topics insufficiently specific.  We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance 
with the Open Meeting Law and caution that similar future violations may be considered 
evidence of an intent to violate the law.
