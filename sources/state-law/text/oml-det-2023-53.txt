 RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Austin: 
 
This office received a complaint from George Harris on November 22, 2022, alleging 
that the Wayland Zoning Board of Appeals (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. 
c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on September 16, 2022, and 
you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated October 13, 2022, 
following an extension of time granted by this office.  The complaint alleges that the Board 
failed to timely respond to a request for executive session minutes.   
 
We resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(1), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review, and the notice, minutes and video recording of the 
Board meeting held on October 11, 2022.
1  Following our review, we find that the Board 
violated the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  A public body 
must respond to a request for executive session minutes within 10 days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  
If the public body or a designee has determined, prior to the request, that certain executive 
session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester directly, notifying him or her

of the availability of those minutes.  See OML 2013-105; OML 2013-99.  And if, at the time of a 
request, the public body has not conducted a review of the minutes to determine whether 
continued nondisclosure is warranted, the body must perform such a review and release the 
minutes, if appropriate, no later than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  In such circumstances, the body must still respond to the requestor 
within 10 days, notifying him or her that it is conducting this review.  See OML 2013-99.   
 
On August 9, 2022, the complainant sent an email to the Board requesting the executive 
session minutes of meetings held on January 19, 2021, February 3, 2021, and March 31, 2021.  
On August 26, 2022, having received no response to his request, the complainant sent an email 
to Board Chair Joshua Wernig checking on the status of his request.  On August 28, 2022, Chair 
Wernig responded that he would follow-up with town staff “so that your request can be 
processed appropriately.”  During a September 13, 2022, meeting, the Board reviewed the 
executive session minutes of the meetings held on January 19, 2021, February 3, 2021, and 
March 31, 2021, and determined that such minutes could be released.  The minutes were 
thereafter provided to the complainant.   
 
A public body must respond to a requestor within 10 days and, at the very least, notify 
him or her that it is conducting a review to determine whether minutes can be released.  See 
OML 2022-123; OML 2015-86; OML 2013-199; OML 2013-99.  We encourage public bodies to 
also include specific information about when the minutes will be reviewed.  See OML 2015-130.  
We commend the Board for conducting a review of the executive session minutes at its next 
meeting following the request.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2) (Open Meeting Law requires a 
public body to perform a review of the requested executive session minutes no later than its next 
meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first).  Nonetheless, we find that the Board violated 
the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to a request for executive session meeting minutes 
within the timeframe required by the Law.  See OML 2016-135; OML 2015-130.  We order no 
further remedial action as the Board has since reviewed and released the requested executive 
session minutes and provided them to the complainant.  
 
We take this opportunity to remind the Board that public bodies have an obligation to 
review the minutes of executive sessions at reasonable intervals to determine if continued non-
disclosure of minutes is warranted, even in the absence of a specific request, and to announce 
that determination at the next meeting following its review.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); OML 
2019-35; OML 2015-94; OML 2013-56.  Although the Open Meeting Law does not define 
“reasonable intervals,” we have found that quarterly reviews or reviews every six months satisfy 
the law.  See OML 2017-104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180.  
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to properly respond to a request for executive session minutes.  We order immediate and 
future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations 
could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
