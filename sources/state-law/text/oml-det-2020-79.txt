 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Corbo: 
 
This office received a complaint from Attorney Mark O’Brien on April 21, 2020, alleging 
that the Lincoln Board of Health (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on January 28, 2020, and you 
responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated February 18, 2020.  The complaint alleges that 
the Board’s December 18, 2019, meeting minutes are inaccurate.   
  
We resolve this matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, the complaint filed 
with our office requesting further review, and the notices and open session minutes of the 
Board’s December 18, 2019, and February 12, 2020, meetings.   
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.

The complaint alleges that the December 18, 2019, meeting minutes include inaccurate 
statements with respect to the Board’s discussion of a pending Land Court complaint of odors 
coming from a property housing horses.  Specifically, the complaint alleges that the minutes 
incorrectly identify the Land Court complaint as having been filed by the residents of Brooks 
Hill Road rather than the Oak Ridge Realty Trust.2  In response to the Open Meeting Law 
complaint, the Board reviewed and revised its December 18, 2019, meeting minutes during its 
February 12, 2020, meeting.  The revised minutes of the December 18, 2019, meeting now read 
as follows: “The members were informed that the Land Court will hear a motion to dismiss the 
court case between the Board of Health and Oak Ridge Realty Trust on January 27, 2020.  Town 
Council is also looking into the Board’s obligation to license barns.  The Town has never 
licensed barns before but this was brought up by Oak Ridge Realty Trust during Public Records 
requests.”   
 
We note that, at the time the complaint was filed with our office, the Board had already 
revised the minutes at issue and the complaint filed with our office “has no dispute with the new 
minutes.”  Rather, the complaint for further review requests that the original minutes of the 
Board’s December 18, 2019, meeting be removed from the municipal website.  We acknowledge 
that both the original and amended minutes of the December 18, 2019, meeting are posted to the 
Town’s website.
3  However, the Open Meeting Law does not require that public bodies post their 
minutes to a website.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c); OML 2013-103.  Nonetheless, the Board has 
chosen to post its meeting minutes on its website and has notified the complainant that minutes 
from its meetings are available at that location.  While we do not find a violation of the Open 
Meeting Law in the circumstances here and therefore do not have authority to order any remedial 
action, we caution public bodies that posting two sets of minutes of the same meeting could be 
misleading to the public if there is no specific indication that minutes have been revised or 
amended.4 
 
Finally, although not specifically raised in the complaint, we note our concern with the 
executive session topic listed on the Board’s December 18, 2019, meeting notice.  A public body

may enter a closed, executive session for any of ten enumerated purposes, provided that the chair 
of the public body first announces in open session the purpose for the executive session, “stating 
all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which the executive 
session was called.”  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), 21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for the N. 
Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the 
reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification 
given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the 
public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  This level of detail 
about the executive session topic must also be included in the meeting notice.  See G.L. c. 30A,  
§ 20(b); OML 2016-72.  
 
Here, the notice stated that the Board would convene in executive session under purpose 
3 to discuss “strategy with respect to ongoing litigation and not to return to the regular meeting.”  
A public body should include the specific case name of the litigation matter it plans to discuss 
unless doing so would compromise the Board’s litigating position.  See OML 2018-11.  The 
Board does not suggest that inclusion of such information would have compromised the purpose 
for the executive session.  In fact, the Board decided not to convene in executive session on 
December 18, 2019, and instead discussed two litigation matters in open session.  The Open 
Meeting Law does not require public bodies to meet in executive session but rather, if a public 
body chooses to meet in executive session, it must do so under one of the enumerated purposes.  
See OML 2017-18.  Moreover, the topic “ongoing litigation” would not have been sufficiently 
specific even for discussion in open session.  Public bodies are required to list topics on a notice 
with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1) (b).  We generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a 
reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of 
the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.  We remind the Board of its obligations under 
the law and advise that the meeting notice should include the names of the litigation matters that 
are to be discussed in either open or executive session.     
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law as alleged.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  
This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or 
the Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
