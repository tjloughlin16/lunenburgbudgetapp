This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on May 12, 2025, alleging that the 
Ludlow Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The 
complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about April 19, 2025, and you responded to 
the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated May 12, 2025.1  The complaint alleges that 
the Board posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a meeting held on April 15, failed to 
announce that the April 15 meeting was being recorded, and created inaccurate minutes of the 
Board meeting held on April 1.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by posting 
a notice with insufficient specificity for the April 15 meeting and by failing to announce that the 
meeting was being recorded.  We decline to review the third allegation for the reasons stated 
below.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response 
to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, 
we reviewed the notices, minutes and video recordings of the Board meetings held on April 1 
and April 15.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Board duly posted notice of a meeting to be held on 
April 15 at 5:30 P.M.  The notice listed seven main topic headings, one of which was “New 
Business.”  This topic heading included several specific item descriptions that further advised the 
public of the topics to be discussed, including “Board to approve Annual Appointments.”   
 
The April 15 meeting was held as planned and Ludlow Community Television recorded 
the meeting.  During the meeting, the Board appointed numerous individuals to 24 different 
public bodies and appointed specific individuals to 19 town department positions.  The Board 
also approved the minutes of the meeting held on April 1. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at least 
48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays” and that every 
notice include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are 
required to list topics on a notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of 
the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally consider a topic 
sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand 
the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2018-7; OML 2015-35.
3  When 
reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not 
review a meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we 
review what was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently 
detailed to make the public aware of the discussion topics. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Board posted a notice with insufficient specificity for the 
April 15 meeting where the notice did not list the names of the individuals being considered for 
“Annual Appointments.”  We have consistently stated that the names of applicants for 
appointment which are known 48 hours in advance of a meeting should be included on a meeting 
notice.  See OML 2022-217; 2019-24; OML 2016-150; OML 2013-163.  During the April 15 
meeting, the Board appointed numerous individuals to 24 different public bodies and appointed 
specific individuals to 19 positions within the town.  The Board has not claimed that the 
appointments were not reasonably anticipated 48 hours in advance of the April 15 meeting.  As 
such, we find that the notice for the Board meeting held on April 15 should have included the 
names of the individuals to be considered by the Board.  See OML 2025-23.  Accordingly, the 
Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to include this information on the notice.  We 
take this opportunity to clarify that the list of candidates for appointment must be included within 
the notice document itself, even if it is several pages long; it is not sufficient to reference a 
separate list that is available elsewhere.  See OML 2024-225; OML 2022-68; OML 2020-21; 
OML 2019-22.  In the event of a similar situation, the Board may include as part of the notice 
document a list of each public body or town department for which an appointment would be

Next, the complaint alleges that the Board’s Chair did not announce that the April 15 
meeting was being recorded.  The Open Meeting Law requires that “after notifying the chair of 
the public body, any person may make a video or audio recording of an open session of a 
meeting pf a public body, or may transmit the meeting through any medium, subject to 
reasonable requirements of the chair as to the number, placement and operation of equipment 
used so as not to interfere with the conduct of the meeting.  At the beginning of the meeting, the 
chair shall inform other attendees of any such recordings.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(f).  Here, Ludlow 
Community Television recorded the meeting, and thus the Chair was required to inform 
attendees that the meeting was being recorded.  See OML 2023-111.  The requirement that the 
chair inform attendees of any recording includes any recording made by members of the public 
body itself, as well as recordings made for public broadcasting or administrative purposes, such 
as assisting in the drafting of meeting minutes.  See OML 2023-111; OML 2019-129; OML 
2016-155; OML 2013-136.  Because the Chair did not announce that the meeting was being 
recorded, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law.  We commend the Board for 
its commitment to comply with the Law in the future.  
 
Finally, the complaint alleges that the minutes of the Board meeting held on April 1 do 
not “properly record the votes taken” during the meeting but does not identify in what way the 
votes were improper.

22(a).  In certain circumstances—including when a public body member participates in a 
meeting remotely, when a public body votes to enter executive session, and when in executive 
session—votes must be taken by roll call, and the roll call votes recorded accordingly in the 
minutes.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(b)(2), 22(b); 940 CMR 29.10(7)(c); OML 2025-54; OML 
2015- 131; OML 2013-195.  We note here that the votes taken during the Board’s April 1 
meeting were not required to be—and were not—conducted by roll call and therefore the 
minutes need not document how each member voted.  Although almost every vote taken during 
the April 1 meeting was unanimous and was recorded in the minutes as “Motion passed 4-0,”  
one Board member abstained twice from voting and these two votes were recorded in the 
minutes as “Motion passed 3-0-1.”  We recommend that in such instances where a member 
abstains from voting, the minutes should also include the name of the member who did not vote. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting a notice with insufficient specificity for the April 15 meeting where the notice did not 
include the names of the individuals to be appointed and by failing to announce that the meeting 
was being recorded.  We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s 
requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.   
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the
