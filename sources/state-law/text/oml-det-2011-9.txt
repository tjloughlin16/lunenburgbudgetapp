RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Dr. Jurist: 
On July 13, 2010 our office received a complaint filed by Mr. George Harris against the 
Wayland School Conmnttee ("the Committee") that alleges a series of violations of the Open 
Meeting Law between the dates June 8, 2009 and May 3, 2010. Specifically, Mr. Harris alleges 
that on twenty-five occasions, the Committee failed to appropriately state the purpose for 
entering executive session and did not conduct a roll call vote of its members prior to entering 
executive session, in violation of the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 39, §23B (now repealed). The 
complaint was initially filed with the Middlesex District Attorney's Office, which had 
jurisdiction over enforcement of the Open Meeting Law during the time of the meetings, on May 
26, 2010. The Middlesex District Attorney's Office directed Mr. Harris to file the complaint with 
the Attorney General's Office in a letter dated June 8, 2010. The Committee responded to Mr. 
Harris's complaint in an email sent to Mr. Harris on or about June 28, 2010. 
Our office reviewed the initial complaint filed with the Middlesex District Attorney's 
Office; the complaint filed with our office; the response by the Committee to Mr. Harris; and the 
minutes and corresponding video recordings of the Committee meetings on June 8, 2009; June 
15, 2009; June 22, 2009; July 27, 2009; August 31, 2009; September 21, 2009; September 29, 
2009; October 13, 2009; November 9, 2009; November 18, 2009; November 30, 2009; 
===PAGE 2===
December 7, 2009; December 21, 2009; January 14, 2010; January 19, 2010; January 25, 2010; 
February 4, 2010; February 8, 2010; March 1, 2010; March 8, 2010; March 22, 2010; April 5, 
2010; April 13, 2010; April 26, 2010; and May 3, 2010. 
We acknowledge that at the time the alleged violations occurred, the revised Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§18-25, was not yet in effect. Therefore we apply the version of the 
Open Meeting Law in effect at the time of the alleged violations, G.L. c. 39, §23B. 
Based upon our review of the information above, we find that the Wayland School 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 39, §23B by failing to adequately state or 
state with sufficient specificity the reason for entering executive session on twenty-five 
occasions; failing to conduct a roll call vote prior to entering executive session; and inaccurately 
recording in the minutes that the Committee took a roll call vote. 
With regard to the first allegation, that the Committee failed to adequately state or state 
with necessary specificity the purpose for entering executive session, G.L. c. 39, §23B states in 
relevant part that "[n]o executive session shall be held until...the presiding officer has cited the 
purpose for an executive session." The Middlesex District Attorney, who enforced the Open 
Meeting Law at the time of the meetings in question, recommended in its printed materials that 
governmental bodies provide sufficient information to inform the public what, and with whom, 
the governmental body was deliberating. See Middlesex District Attorney's Office Guide to the 
Open Meeting Law, pages 21, 28, 30 (January, 2007 edition). Here, the Committee consistently 
neglected to provide sufficiently specific information. For instance, the Committee cited 
"collective bargaining" as a reason to enter executive session during seventeen meetings, but 
never stated with what union or group of employees it was negotiating or preparing to negotiate. 
Moreover, on ten occasions, the Committee states "personnel" as the reason for entering 
executive session. However, "personnel" is not now, nor was it ever, an appropriate reason to 
enter executive session. While several of the delineated purposes under both the old law, G.L. c. 
39, §23B, and the revised law, G.L. c. 30A, §21, exist to allow bodies to deliberate on personnel-
related issues, "personnel" by itself is not a statutorily authorized purpose to enter executive 
session. The Open Meeting Law requires "a precise statement of the reason for convening in 
executive session...because that is the only notification given to the public that the school 
cominittee would conduct business in private, and the only way the public would know if the 
reason for doing so was proper or improper." District Attorney for the Northern District v.  
School Committee of Wayland,  455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009). By failing to state the purpose for 
which the body entered executive session with sufficient specificity to inform the public of what 
business it was deliberating upon and failing to state a valid reason for entering executive 
session, the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law. 
With regard to the second and third allegations, the complainant asserts that the 
Committee failed to conduct a roll call vote prior to convening in executive session during the 
meetings on the dates listed above, in violation of G.L.c. 39, §23B. The complainant further 
alleges that the minutes of the above listed meetings erroneously reflect that a roll call vote was 
taken when, in fact, none was conducted, in violation of the law. Based upon our review of the 
video recordings of the meetings between June 8, 2009-May 3, 2010 and the corresponding 
meeting minutes, we agree. 
The Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 39, §23B, states in relevant part that: 
===PAGE 3===
No executive session shall be held until the governmental body has first 
convened in open session...a majority of the members have voted to go 
into executive session and the vote of each member is recorded on a roll 
call vote and entered into the minutes...A governmental body shall maintain 
accurate records of its meetings, setting forth the date, time, place, members 
present or absent and action taken at each meeting, including executive sessions... 
Here, as the video recordings we reviewed show, the Committee followed a general 
pattern of convening in Open session; stating a general purpose for entering into executive 
session; and conducting a vote of the "ayes" and "nays." While the votes were unanimous in 
each instance, and the minutes reflect the unanimity of the Committee, this does not excuse the 
Committee from the explicit requirements of the Open Meeting Law. By failing to conduct a roll 
call vote of the Committee and failing to accurately record the actions taken during the meeting, 
the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 39, §23B. 
• We note that, though not applicable to this complaint, the revisions to the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§18-25, which took effect on July 1, 2010, retain the requirements for 
entering, conducting and recording executive sessions that were included in G.L. c. 39, §23B. 
The revisions to the Open Meeting Law also include the requirement that the chair "stat[e] all 
subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which the executive session 
was called," in addition to stating the purpose for the executive session. G.L. c. 30A, §21(b)(3). 
Though, as noted above, it has always been recommended that public bodies provide sufficiently 
specific information to enable a member of the public to understand the business that the body 
intends to transact in executive session, this revision codifies that recommendation. 
The Committee, in its response to the complainant, has indicated that it understands these 
requirements and intends to "fully and completely comply with the new Open Meeting Law." 
See Response. Specifically, the Committee has stated that it will be more specific about the 
purpose for entering executive session and will ensure that it conducts roll call votes to enter 
executive session. Id. We appreciate this acknowledgment on the, part of the Committee and 
anticipate that it will follow through on its commitment. 
As stated above, at the time these violations of the Open Meeting Law occurred, G.L. c. 
30A, §§18-25 was not yet in effect. Therefore, we acknowledge that in this instance our authority 
to order remedial action by the Committee •is limited by the law in effect at the time of the 
violations, G.L. c. 39, §23B. While we cannot order the Committee to take remedial action, we 
strongly recommend that the Committee review and amend its minutes to accurately reflect the 
business transacted during the twenty-five meetings in question. This will entail reviewing its 
executive session minutes and editing the open session minutes to accurately state the purpose 
for entering executive session. In addition, the open session minutes should be edited to 
accurately reflect the votes taken prior to entering executive session.
