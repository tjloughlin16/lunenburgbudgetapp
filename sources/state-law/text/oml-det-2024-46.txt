 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints 
 
Dear Attorney Galvin: 
 
This office received three complaints from Patricia Sterite on February 7, 2024, alleging 
that the Everett Superintendent Search Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaints were filed with the Committee between September 
27, 2023, and December 13, 2023, and you responded to all three complaints, on behalf of the 
Committee, by letter dated January 19, 2024.  The complaints allege that the Committee failed to 
conduct certain procedural requirements prior to convening in executive session during meetings 
held on November 29, 2023, and December 9, 2023; that Committee members failed to state at 
the beginning of the executive sessions that no other person was present in the member’s remote 
location; that minutes of meetings have not been posted; that meetings are not accessible; and 
that Committee members do not turn on their cameras when participating remotely. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to vote by roll call prior to convening in executive session and failing to announce that no 
other person was present in the members’ remote locations.  We find that the Committee did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaints, the Committee’s response to the complaints, and the complaint 
filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notices and video

DISCUSSION 
 
I.   We Find that the Committee Failed to Com
 ply with Certain of the 
Procedural Requirements for Convening in Executive Session, Including   
Failing to Vote by Roll Call and Failing to Announce that No Other 
Person was Present in the Members’ Remote Location. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
berations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session is convened.  
See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for 
any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened 
in an open session, that a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive 
session, that the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and 
the chair has publicly announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of 
the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.
2    
 
One permissible reason to convene in executive session is to “consider or interview 
applicants for employment or appointment by a preliminary screening committee if the chair 
declares that an open meeting will have a detrimental effect in obtaining qualified applicants; 
provided, however, that this clause shall not apply to any meeting, including meetings of a 
preliminary screening committee, to consider and interview applicants who have passed a prior 
preliminary screening.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(8) (“Purpose 8”).   
 
The Committee held meetings on N
 ovember 29, 2023, and December 9, 2023, during 
which all members participated remotely.  During each meeting, the Committee convened in 
open session, announced that it would convene in executive session pursuant to Purpose 8 to 
consider or interview applicants for employment or appointment by a preliminary screening 
committee, and stated that it would not resume in open session following the executive session.  
However, the Committee failed to conduct a roll call vote; instead, Chair Michael Mangan 
simply asked, “All those in favor signify by saying Aye” and members responded by saying 
“Aye.”  A roll call vote is “the act or an instance of calling off a list of names.”  Webster’s Third 
New International Dictionary of the English Language, 683 (unabridged ed. 1961).  This 
requirement is intended to create a clear record of what occurred and to reflect the significance of 
holding a discussion behind closed doors.  See OML 2015-131; OML 2014-94; OML 2012-10.

The Open Meeting Law regulations further require that “[a]t the start of the meeting, the 
chair shall announce the name of any member who will be participating remotely.”  940 CMR 
29.10(7)(b).  While Chair Mangan did announce the name of each Committee member who was 
participating remotely during the November 29, 2023, and December 9, 2023, meetings, he 
neglected to announce his own name.  Moreover, Chair Mangan’s name was not displayed at the 
bottom of his virtual tile, like some of the other Committee members, which may have relieved 
him of the requirement to announce his own name at the start of the meeting.  See OML 2020-
138.   
 
The complaints also allege that Committee members did not announce that no other 
pers
on was present or able to hear the executive session discussions prior to convening in 
executive session on November 29, 2023, and December 9, 2023.  The Open Meeting Law 
regulations state that a “member participating remotely may participate in an executive session, 
but shall state at the start of any such session that no other person is present and/or able to hear 
the discussion at the remote location.”  940 CMR 29.10(7)(d).  This statement does not have to 
occur in open session, but rather at the beginning of the executive session.  See id.  Nonetheless, 
the Committee acknowledges that members did not make such a statement during the November 
29, 2023, and December 9, 2023, executive sessions.  As such, we find that the Committee 
violated the Open Meeting Law for failing to state at the beginning of the executive sessions that 
no other person was present in the members’ remote location.  Although neither the Open 
Meeting Law nor the regulations expressly require that this declaration made at the start of the 
executive session be documented in the minutes, we encourage public bodies to record such 
statement in the minutes since that is the only way for members of the public to know that the 
declaration was made.  See OML 2019-91. 
 
As a result of these procedural violations, the Committee may not continue to withhold 
the m
inutes of the November 29, 2023, or December 9, 2023, executive sessions under the Open 
Meeting Law.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(1) (minutes of an executive session may be withheld from 
disclosure to the public as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of an executive 
session, provided that the executive session was held in compliance with section 21 of the Open 
Meeting Law); OML 2014-42; OML 2014-30; OML 2014-17.  The Committee may continue to 
withhold the minutes from the general public, in whole or in part, under any applicable 
exemptions to the Public Records Law or the attorney/client privilege.  See OML 2016-121. 
 
II.  The Open Meeting Law Requires that Meeting Minutes be Created and  
Maintained but Does Not Require that Minutes be Posted to a Website. 
 
The complaints allege that the Committee has not posted minutes of meetings held 
between September and December.  Public bodies must create and maintain accurate minutes of 
all meetings.  G.L. c. 30A,§ 22(a).  The minutes of an open session, if they exist and whether 
approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 days.

G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  If minutes do not yet exist at the time of a request, the public body is still 
required to respond to the request within 10 days with an explanation of whether the minutes do 
or do not exist in either approved or draft form.  See OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; OML 2015-
173.  
 
A public body must also respond to a request for executive session minutes within 10 
days.  G
.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  If the public body or a designee has determined, prior to the 
request, that certain executive session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester 
directly, notifying him or her of the availability of those minutes.  See OML 2013-105; OML 
2013-99.  And if, at the time of a request, the public body has not conducted a review of the 
minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure is warranted, the body must perform such 
a review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no later than its next meeting or within 30 days, 
whichever occurs first.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  In such circumstances, the body must still 
respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her that it is conducting this review.  
See OML 2013-99.   
 
Executive session minutes may be withheld from disclosure to the public “as long as 
public
ation may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 22(f).  When the purpose for a valid executive session has been served, the minutes and 
any documents or exhibits used at the session must be disclosed unless the attorney-client 
privilege or an exemption to the public records law applies to withhold them, in whole or in part, 
from disclosure.  See id.  Public bodies have an obligation to review the minutes of executive 
sessions at reasonable intervals to determine if continued non-disclosure of minutes is warranted, 
and to announce that determination at the next meeting following its review.  See G.L. c. 30A,    
§ 22(g)(1); OML 2015-94; OML 2013-56.  Although the Open Meeting Law does not define 
“reasonable intervals,” we have found that quarterly reviews or reviews every six months satisfy 
the law.  See OML 2017-104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180. 
   
We note that a request for meeting minutes that is directed to a public body will fall 
under t
he Open Meeting Law.  On the other hand, a request for minutes that is directed to a 
municipal employee, such as the Town Clerk, will likely fall under the Public Records Law.  See 
OML 2019-126; OML 2019-68.  A request for meeting minutes made under the Public Records 
Law requires a response within 10 business days, whereas the Open Meeting Law requires that 
public bodies respond to a request for minutes within 10 calendar days.  See G.L. c. 66, § 10; 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (g).  Moreover, the Open Meeting Law does not govern a public body’s 
response to a request for records other than meeting minutes.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 22(c), (e); 
OML Declination 8-19-13 (Southwick Board of Selectmen).  Therefore, failure to timely respond 
to requests for documents other than minutes concerns a potential violation of the Public Records 
Law, rather than the Open Meeting Law. 
 
Fina
lly, the Open Meeting Law does not require that public bodies post their minutes to a 
website or that minutes be submitted to the Town Clerk, but merely that meeting minutes be 
made available within 10 days of a request.

III.  We Fin
 d that Committee Meetings Were Accessible. 
 
At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement that meetings of public bodies be 
open and a
ccessible to the public.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except when meeting in executive 
session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”).  Generally, access to a 
meeting must include the opportunity to be physically present, as well as to see and hear what is 
being discussed by the members of the public body.  See OML 2020-100; OML 2017-135; OML 
2016-54.  In order for a meeting to be truly open to the public, the meeting venue must be 
accessible to the public for the duration of the meeting.  See OML 2018-51; OML 2017-172; 
OML 2014-18. 
 
Chapter 20 of the Acts of 2021, entitled An Act Relative to Extending Certain COVID-19 
Measures Adopted During the State of Emergency (“Chapter 20”), suspended the requirements 
under the Open Meeting Law and the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations that a 
quorum of a public body, including the chair, be physically present at the meeting location and 
allows all members of a public body to participate in a meeting remotely, provided that the 
public body provides “adequate, alternative means” of public access, such as through telephone, 
internet or satellite enabled audio or video conferencing or any other technology that enables the 
public to clearly follow the proceedings of the public body while those activities are occurring.
5  
See Chapter 20, § 20(b); OML 2021-135.  Public bodies are allowed to provide the public with 
“adequate, alternative” access to meetings instead of providing in-person access but Chapter 20 
does not require public bodies to provide “adequate, alternative” access if in-person access is 
provided. 
 
The complaints allege that the Committee meetings held on September 27, 2023, 
November 29, 2023, and December 9, 2023, were not accessible because the meetings were only 
broadcast live on Facebook, which requires an account.  Here, the notices posted for the three 
meetings indicated the location of the meeting “for the viewing public” to be 
https://m.facebook.com/cityofeverettma/.  Although the Committee does not dispute that in order 
to watch Committee meetings, an individual would need a Facebook account, we disagree that 
this renders Committee meetings inaccessible.  In fact, our office has published guidance 
explaining that “‘[a]dequate, alternative means of access’ could include Zoom, a high-capacity 
telephone conference line, Facebook Live, YouTube Live, and broadcasting on live TV, 
including local cable access television.”  See Attorney General’s Updated guidance on holding 
meetings pursuant to the Act Extending Certain COVID-19 Measures.6  Here, we find that the 
Committee’s September 27, 2023, November 29, 2023, and December 9, 2023, meetings were 
accessible to the public via Facebook, such that the general public could clearly follow the 
proceedings of the Committee in real time as required by the Open Meeting Law and Chapter 20.  
As for the requirement that public bodies offer alternative means of access to their meetings 
“without subscription, toll, or similar charge to the public,” we find that the Committee’s use of

Next, we address the allegations that the Committee does not provide “language access” 
for its meetings and does not post notices or other information in languages other than English.  
The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings be accessible to all individuals, including the 
ability to see and hear what is being discussed by members of the public body at the time such 
deliberations are occurring.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a).  While the Open Meeting Law does not 
specifically require that a public body broadcast a meeting in different languages, we encourage 
the Committee to provide access to a meeting in a language other than English if notified that 
individuals need such accommodation.  
 
With respect to notices, the Ope
 n Meeting Law provides that notices “shall be printed in 
a legible, easily understandable format and shall contain the date, time and place of such meeting 
and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  
G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  We have previously stated that a public body should ensure that meeting 
notices are at least 12-point standard font, with an ideal font size of between 16 and 18-point 
standard font.  See OML 2018-75; OML 2018-55; OML 2016-133.  And, if a notice is illegible 
to a particular person, a public body should provide an accommodation to that person, if notified.  
See 940 CMR 29.03(2)(4) (requiring that a copy of each notice also be accessible to the public in 
the municipal clerk's office during the clerk’s business hours).  However, the Open Meeting Law 
does not specifically require that notices be posted in a language other than English but we 
encourage the Committee to do so.  
 
Finally, the Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body attach supporting 
docume
nts to a meeting notice or that it publicly post a “meeting packet” that contains such 
documents.  See OML 2022-24; OML 2022-19; OML 2021-181.  To the contrary, the meeting 
notice itself must contain the level of specificity that the Open Meeting Law requires; posting 
attachments does not cure an otherwise defective notice.  See OML 2021-138; OML 2021-57; 
OML 2019-22. 
 
IV.  We Encourage Public Bodies to Use Video Technology During Remote 
Meetings and Decline to Find a Violation When Only Some Members are 
Visible When Speaking.  
 
Finally, we address the allegation that Committee members do not always turn on their 
cameras while participating remotely.  The Attorney General’s 2017 Open Meeting Law 
regulations regarding remote participation state that “[w]hen video technology is in use, the 
remote participant shall be clearly visible to all persons present in the meeting location.”  940 
CMR 29.10(6)(b).  These 2017 regulations contemplated a situation in which at least a quorum 
of the public body members, as well as members of the public, would attend a meeting at a 
physical meeting location, and one or more public body members could participate remotely if 
physical attendance at the meeting would be unreasonably difficult.  See 940 CMR 29.10(4) and 
(5).  Chapter 20 allows public bodies to hold meetings at which some or all public body members 
attend remotely, and/or access to the public is provided via remote means rather than in-person.   
 
===PAGE 7===
7 
 
The Attorney General encourages public bodies to use video technology whenever 
possi
ble to enhance the ability of the public to follow the proceedings of a public body remotely.  
However, we are cognizant that some public body members may not have personal technology 
available to participate in a meeting by video, and that public body members who utilize video 
may need to step off camera briefly.  A strict requirement that if video technology is in use all 
public body members must be visible on camera at all times could result in public bodies opting 
not to use video technology at all, which surely would not serve the Open Meeting Law’s 
transparency goals.  Therefore, we decline to find a violation of the Open Meeting Law where, as 
here, video technology is generally in use for a meeting, but some members of the public body 
are not visible at all times.  We note that when Committee members spoke, their tile box would 
light up, providing a visual indicator of who was speaking.  Thus, even if a Committee member’s 
camera was not in use, it was still possible at times to follow who was speaking.  Nonetheless, 
the Attorney General recommends, as a best practice, that members of public bodies turn on their 
video cameras, especially while speaking, whenever possible.  See OML 2021-2.  We otherwise 
find, after reviewing videos of the meetings held on  November 29, 2023, and December 9, 2023, 
that the public could clearly hear the proceedings as Committee members were audible to the 
observing public when speaking, and the audio quality was clear and not obfuscated.  See OML 
2021-160. 
 
In her request for further review, the complainant alleges that the Committee did not 
r
eview or timely respond to Open Meeting Law complaints.  We generally decline to review an 
allegation that was not included within the original complaint to the Committee because the 
Committee has not had an opportunity to respond to it.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 
29.05(3).  We find that this allegation was not clearly raised in the original complaints and thus 
we do not review its merits.  However, we take this opportunity to advise the Committee that the 
Open Meeting Law requires that within 14 days of receipt of a complaint, the public body review 
the complaint’s allegations; take remedial action, if appropriate; and send to the Attorney 
General a copy of the complaint and a description of any remedial action taken.  G.L. c. 30A, § 
23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  A public body must meet to review the complaint and formulate a 
response, or meet to delegate that authority, and respond to the complaint within 14 business 
days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); OML 2017-69; OML 2012-90.  Proper procedure requires that a 
public body review a complaint before authorizing legal counsel to respond on its behalf.  See 
OML 2017-132; OML 2012- 95; OML 2011-6.  Once a public body has reviewed a complaint 
during a meeting, its decision to simply refer the complaint, rather than discuss its substance, is 
the public body’s prerogative.  See OML 2019-40; OML 2017-96.  Moreover, upon the filing of 
an Open Meeting Law complaint with a public body, the chair “shall disseminate copies of the 
complaint to the members of the public body.”  940 CMR 29.05(3).  The purpose of this 
requirement is to make sure all members of the public body are aware of the allegations in the 
complaint.  See OML 2016-88.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to vote by roll call prior to convening in executive session and failing to announce that 
no other person was present in the members’ remote location.  We order the Committee to 
release the minutes of the November 29, 2023, or December 9, 2023, executive sessions within
