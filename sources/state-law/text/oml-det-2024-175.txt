FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Committee, which was established under 
Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 39, Section 16, consists of 20 to 30 registered voters of the 
Town of Brookline (the “Town”).  A quorum of the Committee is 15 members.  The primary 
function of the Committee is to review and make nonbinding recommendations on all matters to 
be considered by Town Meeting.   
 
Brookline’s representative Town Meeting is comprised of 255 elected Town Meeting 
members with fifteen registered voters elected from each of Brookline’s 17 precincts, along with 
eight at-large members, including the Moderator, the Town Clerk, the five members of the Select 
Board, and any residents who are the elected representatives of the Town in the General Court of 
the Commonwealth of Massachusetts.  The Town Meeting Members Association (“TMMA”) 
Listserv is open to the 269 elected and at-large Town Meeting members, the Committee 
members, and two Assistant Moderators appointed by the Moderator, and includes over 280 
individuals.  Although the TMMA Listserv is expressly intended for the “discussion of issues 
pertaining to Town Meeting, the Warrant, and any related Town, precinct, or neighborhood 
issues,” it is often used to discuss broader issues involving the Town.  The TMMA Listserv is 
not accessible to the general public. 
 
In July 2022, the Committee discussed a proposed debt exclusion to fund the construction 
of a new John Pierce School.
2  The Committee recommended that the Select Board postpone a 
vote on the Pierce School debt exclusion by three months, so as to permit time to address 
financial issues, as well as the need for a new school in light of changing enrollments and the 
receptiveness of voters to a tax override and possible debt exclusion. 
 
During separate meetings held in September 2022, the Pierce School Building Committee 
and School Committee each unanimously approved a projected budget for a new John Pierce 
School of approximately $199.6 million, while the Select Board unanimously approved a budget 
of $210 million.  
 
The ballot for the annual Brookline Town election, which was scheduled for May 2, 
2023, included three questions.  The first two questions asked residents whether they would fund 
reconstruction and renovation of the John Pierce School by increasing property taxes, whether 
they would approve a permanent property tax increase to fund the costs of Brookline Public 
Schools, and whether they would fund the costs of a municipal composting collection service.
3  
In the months leading up to the annual election, the Committee discussed, during properly posted 
meetings, the amount of funds needed, approximately $209 million, for reconstruction and 
renovation of the John Pierce School.

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deli
beration and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session 
is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a 
deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 18.  The law defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any 
medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public 
business within its jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the 
distribution of other procedural meeting [sic] or the distribution of reports or documents that may 
be discussed at a meeting, provided than no opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  For the

The complaint alleges that the Committee improperly deliberated when Committee 
mem
bers posted on the TMMA Listserv their thoughts and opinions on matters within the 
jurisdiction of the Committee.  The Open Meeting Law does not restrict an individual’s right to 
make comments to the general public, including through social media.  See OML 2017-111.6  
However, if a member of a public body were to communicate directly with a quorum of the 
public body on a matter within the public body’s jurisdiction through social media, such 
communication may violate the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2020-139; OML 2017-192.  
Posting to a Listserv that includes a quorum of members of a public body is not in and of itself a 
violation of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-145, OML 2017-111.  Nonetheless, public 
body members should use particular care to avoid deliberation when writing a post that may be 
visible to other members of the public body, especially in a “closed” Listserv and in particular if 
the closed group is relatively small.  See OML 2018-153.  In order to differentiate between social 
media posts that are directed to the public and those that are directed to other members of the 
public body, we must examine the communicator’s intent.  See OML 2017-111; OML 2015-15.  
 
We reviewed the 43 posts provided to this office as part of the complaint and which 
occur
red between April 18, 2023, and May 18, 2023.  Though certain of the posts related to the 
cost
s and appropriations associated with the construction of a new John Pierce School (# 6, 11, 
13, 18, 33, 35, 38), which was a matter within the jurisdiction of the Committee, we find no 
evidence that the posts involved communication directed to a quorum of the Committee, or that a 
quorum of Committee members responded to the posts of other members.  In fact, several of the 
posts were directed to Town Meeting members or Select Board members.  Moreover, we find no 
evidence that other members of the Committee commented on these posts, and, indeed, it is not 
evident that other Committee members even read these comments.  Given the lack of evidence 
showing deliberation between or among a quorum of the Committee, we find no violation of the 
Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-145. 
 
We find that the remaining posts all related to matters that either did not fall within the 
jurisdiction of the Committee or were administrative or procedural in nature and did not include 
the opinions of Committee members.  For example, posts pertaining to the content of the mailer 
that
 was sent to residents describing the ballot questions (# 3, 15-17, 27, 29) was a matter within 
the jurisdiction of the Select Board, not the Committee.  See OML 2023-166; OML 2012-64.  
Posts pertaining to information that was available on various town and school websites, and 
which summarized information from independent, factual sources (# 4, 5, 7, 8-10, 12, 24-26, 28) 
fits within the narrow exemption to the definition of deliberation and is considered the 
distribution of reports or documents.  See OML 2024-16; OML 2023-126; OML 2016-172.  
Moreover, posts that shared comments of constituents regarding the construction of a new John 
Pierce School (# 20-22, 31, 34) and which did not otherwise reflect the opinions of Committee 
members do not constitute deliberation.  See OML 2023-156; OML 2016-172.  Posts pertaining 
to the rules for participating on the TMMA Listserv and whether Committee members might be

Although we find that the Committee did not deliberate outside of a properly posted 
meeting
, we take this opportunity to remind the Committee that social media may not be used to 
facilitate discussion between or among a quorum of its members.  Where a social media group is 
closed to the public—as the TMMA Listserv was in this case—it raises concerns that 
conversations may be happening outside of public view.  When comments are made on an open 
social media group, those comments are more likely to be targeted towards a general public.  
While the closed group at issue here certainly had a large number of members, it is nonetheless 
closed to the public such that when comments are made in a closed group, it is reasonably 
inferable that posts are directed solely at the members of that group.  See OML 2018-145.  
Therefore, such communications create at least the appearance of an Open Meeting Law 
violation, and we caution public bodies on the use of electronic communications.
 
  
CONCLUSION 
 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.
