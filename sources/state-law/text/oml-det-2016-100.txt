RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Carley: 
This office received a complaint from Charles Hillman on May 16, 2016, alleging that 
the Building Official Certification Committee (the "Committee") violated the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on 
March 13, 2016, and you responded, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated April 28, 
2016.' The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to specify on the notice for its March 
2, 2016 meeting that it planned to hold a public hearing on a particular matter. 
Following our review, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law. In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint filed with the 
Committee, the Committee's response, and the complainant's request for further review by 
our office. Additionally, we reviewed the notice, minutes, and audio-recording of the 
Committee meeting held on March 2, 2016. 
FACTS 
We find the facts as follows. On December 8, 2015, the Committee issued a written 
decision concerning a complaint against George Peterson, the Building Commissioner for the 
Town of Huntington, following a hearing held on November 4, 2015. In its decision, the 
Committee suspended Mr. Peterson's building inspector certification effective until the 
Committee's March 2, 2016 meeting, at which time the Committee would "hold a hearing to 
determine if Mr. Peterson is competent to act as a building inspector."

The Committee had planned to hold a hearing regarding Mr. Peterson's certification 
on March 2, 2016, as indicated in the decision. However, due to an administrative error, the 
Committee listed the topic on its notice as, "Follow-up BOCC 18-2015 George Peterson" 
under the heading, "New Business/Additional Business/Other matters not reasonably 
anticipated 48 hours in advance of meeting." Because the notice did not identify that the 
Committee would be holding a hearing regarding Mr. Peterson, the Committee decided not to 
hold a hearing that day.2 Instead, the Committee allowed two public officials who were 
present for the anticipated hearing to provide the Committee with an update on Mr. Peterson's 
progress during the suspension period. Following this update, the Committee voted to 
reinstate Mr. Peterson's certification for a three-month probationary period.3 
DISCUSSION 
A public body must post notice 48 hours in advance of a meeting that includes a 
"listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting." G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(b). We generally consider a list of topics sufficiently specific when a reasonable 
member of the public could read the notice and understand the anticipated nature of the public 
body's discussion. See OML 2011-44.4 
The complainant alleges that the March 2, 2016 notice failed to specify that the topic 
regarding Mr. Peterson would be a hearing. Before the Committee may suspend or revoke a 
building inspector's certificate, it must hold a hearing pursuant to G.L. c. 30A, 780 CMR 
110.R7, and 801 CMR 1.02, The Committee realized that it had not complied with the notice 
requirements, under both the Open Meeting Law and the laws governing the hearing itself, 
and therefore decided not to proceed with a hearing regarding Mr. Peterson's suspension in 
order to protect Mr. Peterson's rights. Although the Committee allowed two public officials 
to address it regarding Mr. Peterson's case, this did not constitute action that necessitated a 
hearing pursuant to 780 CMR 110.R7.4.2.1 (subject to the requirements of Administrative 
Practices as defined by M.G.L. c. 30A, the Committee may suspend or revoke certification).5 
Rather, following an update, the Committee voted to reinstate Mr. Peterson on a three-month 
probationary period, pending a full hearing that occurred on June 1, 2016.

requirements set forth in any other law that may require specific procedures such as a pre-

revocation hearing. We agree with the complainant that, had the Committee gone forward 
with the hearing, the notice would not have been sufficient under the Open Meeting Law. See 
OML 2016-60 (omitting the fact that a particular topic is a hearing is significant because a 
public hearing offers members of the public certain rights that they may not otherwise be 
afforded during a meeting of a public body). Because it did not hold a hearing, however, the 
Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law by discussing a matter of which sufficient 
notice had not been given. 
Additionally, the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law by limiting public 
participation during the meeting. See OML 2014-23; OML 2013-64; OML 2012-59. The 
Open Meeting Law states that "no person shall address a meeting of a public body without 
permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent." G.L. 
c. 30A, § 20(f). It is within the Chair's discretion to limit public participation during a 
meeting, and thus he did not violate the Open Meeting Law by doing so here. However, we 
encourage public bodies to allow public participation whenever possible. See OML 2015-12; 
OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59. 
CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law. We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be 
resolved. This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with 
our office or the Committee. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you 
have any questions.
