This office received a complaint from Jason Alfred on April 21, 2026, alleging that the 
Attleboro School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.1  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on or about March 25, and you 
responded on behalf of the Committee by letter dated April 14.  The complaint alleges that (1)

Following our review, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law 
by chilling the complainant’s participation in open meetings.  We decline to review the 
remaining allegations of the complaint.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open 
Meeting Law complaint, the Committee’s response, and the complainant’s request for further 
review.  We reviewed documents secured by the complainant in response to a request under the 
Public Records Law, and an email Chair Geddes sent to the complainant on March 6.  We also 
viewed a recording of a meeting that the Committee held on March 2.3

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Committee met on March 2.  In keeping with the 
Committee’s usual practice, the meeting included an Open Forum, during which interested 
members of the public could offer public comment of up to three minutes each.  The complainant 
spoke during Open Forum.  He appeared agitated and upset, and he singled out one educator for 
allegedly mistreating students, calling her “disgusting” and “vile.”  He put members of the 
Committee “on notice” that: 
 
If you continue to allow employees of the school district to engage in these illegal 
activities, then I am going to sue all of you.  Not only in your official capacity but also in 
your individual capacity under 42 USC 1983 for fourth amendment and fourteenth 
amendment violations.   
 
As the complainant concluded his remarks, he was interrupted by Chair Geddes because 
he had exhausted his allotted three minutes.  The Chair and the complainant talked over each 
other briefly.  The complainant then remarked that “Massachusetts does not have a homestead 
exemption.  I will sue all of you individually and go into your homes and force into into [sic] 
bankruptcy.” 
 
That night, after the meeting, Chair Geddes sent an email to the other Committee 
members, apologizing for losing his composure and expressing appreciation for the support he 
received following the meeting.  He stated that he planned to contact legal counsel regarding 
“comments made tonight, specifically about one of our educators.”  He indicated that he spoke 
with the educator and expressed the Committee’s support for their work and apologized for not 
defending them more forcefully during the meeting.  Two members of the Committee responded 
to Chair Geddes individually, without copying other members, stating that an apology was not 
owed. 
 
Principal Frank Rich also sent an email on the night of March 2.  In that email, sent to 
school staff, Principal Rich defended the educator who was the subject of the complainant’s 
public comment.  He described her as “someone who is deeply committed to our students, our 
schools, and each of you.”  Principal Rich’s email was forwarded to Chair Geddes, who then 
forwarded it to the full Committee the next morning, with the following message: 
 
Hi everyone,  
 
Please [sic] the message below that was sent by Principal Rich following last night’s 
meeting. 
 
Rob Geddes 
Chairperson 
Attleboro School Committee – At Large

DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law when the chair sent the 
March 6 Email. 
 
At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement that meetings of public bodies be 
open and accessible to the public, unless convened in executive session for a proper purpose.  
See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a).  Generally, access to a meeting must include the opportunity to be 
physically present as well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the members of the 
public body.  See OML 2020-100; OML 2016-54; OML 2014-19.4

The complainant alleges that the Chair, through the March 6 Email, sought to chill his 
lawful participation in meetings.5  We disagree.  A public body may not take action which 
reasonably could be seen as intimidation aimed at discouraging public participation in open 
meetings.  For example, we found that a public body’s request to review and photograph the 
identification of a person who expressed criticism of the public body presented an unacceptable 
risk of discouraging public attendance at meetings.  See OML 2025-73.  Such an act, directed at 
those who disagree with the public body, has a chilling effect on public attendance.  Id.  By 
contrast, a security policy requiring photo identification for admission to offices may be enforced 
as to persons entering those offices to attend a public meeting, provided that the policy is not 
used as a pretext to discourage public attendance at public meetings.  See OML 2024-135; OML 
2015-92.  See also OML 2018-141 (finding that public body’s voluntary sign-in policy did not 
create a chilling effect in violation of the Open Meeting Law).   
 
Here, the March 6 Email sought confirmation that the complainant threatened legal 
action, not physical harm, when he spoke during Open Forum at the March 2 meeting.  The 
Chair’s request for clarification was reasonable in light of the complainant’s statement that he 
would “go into [members’] homes” in the context of a threat of litigation.  Indeed, the Chair 
explained in his March 6 Email that “[w]hile I heard what you said in the moment, I thought I 
had misheard you and took your statements to mean that you were simply going to take our 
homes through legal action.  Watching the video back, I think you were trying to rush through 
the end of your statement and may have misread or misspoke by saying that you are going to ‘… 
come into [our] homes….’”  The March 6 Email reasonably can be read to seek confirmation 
that the complainant was not contemplating physical harm to the Committee members, and not 
as a tool to chill public participation in open meetings.  Therefore, we find no violation as to this 
allegation. 
 
II. We decline to review the allegation that the Committee deliberated via email.  
 
The complaint appears to allege that the Committee deliberated outside a meeting.6  
Without identifying any such deliberation with particularity, the complaint notes “concern that 
deliberation, coordination, or response alignment might have occurred outside of public view.” 
 
Complainants must allege violations with a degree of specificity, as our office will not 
conduct broad audits of public bodies based on generalized allegations.  See OML Declination 6-
2-26 (Town of Ware); OML Declination 7-19-23 (Fairhaven Historical Commission); OML 
2021-133.  Although the complaint notes “concern that deliberation . . . might have occurred 
outside of public view,” no further detail is provided in support of this allegation.  Moreover, 
although the March 6 Email was appended to the complaint, no other communication was

Because the complaint does not allege this violation with sufficient specificity, we decline to

 Although we decline to review this allegation, we take this opportunity to caution the 
Committee against improperly deliberating via email.  Except when convened in executive 
session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a).  A 
“meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public body with respect to any 
matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The Open Meeting Law defines 
“deliberation” broadly as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including 
electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its 
jurisdiction; provided, however, that “‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of a 
meeting agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other procedural meeting 
[information] or the distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, 
provided that no opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  
 
Of particular concern here is the email that Chair Geddes sent to the Committee on 
March 2, after that evening’s meeting.  An expression of appreciation or an apology, without 
more, may not constitute public business within the Committee’s jurisdiction, and as such, would 
not fall within the broad definition of “deliberation.”  However, Committee members should 
refrain from discussing a plan of action or reporting on a conversation with an educator, outside 
of a meeting.  Moreover, the Committee is reminded that a one-way communication from one 
public body member to a quorum on business within a body’s jurisdiction is deliberation, even if 
no other member responds.  See OML 2025-168; OML 2023-139; OML 2020-136. 
 
III. We decline to review the allegation that the Chair wrote the March 6 Email 
without authorization from the Committee.  
 
Finally, we decline to review the complaint’s allegation that through the March 6 Email, 
Chair Geddes was “speaking on behalf of the public body outside of a properly posted meeting, 
without formal deliberation or authorization.”  But for limited circumstances not material to this 
complaint, the Open Meeting Law does not govern a chair’s authority to act on behalf of a public 
body.  Therefore, we decline to review any allegation within the complaint that Chair Geddes 
should have sought the Committee’s blessing prior to sending the March 6 Email. 
  
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law in the ways alleged.
