 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Castro: 
 
This office received a complaint from Anne Beauchamp on September 17, 2026, alleging 
that the Hopkinton Library Board of Trustees (the “Board”), and specifically, Board Chair 
Warren Carter, violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.1  The complaint was 
originally filed with the Board on or about June 3, and you responded on behalf of the Board by 
letter dated June 24.  The complaint alleges that Chair Carter violated the Open Meeting Law 
when he (1) acted unilaterally on matters within the Board’s jurisdiction; and (2) deliberated 
outside a posted meeting. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when 
members communicated via email on June 1.  We decline to review the other allegation of the 
complaint.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaint, the 
Board’s response and the request for further review of the complaint.  We also spoke with you 
and the complainant by telephone.  We reviewed the Board’s by-laws and the notices of 
meetings the Board held in August and September 2026.  Finally, we reviewed an email chain, 
dated June 1, provided to us by the complainant.  
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Board has five members; thus, three members 
constitute a quorum.  At all times relevant to the complaint, the complainant was a member of 
the Board.  On June 1, Member Jessica McCaffrey emailed the other members of the Board, 
noting that then-Library Director Nanci Hill would be on medical leave starting on June 4.  She

DISCUSSION 
 
I. The complainant has demonstrated good cause for delay in filing the complaint 
with our office. 
 
The email that is the subject of the complaint was sent on June 1.  Therefore, the 
complaint should have been filed with our office on or before August 30.  However, as noted 
above, it was filed with our office on September 17. 
 
The Open Meeting Law provides that “[a]t least 30 days prior to the filing of a complaint 
with the attorney general, the complainant shall file a written complaint with the public body, 
setting forth the circumstances which constitute the alleged violation and giving the body an 
opportunity to remedy the alleged violation.”  Moreover, the Attorney General’s regulations 
provide: 
 
The Attorney General shall decline to investigate complaints filed with the 
Attorney General more than 90 days after the alleged violation of [the Open 
Meeting Law], or if the alleged violation ... could not reasonably have been 
known at the time it occurred, then within 90 days of the date it should reasonably 
have been discovered. However, this time may be extended if the Attorney 
General grants an extension to the public body to respond to a complaint or if the 
complainant demonstrates good cause for the delay in filing with the Attorney 
General. 
 
940 CMR 29.05(7). 
 
In its response to the complaint, the Board does not challenge the content of the Chair’s 
June 1 email, as quoted in the complaint, or deny that it was sent.  Without conceding that the 
Chair’s email constituted deliberation outside a meeting, the Board in its June 24 response 
committed to scheduling an Open Meeting Law training with Town Counsel, including training 
on “what constitutes a deliberation under the Law.”  The Board further promised to advise the 
complainant and our office when the training was scheduled and completed.  By mid-September, 
no training had been scheduled.

When the complainant requested review of her complaint on September 17, she 
acknowledged that the deadline to do so had passed.  However, she noted that the Board had not 
undergone Open Meeting Law training, despite its promise to do so.  When we spoke by 
telephone, she explained that during the years she served as a Board member, the Board 
generally did not meet in July or August.  This summer the Board did meet in August, and once 
in early September, for limited purposes. The Board met on August 1 to discuss closing the 
library on two days for recarpeting and to meet in executive session to discuss a “personnel 
matter” related to Director Hill.  The Board met again on August 21 and August 25 to discuss 
Director Hill’s leave. Finally, the Board met on September 3 to discuss Director Hill’s 
resignation and the process of hiring a new director.  The complainant explained to us that when 
the Board resumed its regular meetings on September 15, and was no longer focused on the 
status of the Director, it became clear to her that the chair did not intend to schedule the 
promised training and that he would not allow deliberation on the matter.  She requested review 
of her complaint two days later, on September 17. 
 
As noted above, the Open Meeting Law provides a public body an opportunity to remedy 
an alleged violation as part of the complaint process.  Here, the Board committed to undergo 
training in its response to the Open Meeting Law complaint and promised to update the 
complainant and our office when it did so.  The complainant accepted this offer to remedy the 
alleged violation.  She relied on the Board’s representation that it would receive training when 
she postponed requesting our review of the complaint.  When she reasonably came to believe, at 
the September 15 meeting, that the chair did not plan to schedule the training, she requested that 
we review the complaint.  We appreciate the complainant’s attempt to work with the Board to 
address her allegations, thereby saving our office’s resources.  The complainant should not be 
punished for her reliance on the Board’s representation that it would attend an Open Meeting 
Law training and her patience in waiting a reasonable amount of time for confirmation of the 
training.  We find that the complainant has demonstrated good cause for the short delay of less 
than three weeks, and we review her complaint.2 
 
II. The Board violated the Open Meeting Law when members deliberated via email 
on June 1. 
 
Except when convened in executive session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open 
to the public.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a).  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation 
by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  
The Open Meeting Law defines “deliberation” broadly as “an oral or written communication 
through any medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on 
any public business within its jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not 
include the distribution of a meeting agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other 
procedural [information] or the distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a 
meeting, provided that no opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.   
 
Inclusion of scheduling information in the emails of June 1 did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law, as such information is explicitly excluded from the definition of deliberation.  
However, when Board members engaged in a discussion via email of who would serve as acting

library director in Director Hill’s absence, they engaged in deliberation outside a meeting.  The 
Board’s bylaws list two objectives, one of which is “[t]o employ the library director and staff to 
operate the Library.”  Indeed, according to the notice of the Board’s September 3 meeting, the 
Board was to consider two topics, the resignation of Director Hill, and the hiring of a new 
director.  Ensuring that an acting library director was in place to operate the library while 
Director Hill was on medical leave was public business within the Board’s jurisdiction and 
should only have been discussed in a properly noticed meeting.  When the Board discussed this 
topic via email, it violated the Open Meeting Law. 
 
III. We decline to review the remaining allegation of the complaint. 
 
To the extent that the complaint alleges that the Chair improperly acted unilaterally on 
matters of Board business, we decline to review that allegation.  The Division of Open 
Government’s review concerns compliance with the Open Meeting Law.  We decline to review 
those allegations that, even if true, would not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  
With few exceptions, the Open Meeting Law does not dictate the division of labor between 
members and the Chair, or the subjects within the Chair’s authority.  Because the selection of an 
acting library director falls outside the scope of the Open Meeting Law, we decline to review an 
allegation that it was done improperly.  See OML Declination 8-10-26 (North Brookfield Board 
of Selectmen); OML Declination 4-3-25 (Middlefield Conservation Commission); OML 
Declination 9-4-12 (Cambridge Historical Commission).3 
  
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it deliberated via email on June 1.  We order immediate and future compliance with the 
Open Meeting Law, and we caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.  We also order the release within the next thirty (30) days of the date 
of this letter of any written communication, including emails and text messages, exchanged by 
members of the Board about who would serve as acting library director while Director Hill was 
on medical leave.4  We further order all members of the Board to attend a training by the 
Division of Open Government on the Open Meeting Law within the next ninety (90) days.5  
Finally, we order that the Board certify to our office that it has complied with the above orders.  
See 940 CMR 29.07(4). 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Board or with 
our office.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
