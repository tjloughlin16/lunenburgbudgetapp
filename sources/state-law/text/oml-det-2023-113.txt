 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Hennigan: 
 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on June 5, 2023, alleging that the 
Auburn Conservation Commission (the “Commission”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Commission on May 25, 2023, and 
you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Commission, by letter dated May 31, 2023. The 
complaint alleges that the Commission failed to accept minutes for meetings held on July 13, 
2022, July 27, 2022, August 10, 2022, August 24, 2022, September 14, 2022, October 12, 2022, 
October 26, 2022, and November 16, 2022, in a timely manner.  
 
We resolve this matter by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Commission’s response to the complaint, the 
complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the notice and minutes of the 
Commission meeting held on May 24, 2023. We find that the Commission violated the Open 
Meeting Law in the way alleged. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made, and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting minutes must be reviewed 
and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). “Timely manner” means “within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can

show good cause for further delay.” See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.1 Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting. See OML 2018-67; 
OML 2017-133. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Commission did not approve the minutes of meetings held 
on July 13, 2022, July 27, 2022, August 10, 2022, August 24, 2022, September 14, 2022, 
October 12, 2022, October 26, 2022, and November 16, 2022, within the later of 30 days or three 
meetings as required by the Open Meeting Law. During a meeting held on May 24, 2023, the 
Commission approved these eight sets of minutes and acknowledges that the minutes were not 
approved in a timely manner. However, the Commission explains that it had good cause for 
delay because of staffing shortages. In general, staffing shortages of the type that all 
governmental bodies with limited resources experience, or periodic staff vacancies, do not 
constitute good cause for failing to approve minutes in a timely manner, as it is the responsibility 
of the public body and its members to take steps to ensure that staff have adequate assistance to 
complete the minutes in a timely manner, including having members of the public body assist in 
the creation of minutes if necessary. See OML 2021-177; OML 2018-105; OML 2015-86. 
Ultimately, the responsibility for complying with the Open Meeting Law’s requirement that 
meeting minutes be created and approved in a timely manner rests with public bodies and 
members of such bodies, not with individual public employees, such as administrative staff. See 
OML 2018-105. Therefore, we find the Commission failed to timely approve the minutes of 
meetings held on July 13, 2022, July 27, 2022, August 10, 2022, August 24, 2022, September 14, 
2022, October 12, 2022, October 26, 2022, and November 16, 2022.  
 
We note that the Commission approved all the meeting minutes at issue before the instant 
Open Meeting Law complaint was filed.  Indeed, it appears it was the Commission’s own 
approval of the minutes that called attention to the timeliness of its approval and prompted the 
complainant to file this complaint; the complainant had not requested or otherwise expressed 
concern with the minutes prior to their approval.  In instances where all appropriate corrective 
action has already been taken, we urge complainants not to consume resources by filing 
complainants where the transparency purpose of any such complaint has already been addressed 
by corrective action taken by the public body.  Pursuing such complaints takes up scarce state 
and local resources without advancing the Open Meeting Law’s goal of improved governmental 
transparency.
