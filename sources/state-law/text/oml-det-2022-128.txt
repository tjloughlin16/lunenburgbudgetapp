 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Reich: 
This office received a complaint from Tracy Stewart on March 28, 2022, alleging that the 
Medway Board of Health (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. 
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on February 22, and you responded on behalf 
of the Board by letter dated March 14.1 In her complaint, Ms. Stewart alleges that the Board 
failed to timely approve meeting minutes and amended six meeting notices months after the 
meetings had taken place.2 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to approve its October 4 meeting minutes in a timely manner. We find that the Board did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law in the other way alleged. In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complainant’s 
request for further review. We also reviewed the relevant notices and minutes of several Board 
meetings held between July 19, 2021, and March 21, 2022.

1. The Board failed to timely approve its October 4 meeting minutes.  
 The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting minutes must be reviewed 
and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). “Timely manner” means “within the next 
three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can 
show good cause for further delay.” See 940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48.3 Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting. See OML 2021-91.  
 The complaint alleges that the Board failed to approve minutes for its meetings held on 
October 4, November 1, and December 6 in a timely manner. The Board reports that, although it 
posted notices for meetings to be held on November 1 and December 6, it later cancelled these 
two meetings. When a public body does not actually deliberate on a noticed topic, the public 
body cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with respect to that topic, even if it would have been 
insufficiently detailed had the public body proceeded with its deliberation. See OML 2020-53; 
OML 2015-63. Accordingly, because the Board did not meet on November 1 and December 6, 
we find no violation for failing to approve minutes for the meetings which were cancelled.  
However, the Board concedes, and we find, that the Board failed to approve its October 4 
meeting minutes in a timely manner. Here, the October 4 minutes were not approved until March 
21, 2022, which far exceeded both 30 days and three meetings. In its response, the Board asserts 
that it had good cause to delay the approval of these meeting minutes, because of “the significant 
constraints on municipal government caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and resulting staffing 
shortages.” We disagree. The responsibility for complying with the Open Meeting Law rests 
with public bodies and members of such bodies, not with individual public employees, such as 
administrative staff. See OML 2018-105. Therefore, we have generally found that shortages in 
staff do not provide good cause for failing to approve minutes in a timely manner. See 
OML 2018-105; OML 2015-86. If staff resources are not available to create minutes for a public 
body to approve, then public body members must take on this responsibility to ensure 
compliance with the Law. See OML 2019-163; OML 2018-111. We thus find that the Board 
violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely create and approve meeting minutes.  
2. The Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when six of its notices were updated 
months after the meetings were held.  
 A foundational requirement of the Open Meeting Law is that public bodies post notice of 
each meeting “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal 
holidays” and that every notice include, among other things, “a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Public bodies 
must list topics for discussion with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the 
issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).

 Here, Ms. Stewart alleges that the Board should not have amended six notices several 
months after those meetings took place. The Board explains that the software it uses for meeting 
notices has a glitch which causes notices to be marked as “revised” each time a staff member 
opens the document, regardless of whether any changes are made. The Open Meeting Law does 
not regulate what public bodies may do with meeting notices after the meeting has passed. 
Therefore, we do not review whether any changes were made to the content of the notices after 
the meetings took place, and we find no violation of the Open Meeting Law.  
 We take this opportunity to remind the Board that notices for upcoming meeting must 
include the date and time the notice is posted,. The Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law 
regulations require that the date and time that a meeting notice is posted be conspicuously 
recorded on or with the notice. See 940 CMR 29.03(l)(d). In addition, notices that are revised 
within the 48 hours before the meeting must conspicuously record both the date and time that the 
amended notice was posted and the date and time that the notice was originally posted. See 940 
CMR 29.03(l)(d); OML 2017-183; OML 2013-44. 
CONCLUSION 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to approve the minutes of the October 4 meeting in a timely manner. We order the 
Board’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution the Board 
that a determination by our office of a similar violation in the future may be considered evidence 
of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law. As the Board has now approved the late meeting 
minutes, we order no further relief.   
 We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any questions.
