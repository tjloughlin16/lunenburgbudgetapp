 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint

This office received two complaints on November 28, 2023,
1 alleging that the Billerica 
Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The first 
complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about October 27 by Kelley Sardina and the 
second complaint was filed with the Board on or about November 1 by Terri Witts. You 
responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated November 17.
2 The first complaint alleges that 
the notice for the Board’s October 26 meeting included an insufficiently specific topic. Together, 
the complaints also raise several allegations which, for the reasons explained below, we must 
decline to review.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged. In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaints, the 
Board’s response, the requests for further review, and the notice for and video recording of the 
Board’s October 26 meeting.3 
 
The complaints concern the Board’s discussion of a citizen petition regarding a plan for a 
new town center voted on by Town Meeting. The Board posted notice for a meeting to be held

The first complaint alleges that portions of the Board’s discussion related to the petition 
did not fit within the scope of the notice topic “Set Date and Vote For A Special Election -Town 
Center Referendum Petition from the 2023 Annual Fall Town Meeting, Article 14.” The Open 
Meeting Law requires that every meeting notice include, among other things, a list of the topics 
the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). A public 
body is required to list such topics with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of 
the issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(1)(b). We generally consider a topic 
sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand 
the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion. See OML 2015-35.

After review of a video recording of the Board’s October 26 meeting, we find that the 
notice topic was sufficiently specific. We find that it was reasonably foreseeable that the Board 
might discuss whether to schedule a special election and related topics such as the sufficiency of 
the underlying petition requesting the special election. The Open Meeting Law does not require 
public bodies to list all tangential topics related to a discussion nor does the law require a public 
body to anticipate the course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion; the law requires only 
that the public body identify the discussion topic with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise 
the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 
29.03(l)(b); OML 2020-153; OML 2014-127. 
 
Next, we must decline to review the allegations raised in the second complaint and 
several allegations raised in the first complaint. The complaints allege that the Board failed to 
uphold the Town of Billerica’s Charter and violated the Board’s policies and procedures. The 
complaints also raise several allegations regarding the Board’s process in considering the 
petition, the Board’s authority, and various Board members’ reasoning for rejecting the petition. 
The Division of Open Government’s jurisdiction concerns compliance with the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The Open Meeting Law does not, in general, govern a public body’s 
authority to act, nor does it govern the substance of a public body’s discussions. See OML 2023-
5, n.6; OML 2021-131, n.5; OML 2019-85; OML 2020-27. Therefore, the allegations listed 
above, even if true, would not constitute violations of the Open Meeting Law and we decline to 
review them. See OML Decimation 1-25-16 (Nahant Housing Authority Board of 
Commissioners). Likewise, to the extent the complaints allege that the public was not allowed to 
speak at the October 26 meeting, the Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body 
allow public participation, but rather states that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public 
body without permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”
