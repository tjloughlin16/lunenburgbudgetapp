DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when it posted insufficient notice 
of its April 3 meeting. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires a public body to post notice of a 
meeting at least 48 hours in advance, including a “listing of topics that the chair reasonably 
anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  Public bodies are required to 
list topics on a notice with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to 
be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be 
sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand 
the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2024-180; OML 2019-134; 
OML 2015- 35.8  The Open Meeting Law requires that the notice describe topics with sufficient 
specificity so that the public can make an informed decision whether to attend the meeting in 
order to observe the discussion regarding a topic of interest.  See OML 2024-180; OML 2019-
95; OML 2018-22. 
 
All meetings of a public body must be open to the public unless the body has a purpose to 
enter executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a).  Public bodies may enter a closed, executive 
session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law.  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a).  
One permissible purpose for convening in executive session is to “discuss strategy with respect 
to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on the 
bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).  When convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 3 to discuss 
collective bargaining, a public body must identify the collective bargaining unit with whom it 
will be negotiating, if doing so will not compromise the lawful purpose for secrecy.  OML 2024-
127; OML 2023-207; OML 2016-43.   
 
We generally defer to a public body’s assessment of whether identification of the 
bargaining unit would compromise the purpose for the executive session.  However, when 
challenged, a public body must be able to demonstrate a reasonable basis for such a claim. See 
OML 2021-25; OML 2018-139; 2015-14.  Here, the Committee does not suggest that identifying 
the collective bargaining units with whom it was negotiating would have compromised the 
purpose for which the executive session was called.  Therefore, the bargaining units should have 
been identified on the meeting notice and should have been referenced in conjunction with the 
vote to enter executive session.  See OML 2023-207; 2020-30; OML 2013-97.  By failing to 
identify the bargaining units to be discussed during  the April 3 executive session, the Committee 
violated the Open Meeting Law. 
 
We do not find a violation of the Open Meeting Law relative to the notice of the March 
20 meeting.  The notice listed an executive session “(if necessary)” but the Committee did not 
convene an executive session at that meeting.  When a public body does not actually deliberate

II. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when it approved collective 
bargaining agreements without following the Law’s procedure to enter executive 
session under Purpose 3.   
 
The complainant alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law because it 
executed collective bargaining agreements in executive session.  This is not accurate.  Where a 
public body has taken the proper steps to enter executive session under Purpose 3, it may 
approve a collective bargaining agreement in executive session. 9  See OML 2021-145; OML 
2015-52; OML 2013-84.   
 
Because there are circumstances in which the bargaining position of a public body may 
be detrimentally affected by voting on a final collective bargaining agreement in open session, 
final approval may occur in executive session if the chair makes the appropriate declaration that 
such circumstances are present.  See OML 2015-52.  See also G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3); OML 
2020-24; OML 2013-84; Open Meeting Law Guide, available at 
https://www.mass.gov/doc/2023-guide-with-ed-materials/download (“Collective Bargaining 
Strategy: . . . At the time the executive session is proposed and voted on, the chair must state on 
the record that having the discussion in an open session may be detrimental to the public body’s 
bargaining or litigating position.”)  The failure to make the required declaration prior to entering 
executive session “is not a mere technical violation.”  See OML 2011-30.  The declaration is 
“meant to engender confidence that the secrecy inherent in executive session is truly necessary.”  
Id.   
 
Here, the Committee did not declare that an open meeting might have a detrimental effect 
on the body’s bargaining position.  It was therefore improper for the Committee to approve the

III. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when it failed to review 
executive session minutes at reasonable intervals to determine whether continued 
nondisclosure was warranted. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body create and maintain accurate minutes 
of all meetings, including executive sessions.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Executive session minutes 
may be withheld for as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive 
session, but no longer.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).  The Law requires that executive session minutes be 
“review[ed] ... at reasonable intervals to determine if continued non-disclosure of minutes is 
warranted.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1).  See al so  OML 2024-158; OML 2024-29; Swansea Board 
of Selectmen v. Attorney General, Civil Action No. 2173CV00906 (Bristol Sup. Ct. December 
27, 2023).  Although “reasonable interval” is not defined by the Law, our office has found a 
quarterly review or a review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2024-181; OML 
2019-45; OML 2017-104.  A public body must announce the result of its review at the next 
meeting following its review and record the announcement in the minutes.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(g)(1).  A public body’s obligation to review executive session minutes for possible release is 
ongoing.  See OML 2024-181.  If a public body reviews executive session minutes and 
determines that the purpose for the executive session remains such that the minutes should 
continue to be withheld from the public, it must continue to review those same executive session 
minutes at reasonable intervals until it determines that the reason for secrecy has expired.  See 
OML 2023-242; OML 2022- 155; OML 2021-129.  The procedural requirements surrounding 
executive session, including periodic review of executive session minutes, are critical to 
advancing the transparency aims of the Open Meeting Law, because the public has no other way 
to know what happened at these closed-door sessions.  See OML 2020-109; OML 2020-15; 
OML 2019-133.  Similarly, announcing the outcome of the review of executive session minutes 
advances the goal of transparency by informing the public that such records are available.  See 
OML 2024-67; OML 2012-2.   
 
The Committee has acknowledged that timely review and release of executive session 
minutes is an important part of its duties and that it advances transparency.  However, over the 
past few years, the Committee has on several occasions reviewed executive session minutes at 
intervals greater than six months.  The information before us suggests that on at least one 
occasion, the Committee went over a year without reviewing executive session minutes to 
determine whether continued nondisclosure was warranted.  In failing to perform a review at 
reasonable intervals, the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law. 
 
IV. We decline to review the allegation that the Committee failed to release executive 
session minutes in response to public records requests. 
 
By emails dated February 4 and May 2, the complainant directed requests to the 
Com
mittee for over a dozen sets of executive session minutes.  Both emails were directed to the

Committee, with a copy to the Superintendent of Schools, Nan Murphy, and her Executive 
Assistant, Amy Hicks.  The emails explicitly stated that they were made “under the Public 
Records Law.”   
 
A request for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law, G.L. c. 66, § 10,  
or the Open Meeting Law, depending on how the request is made.  Where a request does not 
identify the Public Records Law or the Open Meeting Law, we have looked to other aspects of 
the request for indications of the requester’s intent.  We have said that a request for meeting 
minutes that is directed to a clerk or other employee as Records Access Officer will likely fall 
under the Public Records Law.  See OML 2019-126; OML 2020-20 (declining to review public 
records request for minutes that was directed to clerk); OML 2022-55 (declining to review 
allegation by Brian Keaney that Dedham Parks and Recreation Commission failed to respond to 
public records request).  We also have deemed a request for documents other than the minutes 
themselves a public records request.  See OML 2019-68; OML 2014-45; OML Declination 7-16-
20 (Greater Lowell Technical School Committee).   
 
Here, as 
 the complainant’s requests explicitly referenced the Public Records Law, we 
find that the requests are governed by the Public Records Law.  The Division of Open 
Government’s review concerns compliance with the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
As this allegation concerns the Committee’s compliance with the Public Records Law, not the 
Open Meeting Law, we decline to review it.  See OML 2015-17; OML Declination 3-11-15 
(Wayland School Committee).10   
 
Although we decline to review this allegation, we take this opportunity to remind the 
Committee that upon receipt of a request for executive session minutes made under the Open 
Meeting Law, a public body must respond to the requestor within ten days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(g)(2).  If the public body has recently performed a review of the requested executive session 
minutes, then it should provide any minutes it determined should be released and explain that the 
remaining minutes continue to be withheld.  See OML 2024-168; OML 2013-105; OML 2013-
99.  If, however, at the time of a request the public body has not recently conducted the periodic 
review of its executive session minutes, then the body must review and release the non-exempt 
minutes, or any portion thereof, not later than the body’s next meeting or 30 days, whichever 
occurs first.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).     
 
V. The Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it met in executive 
session under Purpose 1 to discuss an Open Meeting Law complaint. 
 
Finally, in the May 17 Complaint, the complainant alleges that it was improper for the 
Committee to review his Open Meeting Law complaint in executive session pursuant to Purpose 
1.  A public body may enter executive session under Purpose 1, “[t]o discuss the reputation, 
character, physical condition or mental health, rather than professional competence, of an 
individual, or to discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or charges brought against, 
a public officer, employee, staff member or individual.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1).  It is well

CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee viol ated the Open Meeting Law 
when it posted insufficient notice of its April 3 meeting; when it approved collective bargaining 
agreements at its April 3 meeting without following the Law’s procedure to enter executive 
session; and when it failed to review minutes of executive sessions at regular intervals.  We order 
immediate and future compliance with the Law’s requirements and we caution that similar future 
violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  We order the Committee to 
review minutes of all executive sessions held from 2019 to present within 90 days of issuance 
of this letter to determine if continued non-disclosure is warranted, to the extent that it has not 
done so within the last three months, consistent with the guidance provided above. We also order 
the Committee to recreate the missing meeting minutes within 90 days of issuance of this 
letter to the best of its ability, through whatever means are available, including the memories 
and individual notations of the attendees.  Because the Committee has released minutes of its 
April 3 executive session, we order no further relief in response to the violations stemming from 
the April 3 meeting. 
 
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee.  Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any 
questions.
