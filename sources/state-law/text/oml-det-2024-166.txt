that the Ashby Selectboard (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-

We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed these matters.  Following 
our review, find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by not timely responding to a 
request for meeting minutes.  We find that the allegation regarding deliberation was untimely 
filed and we decline to review it.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original 
complaints, the Board’s response to the complaints, and the complaint filed with our office 
requesting further review.  We also reviewed the notice and open session minutes of the Board 
meeting held on March 15, 2023, as well as a video recording of that meeting.

DISCUSSION 
 
With respect to the allegation raised in the complaints that a quor um of the Board 
deliberated after the adjournment of the March 15, 2023, meeting, we decline to review this 
allegation as it was not timely filed.  Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law 
must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b).  
Here, the meeting during which the alleged violation occurred was held on March 15, 2023.  The 
complainant was present at this meeting as well as during the discussions following the meeting, 
but the complaint was not filed with the Board until August 28, 2023.  Because the complaint 
was filed with the Board five months later, we find that the complaint is untimely and we decline 
to review it.  See OML Declination 2-1-17 (West Bridgewater Board of Water Commissioners); 
OML Declination 11-21-16 (Middleton Planning Board); OML Declination 12-15-14 (Ware 
Conservation Board).2   
 
However, we review the allegations raised in the complaints that the Board did not timely 
res
pond to a request for open and executive session meeting minutes.  The Open Meeting Law 
requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings, including 
executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The minutes of an open session, if they exist and 
whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 
days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  If minutes do not yet exist at the time of a request, the public body is 
still required to respond to the request within 10 days with an explanation of whether the minutes 
do or do not exist in either approved or draft form.  See OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; OML 
2015-173. 
 
Executive session minutes may be withheld from disclosure to the public “as long as 
public
ation may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 22(f).  When the purpose for a valid executive session has been served, the minutes and 
any documents or exhibits used at the session must be disclosed unless the attorney-client 
privilege or an exemption to the public records law applies to withhold them, in whole or in part, 
from disclosure.  See id.  Public bodies have an obligation to review the minutes of executive 
sessions at reasonable intervals to determine if continued non-disclosure of minutes is warranted, 
and to announce that determination at the next meeting following its review.  G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(g)(1); see OML 2019- 35; OML 2015-94; OML 2013-56.  Upon request by any person to 
inspect or copy the minutes of an executive session or any portion thereof, the body must 
respond to the request within 10 days following receipt and release any such minutes where 
publication would not defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session; provided, however, 
that if the body has not performed a review, the public body shall perform the review and release 
the non-exempt minutes, or any portion thereof, not later than the body's next meeting or 30 
days, whichever first occurs.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2); OML 2012-106; OML 2012-91; OML 
2012-86.

responded later that evening stating that he would forward the “request to the Records Officer.” 
Reque
sts for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law or the Open Meeting Law, 
depending on how the request is made, and the requirements for responding differ.  A request for 
minutes that is directed to a clerk or other municipal employees as Records Access Officer will 
likely fall under the Public Records Law and requires a response in 10 business days.  See G.L. 
c. 66, § 10; OML 2019-126; OML 2019-68.  A request for meeting minutes made directly to the 
public body falls under the Open Meeting Law and requires a response within 10 calendar days.  
See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2); OML 2019-125. 
 
Because the request for meeting minutes was directed to the Board’s Chair, the request 
fel
l under the Open Meeting Law and required a response within 10 calendar days.  See G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 22(c), (g); OML 2019-163.  Although the Board responded to the August 16, 2023, 
request for minutes within 10 days, Chair Nadeau’s response was ambiguous, simply stating that 
he would forward the request to “the Records Officer.”  The complainant received no further 
response from the Board until he filed the complaints at issue here.  An unequivocal explanation 
of whether the minutes did or did not exist in either approved or draft form should have been 
provided to the complainant within 10 days of his request.  See OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; 
OML 2015-173.  Because the Board failed to provide such a response, we find that it violated the 
Open Meeting Law by not properly responding to a request for minutes.   
 
We understand that the Board has since provided copies of the open session portion of 
the M
arch 22, 2023, meeting.  With respect to the executive session minutes of the March 22, 
2023, meeting, during which the Board discussed the discipline or dismissal of a public 
employee, when a public body determines that the purpose for an executive session meeting is 
still ongoing, it may continue to withhold the minutes from publication under the Open Meeting 
Law.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).  However, when the purpose for the executive session has 
concluded, the Open Meeting Law no longer authorizes the continued nondisclosure of the 
minutes, although the public body may assert an exemption under the Public Records Law to 
withhold the minutes, in whole or in part, from disclosure.  Here, the Board met in executive 
session under Purpose 1 to discuss “the discipline or dismissal of a public employee.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 21(a)(1).  We remind the Board that if the purpose for the executive session has 
concluded, i.e. if the individual is no longer an employee of the Town and the Board has taken 
all the action it was authorized to take with respect to the discipline or dismissal of that 
employee, then the Open Meeting Law would no longer provide a basis for withholding the 
executive session minutes.  However, even if the Open Meeting Law no longer authorizes the 
withholding of the minutes, it may be the case that the minutes are exempt from production, in 
whole or in part, under the Public Records Law.  See OML 2019-143OML 2015-170. See OML 
2020-142.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to respond to a request for meeting minutes within 10 days.  We order the Board’s 
immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
