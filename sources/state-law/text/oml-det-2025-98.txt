DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when it failed to timely respond 
to a request for executive session minutes. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body create and maintain accurate minutes 
of all meetings, including executive sessions.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Executive session minutes

Upon receipt of a request for executive session minutes made under the Open Meeting 
Law, a public body must respond to the requestor within ten calendar days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(g)(2).  See OML 2024-46; OML 2023-23; OML 2019-163.  See also Frequently Asked 
Questions about the Open Meeting Law (“A public body has 10 calendar days from the date a 
request is received to provide a response.”).6  A request for meeting minutes made under the 
Public Records Law requires a response within 10 business days.  G.L. c. 66, § 10(a). 
 
The complainant’s January 13 request required a response under the Open Meeting Law, 
even t
hough it also referenced the Public Records Law “and every other applicable law.”  A 
request for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law, G.L. c. 66, § 10, or the 
Open Meeting Law, depending on how the request is made.  When a request does not identify 
the Public Records Law or the Open Meeting Law, or cites to both the Public Records Law and 
the Open Meeting Law, we have looked to other aspects of the request for indications of the 
requester’s intent.  A request that is sent to a public body, as opposed to, e.g., a town clerk or 
other records access officer, requires a response under the Open Meeting Law, even if both the 
Open Meeting Law and the Public Records Law are referenced in the request.  See OML 2024-
168; OML 2018-34; OML 2019-68 (public body was not required to respond to request sent to 
city clerk, even though request referenced both Public Records Law and Open Meeting Law).  
The Committee responded to the complainant’s request for executive session minutes on January 
24, eleven calendar days after receiving the request.
7  As the Committee failed to respond to a 
request for executive session minutes within ten calendar days, it violated the Open Meeting 
Law.

The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body subject to an order set out in an Open 
Meeting Law determination notify the Attorney General in writing of its compliance with the 
order within 30 days of receipt of the order, unless otherwise indicated by the order itself.  940 
CMR 29.07(4).  The Committee did not notify our office of compliance with the order set out in 
OML 2024-191.  More importantly, the Committee was not in compliance with the order within 
90 days of the determination.  Therefore, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting 
Law.  
 
Although we express our deep concern with the Committee’s disregard of our order and 
find another violation of the Open Meeting Law as explained above, we note that immediately 
filing a new Open Meeting Law complaint rather than notifying our office of the non-compliance 
so that we could pursue compliance through informal outreach ultimately consumed additional 
resources of both the Committee and this office that may have been avoidable. 
 
 
III. The Committee’s violations were not intentional. 
 
Finally, we must determine whether the Committee’s violations were, as the complainant 
urges, intentional.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  The Open Meeting Law defines an intentional 
violation as “[a]n act or omission by a public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of 
M.G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18 through 25.”  Evidence of an intentional violation includes a public body 
acting with “deliberate ignorance of the law’s requirements” or being previously advised by the 
Attorney General that the conduct violates the Open Meeting Law.  940 CMR 29.02.   
 
In OML
 2024-191, issued to the Committee on October 17, 2024, we explained the 
process that a public body must follow to respond to a request for executive session minutes.  
Nonetheless, that determination did not address the distinction that requests for minutes under 
the Open Meeting Law require a response within 10 calendar days, whereas such requests under 
the Public Records Law require a response in 10 business days.  Moreover, although we find that 
the request was made under the Open Meeting Law, the Committee’s calculation by business 
days instead of calendar days was by all indications a mistaken interpretation of the law, not an 
intentional flouting of it.  Especially where the request stated that it was made under both the 
Open Meeting Law and the Public Records Law, we do not find the Committee’s violations 
intentional.   
 
CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee viola ted the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to respond to a request for executive session minutes and failed to comply with a 
prior order of this office.  We order immediate and future compliance with the Law’s 
requirements.
