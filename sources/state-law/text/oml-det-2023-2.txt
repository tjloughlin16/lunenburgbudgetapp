FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On January 25, 2022, Commission Chair Daniel Bartkus 
signed the Town Clerk’s own acknowledgment form confirming that he had “been informed 
about the Open Meeting Law.” 
 
On Monday, January 24, 2022, the Commission posted notice for a meeting to be held on 
Thursday, January 27, 2022.  The notice listed the location of the meeting as Room 205 in the 
Westminster Town Hall.  In addition, the notice also stated that the meeting would be accessible 
via Zoom and provided information necessary to access the meeting, including a link to the 
Zoom meeting, several telephone numbers the public could use to dial into the meeting, and the 
Webinar ID number required to log into the meeting.  The notice listed 16 topics, including an 
Open Meeting Law complaint filed with the Commission by Ms. Normandin.   
 
The January 27, 2022, meeting was held as planned.  Four Commission members were 
present in the physical meeting location at Town Hall, while the remaining two members 
participated remotely.  During the meeting, the Commission reviewed but did not approve 
minutes of its previous meeting held on December 9, 2021; continued three public hearings; 
approved three certificates of compliance; held a public hearing regarding the installation of a 
driveway on Ellis Road and a public hearing requested by Mass Department of Transportation 
for resurfacing of Route 2; and approved two payment vouchers and the relocation of a utility 
pole on Mountain Road.  Finally, the Commission reviewed a prior Open Meeting Law 
complaint filed by Ms. Normandin on January 10, 2022.  Although Vice Chair Gary Smith read 
Town Counsel’s proposed response to the complaint, the Commission did not approve such 
response or authorize Town Counsel to send the response.  Rather, the Commission simply voted 
to adjourn the meeting.

DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Chair Failed to Sign the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law Certification 
Form, Although He Acknowledged Awareness of the Open Meeting Law. 
  
The Open Meeting Law requires that “within 2 weeks of qualification for office, all 
persons serving on a public body shall certify, on a form prescribed by the attorney general, the 
receipt of a copy of the open meeting law, regulations promulgated pursuant to section 25 and a 
copy of the educational materials prepared by the attorney general explaining the open meeting 
law and its application pursuant to section 19. ... The certification shall be evidence that the 
member of a public body has read and understands the requirements of the open meeting law and 
the consequences of violating it.”  G.L. c. 30A § 20(h) (emphasis added).   
 
The complaint alleges that Committee Chair Daniel Batkus did not complete the required 
Open Meeting Law certification form.  Here, Chair Batkus signed the Town Clerk’s generic form 
acknowledging that he had “been informed about the Open Meeting Law” rather than the proper 
Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law certification form which requires that public body 
members have “read and understand the requirements of the Open Meeting Law and the 
consequences for violating it,” and that members have received copies of certain educational 
materials.  Id.  We understand that Town Counsel has advised the Town Clerk to amend this 
practice and instead use the Attorney General’s Certification form to ensure full compliance with 
the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2020-30. 
 
II. We Find that the Minutes of the Meeting Held on January 27, 2022, are Accurate. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.  By substantial compliance, we mean that the 
minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not 
attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See 
OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a 
transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion 
presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55.   
 
The complaint does not allege in what way the minutes of the January 27, 2022, meeting 
are inaccurate.  Complainants must allege violations with a degree of specificity, as our office 
will not conduct broad audits of public bodies based on generalized allegations.  See OML 
Declination 3-20-12 (Wilmington Board of Assessors).  Following our review, we find

insufficient evidence to conclude that the meeting minutes are inaccurate.  The minutes include 
the date, time and place of the meeting, a list of the members present and absent, a summary of 
the discussions that occurred that on its face appears sufficiently detailed to comply with the 
Open Meeting Law, and a record of actions taken.  Although the minutes do not contain a list of 
documents used, the complaint does not specifically allege that the minutes are insufficient in 
this regard, nor is it evident from the minutes that any particular documents were used during the 
meeting.  Nevertheless, we remind the Commission that the Open Meeting Law requires that a 
public body include in its minutes a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The Open Meeting Law does not define what it means for a document to 
be used at a meeting but, at a minimum, it is clear that where a document is physically present, 
verbally identified, and the contents are discussed by the members of a public body during an 
open meeting, it has been “used” for purposes of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2014-12; 
OML 2012-42.  We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-
50.   
 
III. We Find Insufficient Evidence to Conclude that the January 27, 2022, Meeting 
was Inaccessible. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the  
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of  
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement  
that meetings of public bodies be open and accessible to the public.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except  
when meeting in executive session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”).  
Generally, access to a meeting must include the opportunity to be physically present as well as to 
see and hear what is being discussed by the members of the public body.  See OML 2020-100; 
OML 2018-75; OML 2016-54. 
 
The complaint alleges that the January 27, 2022, meeting was inaccessible because she 
and other members of the public were unable to hear portions of the discussions that occurred.  
Because this meeting was not recorded, there is no definitive evidence establishing whether the 
discussions held by the Commission were difficult to understand.  As such, we are constrained to 
find that the Commission did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  Nevertheless, we take 
seriously the allegation raised in the complaint and remind the Commission to ensure that all 
members speak in an audible tone throughout meetings and use microphones when necessary.  
We note that the Town has informed Ms. Normandin of the availability of a portable microphone 
amplification system that is available at Town Hall for any individual to use should they need 
hearing assistance during a meeting. 
 
IV. We Find that the Commission Failed to Conduct All Votes by Roll Call and 
Failed to Properly Review a Prior Open Meeting Law Complaint during the 
January 27, 2022, Meeting. 
 
On February 15, 2022, Governor Charles Baker signed into law An Act Relative to 
Extending Certain COVID-19 Measures Adopted During the State of Emergency, Chapter 22 of 
the Acts of 2022 (“Chapter 22”).  Section 20 of Chapter 22 extended the remote meeting 
provisions established in Governor Baker's March 12, 2020, Order Suspending Certain 
===PAGE 5===
5 
 
Provisions of the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, § 20 (“Executive Order”).3  Relevant here, 
the Executive Order temporarily suspended the requirements under the Open Meeting Law and 
the Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations that a quorum of a public body, including 
the chair, be physically present at the meeting location and allows all members of a public body 
to participate in a meeting remotely, provided that the public body provide “adequate, alternative 
means” of public access, such as through telephone, internet or satellite enabled audio or video 
conferencing or any other technology that enables the public to clearly follow the proceedings of 
the public body while those activities are occurring.  See Executive Order at § 1; Chapter 20, § 
20(b); OML 2021-135.  The Executive Order allows public bodies to provide the public with 
“adequate, alternative” access to meetings instead of providing in-person access but does not 
require public bodies to provide “adequate, alternative” access if in-person access is provided.  
All other provisions of the law and regulations regarding remote participation remain in effect, 
including the requirement that “[a]ll votes taken during any meeting in which a member 
participates remotely must be by roll call vote.”  940 CMR 29.10(7)(c).   
 
The complaint alleges that the Commission failed to conduct all votes by roll call and 
failed to review a prior Open Meeting Law complaint during the January 27, 2022, meeting.  
Here, two of the five Commission members participated remotely during the January 27, 2022, 
meeting, while the remaining three members were present in the physical meeting location at 
Town Hall.  The Attorney General’s Regulations require that, “all votes taken during any 
meeting in which a member participates remotely must be by roll call vote,” even unanimous 
votes.  940 CMR 29.10(7)(c); see OML 2016-132; OML 2015-145.  The Commission admits 
that votes taken during this meeting were not conducted by roll call vote.  As such, we find that 
the Commission failed to conduct roll call votes during the January 27, 2022, meeting in 
violation of the Open Meeting Law.   
 
The Commission further admits that it did not “acknowledge receipt” of the Open 
Meeting Law complaint or “determine what its response [would] be at a posted meeting.”  Upon 
the filing of an Open Meeting Law complaint with a public body, the chair “shall disseminate 
copies of the complaint to the members of the public body,” and “the public body shall review 
the complaint’s allegations.”  940 CMR 29.05(3), (5).  Thus, a public body must meet to review 
the complaint and formulate a response, or meet to delegate that authority, and respond to the 
complaint within 14 business days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); OML 2017-69; OML 2012-
90.  Proper procedure requires that a public body review a complaint before authorizing legal 
counsel to respond on its behalf.  See OML 2017-132; OML 2012- 95; OML 2011-6.  Once a 
public body has reviewed a complaint during a meeting, its decision to simply refer the 
complaint, rather than discuss its substance, is the public body’s prerogative.  See OML 2019-40; 
OML 2017-96.  The Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to engage in a 
substantive discussion of the merits of each complaint, nor to read a complaint in full during a 
meeting.  See OML 2022-59; OML 2021-128; OML 2020-38; OML 2018-134.  Forwarding a 
complaint to legal counsel prior to a meeting does not violate the law as long as the public body 
reviews the complaint and any draft response during a meeting, approves the draft response 
during that meeting, and then either decides to send the response itself or authorizes legal 
counsel to send the response.  See OML 2018-134.  During the January 27, 2022, meeting, the 
Commission simply read Town Counsel’s proposed response to the complaint and then

We find that the Commission failed to properly review the prior Open Meeting Law complaint

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Commission violated the Open Meeting 
Law by failing to conduct roll call votes and failing to properly review a prior Open Meeting 
Law complaint during the January 27, 2022, meeting.  We also find that the Chair failed to 
complete the required Open Meeting Law Certification form.  We order the Commission’s 
immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
