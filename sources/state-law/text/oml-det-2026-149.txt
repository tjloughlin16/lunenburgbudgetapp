This office received a complaint from Robert Belbin on January 1, 2026, alleging that the 
Carver Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The 
complaint originally was filed with the Board on December 16, 2025, and you responded on 
behalf of the Board on January 1, 2026.  The complaint alleges that the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law when it met in executive session to discuss litigation but did not identify in the 
notice of the meeting the litigation to be discussed. 
 
We appreciate the parties’ patience as we reviewed this matter.  Following our review, 
we fin
d that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.  In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and 
the complainant’s request for further review.   
 
FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  On November 26, 2025, the Board posted notice of a 
meeting to be held on December 2, 2025.  The notice included the following topic: 
 
Executive Session pursuant to M.G.L. chapter 30A, Sec. 21(a)(3), to discuss strategy with 
respect to litigation and to [sic] M.G.L. Ch. 30A, §21 (a)(2), to discuss contract 
negotiations with nonunion personnel (Chief of Police) and to return to open session.   
 
 The meeting was held as scheduled.  When the Board reached the executive session topic, 
Vice Chair Dan Ryan moved to enter executive session, stating the purposes as set forth in the 
notice.  The Board voted to enter executive session.  During the discussion of litigation strategy,

DISCUSSION 
   
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an 
open session, with some exceptions.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  A public body may enter into 
executive session for any of ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law.  G.L. c. 30A, § 
21(a).  Executive session topics must be described, both in the meeting notice and in an 
announcement during open session, in as much detail as possible without compromising the 
purpose for which the executive session was called.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3).  See also 
District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“A 
precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is 
the only notification given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and 
the only way the public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper.”). 
 
One permissible reason to convene in executive session is “to discuss strategy with 
respect to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on 
the bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
21 (a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).  When convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 3, a public 
body should identify the litigation matter to be discussed.  See OML 2016-12; OML 2013-97.
1  
However, a public body need not identify the litigation matter if publicly disclosing it would 
compromise the purpose of the executive session, such as when the body is considering initiating 
litigation against another party.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a)(3), 21(b)(3); OML 2025-185 (finding 
no violation where public body demonstrated that identification of opposing litigant or other 
details about threatened litigation would have compromised public body’s litigating position); 
OML 2017-178 (finding no violation where identification of requestor of public records would 
have alerted them to public body’s consideration of initiating litigation, thereby compromising 
the public body’s litigating position); OML 2016-12 (“The discussion concerned a matter that 
could reasonably be expected to lead to litigation, and, on the unique facts present here, we 
conclude that the Board was justified in believing that the disclosure of identifying information 
about the matter at issue would compromise its litigation position.”).  When convening in 
executive session to discuss strategy with respect to litigation, the purpose of the executive 
session is to protect the public body’s litigating position vis-à-vis opposing parties to the 
litigation, not to keep confidential from the public the existence of the litigation or to avoid the 
inconvenience of public scrutiny.  See OML 2025-137; 2024-168; OML 2023-189. 
 
Although we generally defer to a public body’s assessment of whether the identification 
of the litigation would compromise the purpose of the executive session, a public body must be 
able to demonstrate a reasonable basis for such a claim if challenged.  See OML 2026-28; OML 
2015-14; OML 2012-76.  In considering whether the public body has demonstrated a reasonable 
basis to refrain from identifying the litigation in the notice, we may look at whether the adverse 
party is aware of the potential litigation.  See OML 2020-53 (“The Board has not asserted that its 
litigating position would have been harmed if Mr. Coulombe or another member of the public

where notice “failed to identify the specific litigation matter to be discussed, despite it being a 
pending matter publicly filed in federal court.”). 
 
Here, the notice of the December 2 meeting listed a Purpose 3 topic without any 
information about the litigation to be discussed.  The Board has argued that notice of that topic 
could not have included any further information without compromising the Board’s litigation 
position or the best interest of the Town.  However, the Board has provided no information in 
support of that position.  When challenged, a public body must be able to demonstrate a 
reasonable basis for its position that identification of the litigation would compromise the 
purpose of the executive session.  The Board has not done so.  Planning Director Thomas Bott 
was aware of the threatened litigation; he was the person who threatened the litigation and 
proposed the settlement agreement to be discussed.  Thus, this is not a situation where the 
identity of the potential litigation was withheld so that the adverse party would not learn of it.  
We are not persuaded that identification of the threatened litigation would have compromised the 
Board’s litigating position.  We therefore find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to identify the litigation that was the subject of the December 2 executive session. 
 
The complainant seeks imposition of a financial penalty as 
 a remedy for the violation.  
The Open Meeting Law provides that the Attorney General may impose a civil penalty upon a 
public body of not more than $1,000 for each intentional violation.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c)(4).  An 
intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body or a member thereof, in knowing 
violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An intentional violation may be found 
where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the law’s requirements or has 
previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  There is no 
evidence that the Board acted in deliberate ignorance of the Law.  Moreover, we have not within 
the past five years warned the Board about this specific requirement.  Therefore, we find that the 
violation was not intentional and impose no fine on the Board.2 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board vio lated the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to identify the litigation to be discussed in executive session during its December 2 
meeting.  We order immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and we 
caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
