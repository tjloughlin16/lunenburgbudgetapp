This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on February 23, 2022, alleging that 
the Fall River School Committee (the “Committee”), violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on January 10, and you 
responded, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated February 22, after receiving an extension 
of time to respond.  The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to timely approve minutes 
of a November 3, 2021, meeting, and that the notice for the Committee’s January 10, 2022, 
meeting was insufficiently specific in one respect.       
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  
In reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaint, the Committee’s 
response, and the request for further review.  We also corresponded by email with Mr. Higgins 
and with counsel for the Committee, and reviewed the notice for and minutes of the Committee’s 
January 10, 2022, meeting. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  All open and executive session meeting 
minutes must be reviewed and approved in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely

The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to timely approve minutes of a 
Novem
ber 3, 2021, meeting because the minutes were approved at a meeting of the Committee 
on January 10, 2022, which the complaint alleges was more than the third meeting and 30 days 
thereafter.  We disagree.  The November 3, 2021, meeting in question was not a meeting of the 
Committee; it was a meeting of the Special Education/Alternative Education and Early College 
Subcommittee (the “Subcommittee”).  A subcommittee is itself a separate public body for 
purposes of complying with the Open Meeting Law.  Therefore, the minutes of the 
Subcommittee’s November 3, 2021, meeting were required to be approved within the later of the 
next 30 days or three meetings of the Subcommittee.  See OML 2020-19 (minutes of a 
subcommittee meeting were timely approved by the parent public body over seven months later, 
as the subcommittee had not held any meetings in the interim, even though the parent public 
body had held numerous meetings).   
 
We note further that the Open Meeting Law does not govern the method for approving 
mee
ting minutes and public bodies may choose the method for approval.  See OML 2020-81; 
OML 2019-107; OML 2019-9.  Acceptable methods include, but are not limited to, approval by 
the public body chair alone or by another designated individual, approval by majority vote of the 
public body, approval by consent of the body (approved unless there is an objection), or approval 
by a parent public body on behalf of a subcommittee.  See OML 2019-107; OML 2019-25; OML 
2017-34.  The method of approval of minutes that a public body chooses does not impact the 
standard for timely approval of minutes, which remains “within the next three public body 
meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later.”  940 CMR 29.11.  As the minutes of the 
Subcommittee’s November 3, 2021, meeting were approved on January 10, 2022, and the 
Subcommittee did not hold any meetings in the interim, we find the minutes were timely 
approved and neither the Committee nor the Subcommittee violated the Open Meeting Law. 
 
Next, we consider whether the notice for the Committee’s January 10, 2022, meeting was 
suff
iciently specific.  The notice included the topic “Appointment and Vote to Approve: 2022 
Subcommittee Appointments, as presented by Mayor Paul Coogan.”  The complaint alleges that 
the notice failed to include the names of the individuals being considered for appointment to the 
various subcommittees of the Committee.  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, we 
do not review a meeting notice standing alone, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face.  See 
OML 2021-180; OML 2015-109.  Rather, we review what the public body actually discussed at 
the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently specific to make the public aware of the 
discussion topics.  See id.  In general, a public body must include on its meeting notices the 
names of the specific individuals who the public body will be considered for employment or 
appointment.  See OML 2020-80; OML 2016-167; OML 2011-23.  Here, however, the 
Committee asserts—and the minutes of the January 10, 2022, meeting confirm—that the 
Committee did not actually discuss the subcommittee appointments.  Rather, the Mayor, who has
