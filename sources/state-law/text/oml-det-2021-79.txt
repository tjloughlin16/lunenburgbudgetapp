Dear Attorney Randazzo:  
 
This office received a complaint from David Durgin on April 28, 2021, alleging that the 
Dudley Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about April 7, 2021, and you 
responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated April 27, 2021.  The complaint alleges that the 
Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it met in person on April 5, 2021, but failed to also 
provide live access to the meeting via remote means.  
  
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s 
response to the complaint and the complaint filed with our office requesting further review.  In 
addition, we reviewed the notice and open session minutes of the Board meeting held on April 5, 
2021. 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On Thursday, April 1, 2021, at 3:02 P.M., the Board posted 
notice for a meeting to be held on Monday, April 5, 2021, at 6:30 P.M.  The notice identified the 
location of the meeting as Dudley Municipal Complex, 71 West Main Street in Dudley, Room 
321A.  The notice then listed the specific topics to be discussed.

The April 5, 2021, meeting was held as planned and four of the five Board members were 
present in the physical meeting location at the Dudley Municipal Complex while one member 
participated remotely.  The Board met for approximately two and one-half hours and discussed 
the noticed topics.  The Board did not broadcast the meeting in real time or record the meeting.   
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the  
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Committee of  
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement  
that meetings of public bodies be open and accessible to the public.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except  
when meeting in executive session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”).  
 
On March 10, 2020, Governor Charles Baker declared a state of emergency in response 
to the public health emergency created by the outbreak of the 2019 novel Coronavirus (“COVID-
19”).  See Executive Order No. 591 (Mar. 10, 2020).  On March 12, 2020, Governor Baker 
issued an executive order temporarily suspending certain provisions of the Open Meeting Law 
during the state of emergency.  See Order Suspending Certain Provisions of the Open Meeting 
Law, G.L. c. 30A, § 20 (Mar. 12, 2020) (the “Executive Order”).  The Executive Order relieves a 
public body from the requirement to hold its meetings in locations that are physically accessible 
to the public only if the public body provides adequate, alternative means of public access, such 
as through telephone, internet or satellite enabled audio or video conferencing or any other 
technology that enables the public to clearly follow the proceedings of the public body while 
those activities are occurring.  Executive Order at Section (1).  The Executive Order allows 
public bodies to provide the public with “adequate, alternative” access to meetings instead of 
providing in-person access, but does not require public bodies to provide “adequate, alternative” 
access if in-person access is provided. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Board met in person on April 5, 2021, but did not provide 
live remote access to those proceedings.  When all public body members participate in a meeting 
remotely or when a public body meets in person without providing the public physical access to 
the meeting location, the public body must then provide the public with adequate, alternative 
access to the meeting, which requires access to the proceedings in real time.  See OML 2020-
111; OML 2020-100; Executive Order at Section (1).  However, a public body may hold in-
person meetings, as long as access to the meeting includes the opportunity to be physically 
present, as well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the members of the public body.  
See OML 2020-100; OML 2017-135; OML 2016-54.1  Here, the Board posted notice for a 
meeting to be held on April 5, 2021, in Room 321A of the Dudley Municipal Complex and a 
quorum of the Board was present in this physical location on April 5, 2021.  We have not been 
presented with any evidence that a member of the public sought to physically attend the April 5, 
2021, meeting and was denied access or that the Board limited the number of people who could 
be present in the meeting room.  Compare 2021-43 (public body violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it held a meeting in a location with a strict 12-person limit and turned away members of the

public once the room was at capacity).  Although the Board previously had been meeting 
remotely and thus consistently provided adequate, alternative remote access to its meetings in 
real-time through Dudley Cable Access Television and YouTube, it was not required to provide 
remote access to the April 5, 2021, meeting because that meeting was physically open to the 
public instead.  Moreover, the Board was not required to make an audio or video recording of the 
meeting.  See OML 2014-82; OML2013-40.  Therefore, we find that the Board did not violate 
the Open Meeting Law when it met in person and did not provide live access to the meeting via 
remote means.  To ensure the greatest transparency and consistent with the goals of the Open 
Meeting Law, we encourage public bodies to continue to offer the public alternative access to 
meetings through remote means.  
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law when it met in person on April 5, 2021, and did not provide live remote access to the 
proceedings.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.
