This office received two complaints—one on April 16, 2022, and one on June 27, 2022—
from Gary Willey alleging that the Tri-Town Board of Health (the “Board”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The first complaint was originally filed with the Board on 
or about January 21, 2022, and, following an extension of time granted by our office, James 
Wilusz, Executive Director of Public Health for the Tri-Town Health Department, responded on 
behalf of the Board by letter dated March 21, 2022. The second complaint was filed with the 
Board on or about April 16, 2022, and, following an extension of time granted by our office, you 
responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated June 10, 2022. The two complaints raise 
numerous allegations, the majority of which we must decline to review for the reasons explained 
below.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law. In 
reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaints; the Board’s 
responses; the requests for further review; and notices for Board meetings held on September 2, 
2021; December 15, 2021; January 6, 2022; and January 26, 2022. We also reviewed minutes for 
meetings held on September 22, October 27, and December 15 of 2021; and January 6, January 
26, and March 3 of 2022.  
 
To begin, we note that both the complaints and the requests for further review address at 
length the Complainant’s concerns and opinions regarding the Board’s decisions and decision-
making process with respect to mask mandates and vaccination passports. The Division of Open

Allegations We Decline t o Review 
 
We must decline to review the below allegations because they were either not timely 
raised; not sufficiently raised; or, even if true, would not constitute violations of the Open 
Meeting Law. 
 
 Allegations that were not timely raised   
 
To be considered timely, an Open Meeting Law complaint must be filed with the public 
body withi
n 30 days of the alleged violation. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(4). If the 
alleged violation could not have been known at the time it occurred—for example, if the 
violation took place in executive session—then the complaint must be filed within 30 days of the 
date on which the alleged violation should reasonably have been discovered. 940 CMR 29.05(4). 
When reviewing the timeliness of a complaint we consider when the action that is alleged to 
have violated the Open Meeting Law was reasonably discoverable, not when an individual 
complainant became aware of the action or of the requirements of the Open Meeting Law. See 
OML Declination 2-8-22 (Milton Select Board); OML Declination 12-18-20 (Pembroke Board 
of Health); OML Declination 5-21-12 (Montague Board of Selectmen). Violations that take 
place during an open session can reasonably be discovered at the time they occur. See OML 
2012-52.1 
 
To begin, the first complaint, filed with the Board on or about January 21, 2022, alleges 
that th
e notice for the Board’s December 15, 2021, meeting was insufficient. The second 
complaint, filed with the Board on or about April 16, 2022, alleges that notices for Board 
meetings held on September 2, 2021, and January 26, 2022, were insufficient, and again alleges 
that the December 15, 2021, meeting notice was insufficient. All three notices were for open 
session meetings; therefore, any deficiencies with respect to the notices were reasonably 
discoverable on the date each meeting occurred. Because the complaints were filed more than 30 
days after the meetings occurred, we find these allegations to be untimely and decline to review 
them. See OML Declination 11-27-19 (Marion Board of Assessors).  
 
 Although we decline to review the above allegations, we remind the Board  that g

the anticipated nature of the topics to be discussed. See 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2021-121; 
OML 2021-126. In the Board’s March 21, 2022, response, it acknowledged that generic notice 
topics are insufficient and committed to providing more detailed descriptions of the topics 
anticipated for discussion going forward.

 Finally, the
  second complaint, filed with the Board on or about April 16, 2022, raises 
several allegations concerning the sufficiency of minutes for meetings held on September 2,

meeting minutes was approved in open session, we find the complaint to be untimely with 
respect to the allegations that the minutes are insufficient. Therefore, we decline to review these 
allegations. See OML Declination 6-6-22 (Truro School Committee) (“When a public body 
approves open session minutes, a complaint regarding the sufficiency or accuracy of those 
minutes generally must be filed within 30 days of the meeting during which they were 
approved.”); OML Declination 11-27-19 (Marion Board of Assessors). 
 
 Although we decline to formally review the sufficiency of the meeting minutes, we note 
that 
based on our brief review of the minutes they appear to be insufficient. The minutes are 
essentially a bulleted list of the topics discussed and actions taken, often stating that a discussion 
occurred without summarizing the discussion. The Open Meeting Law requires that meeting 
minutes include more than a statement that a public body held a discussion about a specified 
topic; the Law requires that the minutes summarize the substance of the public body’s 
discussion. See OML 2020-53; OML 2019-167; OML 2018-8. We remind the Board of its 
obligation to create and maintain accurate minutes of all meetings. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). 
 
 Alleg
ation not sufficiently specific   
 
 The second complaint alleges that the Board “communicates with the private internet 
function, chat, at their meetings. Information in the chat is not displayed for all to see and 
therefore in violation of 940 CMR 29.10(6)(a) for permitted types of remote technology.”  
Complaints must allege violations with a degree of specificity, as our office will not conduct 
broad a
udits of public bodies based on generalized allegations. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); OML 
2016-57; OML 2012-106. Where the complaint does not identify any particular meeting during 
which the Board is alleged to have communicated via chat, we decline to review this allegation. 
Although no meeting was identified in the complaint, the Board did respond to this allegation 
stating that to its knowledge a quorum of the Board has never communicated regarding Board 
business via text or chat during any meeting.  
 
 Alleg
ations that, even if true, would not constitute violations of the Open Meeting Law 
 
 The first complaint alleges that the Board considered limiting public comment and 
requiring members of the public to provide their names and addresses in order to participate in 
public comment. We understand the first complaint to also allege that members of the public 
were not allowed to comment during the Board’s December 15, 2021, meeting. The Open 
Meeting Law does not require that a public body allow public participation, but rather gives the 
chair of the public body discretion to decide whether to permit public comment during a meeting.

See G.L. c. 30A § 20(g) (“[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body without 
permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”); see also 
OML 2020-77; OML 2017-189. If public participation will be permitted, the chair may set 
limitations on participation. See OML 2020-114; OML 2018-116 (“The Committee’s procedure 
for public comment—asking members of the public to identify themselves on a sign-up sheet—is 
a permissible exercise of the chair’s discretion to set the terms of public comment.”); OML 
2017-189. Therefore, even assuming the allegations raised are true, they would not constitute 
violations of the Open Meeting Law and we decline to review them. 
 
Allegation We Review
   
 
The first complaint, filed with the Board on January 20, 2022, alleges that the notice for 
the Board’s January 6, 2022, meeting was posted at the Town of Lee town hall but was not also 
posted to the Board’s website as is customary.  
 
The Board is a regional public body, serving the towns of Lee, Lenox, and Stockbridge. 
For meetings of regional public bodies, notice shall be filed and posted in each city or town 
within the region in the manner prescribed for local public bodies. G.L. c. 30A, § 20(c). 
Alternatively, a regional public body may, by majority vote, adopt the regional public body’s 
website as its official notice posting method. 940 CMR 29.03(3)(b). If a regional public body 
adopts a website as its official notice posting method, it must notify the Attorney General and 
provide the website address where notices will be posted; the most current notice posting method 
on file with the Attorney General must consistently be used. 940 CMR 29.03(1)(c).

The B
oard has not adopted its website as its official notice posting location, as the 
complaint concedes in acknowledging that the notice for the January 6, 2022, meeting “was 
posted at Lee Town hall as required by law . . . .” Furthermore, the Board posted notice at the 
town halls of Lenox and Stockbridge as well. Therefore, we find that the Board did not violate 
the Open Meeting Law when it posted notice for its January 6, 2022, meeting at the town hall of 
each member town—Lee, Lenox, and Stockbridge—but did not also post notice on its website. 
Although we do not find a violation here, if the Board does customarily post notices to its 
website, we encourage the Board to do so consistently.
