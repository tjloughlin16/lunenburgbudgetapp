 RE:        Open Meeting Law Complaints 
 
Dear Attorney Howayeck: 
 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on November 26, 2025, and a 
second complaint on February 3, 2026, alleging that the Fall River Board of Park Commissioners 
(the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The first complaint was 
originally filed with the Board on September 30, 2025, and you responded to the complaint, on 
behalf of the Board, by letter dated November 26, 2025.  The second complaint was originally 
filed with the Board on December 4, 2025, and you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the 
Board, by letter dated January 7, 2026, following an extension of time granted by our office.  
The first complaint alleges that the Board created insufficiently detailed minutes of the meeting 
held on September 3, 2025.  The second complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely 
respond to the first Open Meeting Law complaint and discussed the complaint without listing it 
in the October 1, 2025, meeting notice.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to respond to the first Open Meeting Law complaint in the timeframe required by the law.  We 
find that the Board did not violate the law by discussing the complaint during the October 1, 
2025, meeting, even though it was not listed in the notice.  We decline to review the allegation 
raised in the first complaint for the reasons discussed below.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaints, the Board’s responses to the complaints, and the complaints 
filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice, minutes and

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Board held a meeting on September 3, 2025, in the 
Council Hearing Room at One Government Center in Fall River.  Board Chair BJ MacDonald 
convened the meeting at 5:30 P.M., at which time four other members were present.  Board 
member Nicholas Cecilio arrived at the meeting around 5:55 P.M. 
 
On September 29, 2025, at 2:39 P.M., the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on 
October 1, 2025, at 5:30 P.M.  The notice listed 14 topics, one of which was “Acceptance of 
minutes from September 3, 2025 regular Park Board meeting.”  The draft minutes listed the 
members who were present at the September 3, 2025, meeting as follows: Commissioner Victor 
Farias, Commissioner Helen Rego, Commissioner Nicholas Cecilio (arrived late), Commissioner 
Amber Burns.   
 
On September 30, 2025, the complainant filed the first Open Meeting Law complaint 
alleging that the Board created insufficiently detailed minutes of the September 3, 2025, meeting 
by failing to include the time that Mr. Cecilio arrived at the meeting.   
 
The October 1, 2025, meeting was held as planned.  During the meeting, the Board 
reviewed the minutes of the September 3, 2025, meeting and Member Amber Burns made a 
motion to “amend the minutes to show that Commissioner Cecilio arrived at 5:55” P.M.  The 
motion was seconded by Member Victor Farias and the Board unanimously voted to amend the 
minutes.  Next, Chair MacDonald asked the Board for a motion to send the Open Meeting Law 
complaint, which had been received the day before on September 30, 2025, to corporation 
counsel.  Ms. Burns made a motion to “send to corporation counsel” the complaint, which 
motion was seconded by Mr. Cecilio.  The Board then unanimously voted to send the complaint 
to corporation counsel without discussing the substance of the complaint.  Then, Ms. Burns made 
“a motion to approve the amended minutes” of the September 3, 2025, meeting, to reflect the 
time that Mr. Cecilio arrived at the meeting.  The motion was seconded by Mr. Cecilio and the 
Board unanimously voted to approve the amended minutes.  The Board then discussed the 
remaining topics. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy

requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.2  By substantial compliance, we mean that the 
minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not 
attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See 
OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a 
transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion 
presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55. 
 
The first complaint alleges that the minutes of the Board meeting held on September 3, 
2025, do not indicate what time Mr. Cecilio arrived at the meeting.  At the time the complaint at 
issue here was filed, the September 3, 2025, meeting minutes had not been approved and were in 
draft form.  A public body may revise draft minutes at any point prior to their approval to correct 
inaccuracies or add detail.  See OML 2014-62.  Accordingly, because these minutes remained in 
draft form at the time the complaint was filed, we decline to review their sufficiency.  See OML 
2019-11; OML Declination 12-18-20 (Fall River Housing Authority Board of Commissioners). 
 
Nonetheless, we note that the Open Meeting Law regulations require that if, during a 
meeting at which a member of the public body participates remotely, “technical difficulties result 
in a remote participant being disconnected from the meeting, that fact and the time at which the 
disconnection occurred shall be noted in the meeting minutes.”  940 CMR 29.10(6)(d).  
However, the Open Meeting Law contains no express requirement that the minutes record a 
member’s specific arrival or departure time at a meeting that was attended in person or for 
reasons other than technical difficulties.  See OML 2022-52.  We commend the Board for 
revising the minutes to reflect the time that Mr. Cecilio arrived at the meeting and encourage 
public bodies to include such information as best practice and for purposes of openness and 
transparency. 
 
Next, we consider whether the Board properly discussed a topic not listed on the notice of 
the meeting held on October 1, 2025, as alleged in the second complaint.  The Open Meeting 
Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at least 48 hours prior to such 
meeting” and that the notice include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of 
topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b).  Public bodies are required to list topics on a notice with “sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(1) 
(b).  We generally consider a topic sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public 
could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See 
OML 2015-35; OML 2012-71.    
 
During the meeting held on October 1, 2025, the Board unanimously voted to forward the 
first Open Meeting Law complaint to corporation counsel for response.  The Board 
acknowledges that the complaint was not reflected in the notice of the October 1, 2025, meeting.  
However, a public body may discuss a topic not listed on a meeting notice if the topic was not 
reasonably anticipated by the chair 48 hours in advance of the meeting.  See OML 2017-2; OML 
2013-87.  Here, the complaint was filed on September 30, 2025, the day before the meeting, and

thus the topic could not have been anticipated by the chair 48 hours before the meeting.  As such, 
we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law by discussing the complaint during 
the October 1, 2025, meeting.  See OML 2023-124; OML 2017-2; OML 2013-87.  Although the 
Board permissibly discussed the unanticipated topic without adding it to the meeting notice, our 
recommended practice is to amend the meeting notice, if time permits, to add any additional 
topics that arise during the 48 hours before the meeting takes place, so that members of the 
public are given advance notice of the items to be discussed.  See OML 2020-122. 
 
Finally, the second complaint alleges that the Board failed to discuss and timely respond 
to the first Open Meeting Law complaint.  The Open Meeting Law requires that within 14 
business days of receipt of a complaint, the public body review the complaint’s allegations; take 
remedial action, if appropriate; send to the complainant a response and description of any 
remedial action taken; and simultaneously send to the Attorney General a copy of the complaint 
and a description of any remedial action taken.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  A 
public body must meet to review the complaint and formulate a response, or delegate the 
response, and respond to the complaint within 14 business days.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 
CMR 29.05(5); OML 2022-43; OML 2017-69; OML 2012-90.   
 
During the October 1, 2025, meeting, Chair MacDonald announc
 ed that the Board had 
received an Open Meeting Law complaint and asked for a motion to send the complaint to 
corporation counsel for response.  The Board then voted by majority vote to send the complaint 
to corporation counsel.  While we acknowledge that the Board did not substantively discuss the 
allegations raised in the complaint, the Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to 
engage in a substantive discussion of the merits of a complaint, nor to read a complaint in full 
during a meeting.  See OML 2022-59; OML 2021-128; OML 2020-38; OML 2018-134.  It is the 
public body’s prerogative as to whether it will discuss the substance of the complaint or simply 
review the complaint and authorize a response; the public body is not required to discuss the 
substance of the complaint in detail.  See OML 2021-128; OML 2019-40; OML 2017-96.  We 
find that the Board satisfied its obligations under the Open Meeting Law. 
 
However, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to respond to 
the f
irst complaint within the time frame required by the law.  Although the Board reviewed the 
complaint and voted to delegate corporation counsel to respond within 14 business days, 
corporation counsel, due to administrative error, failed to respond to the complaint until 
November 26, 2025—40 business days after the complaint was filed.  While it is the public 
body’s prerogative to simply refer a complaint to legal counsel for response after the body has 
reviewed that complaint during a meeting, a response to the complaint must still occur within 14 
business days, unless the body secures an extension from the Director of the Division of Open 
Government.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5); OML 2019-40; OML 2017-69.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to respond to an Open Meeting Law complaint within 14 business days.  We order 
immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
