 RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Driscoll: 
 
This office received a complaint from Christine McCue Potts on November 14, 2025, 
alleging that the Medfield Housing Authority Board of Commissioners (the “Board”) violated 
the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the 
Board on or about September 9, 2025, and you responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated 
October 7, 2025, following an extension of time granted by this office.  The complaint alleges 
that the Board created insufficient minutes of the meeting held on June 10, 2025. 
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter.  Following our 
review, we find that the Board violated Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.  In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and 
the complaint filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the 
notice, open session minutes and audio recording of the Board meeting held on June 10, 2025.1   
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting,

including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.2  By substantial compliance, we mean that the 
minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not 
attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See 
OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a 
transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion 
presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55. 
 
The complaint alleges 11 instances where the June 10, 2025, meeting minutes, which 
were approved on August 12, 2025, failed to include an accurate summary of the Board’s 
discussions.3  Specifically, the complaint alleges three instances where the minutes are 
insufficient or inaccurate with respect to the Board’s discussion of the Executive Director’s 
report, seven instances where the minutes are insufficient or inaccurate with respect to the 
Board’s discussion of the Annual Plan, and one instance where the minutes inaccurately refer to 
a “Fair Housing Policy” the Board discussed under the topic of “Old Business.” 
 
With respect to the Executive Director’s Report, the minutes state, in pertinent part, the 
following: 1) “1 vacancy: Second floor unit.  Unit offer was accepted by applicant at top of list 
and set for lease this past Friday.  Applicant did not show up.  Next approved applicant 
submitting completed information will be offered unit.  2 bedroom unit will become vacant at the 
end of the month as current resident is moving in with family.  Unit turnover will be completed 
and CHAMP pull lists have already begun in order to lease unit;” 2) “Generator Project: Will be 
complete and powered up by Friday of the week;” and 3) “RSC: doing really well and has been 
well received by the tenants.  She will do a tenant flyer monthly and hold a coffee hour this 
month for residents.  She will also attend the event tomorrow to meet everyone and assist.” 
 
After listening to an audio recording of the meeting, we find that the minutes inaccurately 
summarize the discussion about the Resident Service Coordinator (“RSC”).  During the meeting, 
the Executive Director did not state that the RSC was “doing really well,” had “been well 
received by the tenants,” or would “do a tenant flyer monthly and hold a coffee hour.”  Rather, 
the Executive Director explained that the RSC could help residents with recertification, food 
stamp benefits, tenant events, and intakes for health services.  To be “accurate,” meeting minutes 
must accurately reflect the discussions that took place during a meeting.  See OML 2020-62.  
Because the minutes must accurately capture the discussions and actions that actually took place, 
it is inappropriate to include information in meeting minutes that was not discussed or did not 
occur at a meeting.  See OML 2021-128.

After listening to the audio recording, we find that the minutes inaccurately summarize 
two matters during the Board’s discussion of the Annual Plan.  First, we find the summary of the 
resident’s comments to be inaccurate as Mr. Castillo did not mention that tenants were treated 
with courtesy and respect or that the residents were happy but simply commented that the 
vocational high school students were doing a great job.  Second, we find that the motion to 
approve the Annual Plan is not accurately reflected in the minutes.  The motion made by Board 
member Rob Canavan was to approve the Annual Plan, not approval of the plan “with the 
correction on the noted Reserve Level Error” as reflected in the minutes.  While we acknowledge 
that the Board discussed an error in the Annual Plan with respect to the Operating Reserve Level, 
and the Executive Director agreed with the error and committed to correcting it, the actual 
motion to approve the Annual Plan did not include a reference to make the correction.  We note 
that the additional detail with respect to the motion that was included in the minutes does 
enhance openness and transparency by providing a more thorough record regarding the matter 
that was approved during the meeting.  If the Board wishes to include additional context or detail 
in the minutes that might be helpful to the public in understanding a motion or discussion, it may 
do so but must clearly label such information as included for background or context, rather than 
inaccurately presenting the information as if it were part of the motion or discussion during the 
meeting.  See OML 2024-148. 
 
Finally, with respect to the Board’s discussion of the “Fair Housing Policy,” we note that 
the Board did not actually use that term during the meeting.  Rather, it referred to the policy as 
the Fair Housing Market Plan and the motion made by Mr. Canavan was to reapprove the “Fair 
Housing Market Plan,” not the “Fair Housing Policy” as referenced in the minutes.  As such, we 
find that the term “Fair Housing Policy” included in the minutes does not accurately reflect the 
discussion or the motion that was made during the June 10, 2025, meeting.  See OML 2024-103.

We find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by drafting and approving 
inaccurate minutes for its June 10, 2025, meeting in the ways alleged above.  We find that the 
minutes are otherwise adequate and sufficiently summarize the discussions that occurred during 
the meeting. 
 
We must determine whether this violation was, as the complainant urges, an intentional 
one.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body 
or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An 
intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the 
law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting 
Law.  Id.  As of August 12, 2025, the date that the minutes were reviewed and approved, our 
office twice found that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by creating insufficient 
meeting minutes.  See OML 2026-57; OML 2025-6.  In OML 2025-6, we found that the Board 
created insufficient meeting minutes where the minutes did not include a list of documents used 
at the meeting, failed to sufficiently summarize a lengthy discussion and failed to summarize 
another discussion entirely.  In OML 2025-57, we found that the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law by failing to include in two sets of minutes a list of documents used at the 
meetings.  We find the circumstances here to be different from those present in both OML 2025-
6 and OML 2025-57 and the evidence before us does not support a finding that the Board 
intentionally violated the Open Meeting Law.  
 
We acknowledge that, on August 25, 2026, the Attorney General found that the Board 
violated the Open Meeting Law by creating and approving inaccurate minutes.  See OML 2026-
114.  However, we do not rely on the August 2026 determination to find an intentional violation 
because, at the time the June 10, 2025, meeting minutes were approved, the Board had not yet 
been advised by the Attorney General that minutes must accurately reflect the discussions that 
take place during a meeting.  As such, we decline to find that the violation here was intentional.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
creating insufficient and inaccurate minutes of the meeting held on June 10, 2025.  We order the 
Board to revise the minutes of the meeting held on June 10, 2025, in accordance with the 
guidance provided above, within 30 days of the date of this letter.  In addition, we order the 
Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that 
similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law. 
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Board.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
