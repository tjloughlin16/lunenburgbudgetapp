Dear Mr. Murphy: 
 
This office received a complaint from Deborah Riley on July 16, 2025, alleging that the 
Quincy Cemetery Board of Managers (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about May 30, 2025.  
The Board did not respond to the complaint.
1  The complaint alleges that the Board failed to 
timely post notice of a meeting held on May 1, 2025.    
 
We resolve this complaint by informal action in ac
 cordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review, and the notice and minutes of the Board meeting held on May 1, 2025.  In addition, we 
communicated with you via email on October 16, 2025.  Following our review, we find that the 
Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.   
 
 
1 In an October 16, 2025, email communication, you explained that the Board reviewed the complaint during a 
meeting held on September 4, 2025, and then forwarded the complaint to the City Solicitor for response.  However, 
neither the complainant nor our office received any response.  We remind the Board that the Open Meeting Law 
requires that within 14 business days of receipt of a complaint, the public body must meet to review the complaint’s 
allegations; take remedial action, if appropriate; send to the complainant a response and a description of any 
remedial action taken; and send to the Attorney General a copy of the complaint and a description of any remedial 
action taken.  G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).

Except in the case of an emergency, the Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies 
post not
ice of every meeting “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, 
Sundays and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  “Notice shall be printed in a legible, easily 
understandable format and shall contain the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of 
topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  Id.   
 
On Wednesday, April 30, 2025, at 12:42 P.M., the Board posted notice of a meeting to be 
held on Thursday, May 1, 2025, at 5:30 P.M.  The meeting was held as planned and the Board 
elected a Chair and Vice Chair, approved the minutes of the April 3, 2025, meeting, and received 
updates from the Commissioner of Natural Resources on several cemetery projects.  We find that 
the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by not properly posting notice 48 hours prior to the 
May 1, 2025, meeting.  We commend the Board for taking remedial action in response to the 
complaint by properly posting notice for a meeting to be held on September 4, 2025, by listing 
on the notice the election of officers, and then nominating members for the position of Chair and 
Vice Chair and voting anew during the September 4, 2025, meeting.    
 
With respect to the concerns raised in the complaint that the Board failed to post meeting 
minutes, the Open Law Meeting does not require that public bodies post their minutes to a 
website or elsewhere, but only that minutes be created, maintained, and approved in a timely 
manner, and made available within 10 days of request.  G.L. c. 30A, §§22(a), (c); OML 2020-78; 
OML 2017-98.
2  “Timely manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 
30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 
CMR 29.11; see OML 2018-48.  Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a meeting 
be approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2018-67; OML 2017-133.  There is no specific 
allegation that the complainant requested the minutes and did not receive them, or that the 
minutes were not timely approved.  Nonetheless, we remind the Board of its obligations under 
the law and note that minutes for eight of the nine Board meetings held in 2025 are posted to the 
municipal website.
3   
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law.  We 
order immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar 
future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
