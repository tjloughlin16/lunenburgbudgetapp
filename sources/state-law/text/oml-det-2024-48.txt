DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  Except when convened in executive session, “all 
meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a).  A “meeting” is 
defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the 
body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The Open Meeting Law defines “deliberation” broadly 
as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including electronic mail, between or 
among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction; . . . .”  Id.  
“Deliberation” is broadly defined to include any communication between or among a quorum 
regarding business within the Board's jurisdiction, with limited exceptions, even if no opinion is 
expressed and no action is taken. See OML 2023-224; OML 2020-18; OML 2019-151. 5   
 
Explicitly excluded from the definition of “deliberation” are the distribution of a meeting 
agenda, scheduling information, or reports or documents to be discussed at a meeting, provided 
no opinion of a member is expressed.  G.L. c. 30A, § 18; See also OML 2023-139; OML 2021-
112.  These exceptions are construed narrowly.  See OML 2023-139; OML 2016-172; OML 
2014-51.  We have found limited administrative communication to fall outside the definition of 
deliberation.  See OML 2022-133; OML 2021-122 (mathematical computations to aggregate 
evaluation scores outside a meeting did not constitute a violation where members did not 
substantively discuss the evaluations); OML 2013-160 (signing certification forms outside of a 
posted meeting was an administrative task that did not constitute deliberation).  Although certain 
administrative tasks are excluded from the definition of “deliberation,” that exception includes a 
strong caveat that no opinion of a member is expressed.  See OML 2019-78.  Determining which 
communications are purely “administrative” can be challenging.  See OML 2021-112; OML 
2013-6 (noting that it is often difficult to determine whether communications are administrative 
or substantive in nature).

The communications that are the subject of the instant complaint were among a quorum 
of the Board, as they involved two or three members of the three-member Board.  Additionally, 
the matters discussed (the meeting agenda, Open Meeting Law complaints and correspondence 
to the Board) clearly fell within the Board’s jurisdiction.  Therefore, the communications 
constituted improper deliberation unless subject to one of the narrow exceptions set out in the 
Open Meeting Law.  While it is a close call, we find that the communications did not constitute 
deliberation.  Chair Oney verbally highlighted the additions to the meeting notice; the same 
information could have been gleaned from a comparison of the two documents.  She also 
explained that anonymous correspondence was removed from the meeting materials.  Members 
Warren and Morrell conferred to determine how much of a document should be read into the 
record.6  They did not engage in a substantive discussion of the November 13 Complaint.  The 
exchange between Messrs. Warren and Morrell can be distinguished from improper deliberations 
about how to run a meeting.  See, e.g., OML 2017-91 (discussion during ten-minute recess 
between newly appointed chair and prior chair regarding procedural steps to address a late 
submission and request to continue Zoning Board of Appeals hearing violated the Open Meeting 
Law.) 
 
We reiterate that it can be difficult to determine whether, under the circumstances, a 
given communication constitutes deliberation under the Open Meeting Law.  Like electronic 
communications, exchanges between members before the commencement of a meeting run the 
risk of crossing the line into deliberations and we caution public bodies to be vigilant in limiting 
such communications to the narrow exceptions explicitly set out in the Open Meeting Law.  See 
OML 2023-126; OML 2017-88; OML 2014-80. 
 
CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law as alleged.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  
This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or 
the Commission.  Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963 - 2540 if you have any 
questions.
