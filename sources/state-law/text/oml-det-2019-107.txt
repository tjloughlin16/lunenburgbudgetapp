RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint
Dear Attorney Assad:
This office received 14 complaints from Collin Dias on January 7, 2019, and January 22, 
2019, alleging that the Fall River School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaints were originally filed with the Committee 
on December 5, 2018; December 7, 2018; December 11, 2018; and December 28, 2018, and you 
responded to the complaints, on behalf of the Committee, by letters dated December 24, 2018, 
and January 18, 2019. Complaints 1-4 allege violations during the November 13, 2018, meeting; 
complaints 5-10 and 12 allege violations during the December 10, 2018, meeting; complaint 11 
alleges a violation regarding the Committee’s response on December 24, 2018; and complaints 
13 and 14 allege violations regarding the meeting notice for the January 11, 2019, meeting.
Mr. Dias did not request further review by our office of complaints 6, 13 and 14, and he 
withdrew complaint 3. This letter addresses the remaining complaints, in turn. We resolve these 
complaints by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), after reviewing the 
original complaints, the Committee’s responses to the complaint, and the complaints filed with 
our office requesting further review. Additionally, we reviewed the videos of the November 13, 
2018, and December 10, 2018, meetings, and the notice and minutes from the December 10, 
2018, meeting.1 We find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to timely 
approve minutes from its August 20, 2018, meeting, but the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law in any of the other ways alleged.
Complaints 1 and 5
In complaints 1 and 5, Mr. Dias alleges that during the November 13, 2018, and 
December 10, 2018, meetings, the Chair violated the Open Meeting Law when he asked 
everyone who attended the meetings for award recognition to leave after that portion of the 
meeting had concluded. The Committee invited students who had obtained exemplary scores on

the MCAS exam to attend the November 13 meeting and educators who had received awards to 
attend the December 10 meeting to be recognized. During both meetings, after concluding the 
awards recognition portion of the meeting, the Chair told award recipients and their families that 
they were welcome to leave the meeting if they wished. Access to a meeting must include the 
opportunity to be physically present, as well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the 
members of the public body. See OML 2018-138; OML 2013-189. We have stated that a public 
body can request individuals to voluntarily leave or remain outside a meeting room, such as 
when a public body is performing interviews of multiple applicants and prefers that applicants 
not hear the responses of their competitors, so long as the public body does not direct or exclude 
members of the public from public portions of a meeting. See OML 2018-138. Here, the Chair 
did not even request that members of the public voluntarily leave the meeting, but merely 
notified the awards recipients that they were welcome to leave if they did not wish to stay for the 
entire meeting. We therefore find that the Chair’s announcement did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law.
Complaint 2
In complaint 2, Mr. Dias alleges that the Chair disrupted public comment through 
“annoying and childish antics.” Although the Attorney General encourages public bodies to 
allow members of the public to speak during public comments periods whenever possible, the 
Open Meeting Law does not require that members of the public be permitted to speak, and it is 
within a chair's discretion to limit participation during public comment periods. See OML 20lb- 
82; OML 2016-51; OML 2015-12; OML 2014-23. Therefore, the facts as alleged, even if true, 
would not amount to a violation of the Open Meeting Law.
Complaints 4 and 8
In complaints 4 and 8, Mr. Dias alleges that the Chair did not state the purpose for 
entering Executive Session as required by the Open Meeting Law, as the Committee’s legal 
counsel instead stated the purposes for the executive sessions. The Open Meeting Law 
specifically provides that the chair shall state the purpose for the executive session. G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 21(b)(3); OML 2016-75. We have consistently stated that a chair may delegate such 
responsibility to another individual, such as counsel. See OML 2019-96; OML 2016-124; OML 
2016-108. During both the November 13 and December 10 meetings, the Chair delegated his 
authority to legal counsel for the purpose of announcing the executive session. See OML 2016- 
108 (finding an implied delegation of authority when the Chair asked legal counsel whether there 
was a reason to enter executive session, and legal counsel then stated the purposes for the 
executive session). Accordingly, the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law when 
legal counsel stated the purposes for the executive sessions.
Complaint 7
In complaint 7, Mr. Dias alleges both that the Chair did not follow proper protocol for 
approving meeting minutes and that the Committee did not timely approve meeting minutes from 
August 20, 2018. First, the Open Meeting Law does not govern the method for approving 
meeting minutes and public bodies may choose the method for approval. See OML 2019-9;
FAQ on Meeting Minutes https://www.mass.gov/info-details/frequently-asked-questions-about-

the-open-meeting-law. Therefore, the Committee’s method of approving meeting minutes did 
not violate the Open Meeting Law.
Second, the Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive session.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The meeting 
minutes must be reviewed and approved in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c); 940 CMR 
29.11. “Timely manner’ means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days 
whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.” 940 CMR 
29.11; See OML 2019-91; OML 2018-48. Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a 
meeting be approved at the next meeting. See OML 2019-91; OML 2018-67; OML 2017-133. 
The Committee acknowledges that it did not approve the August 20, 2018, meeting within the 
required timeframe, but asserts that it had good cause for the delay because of a staffing 
shortage. We understand the demands on a public body with limited staff; however, the 
Committee is still obligated to timely approve minutes. See OML 2019-72; OML 2019-21;
OML 2018-111; OML 2017-115. The responsibility for complying with the Open Meeting Law 
rests with public bodies and members of such bodies, not with individual public employees, such 
as administrative staff. See OML 2019-72; OML 2019-21; OML 2018-105. If staff resources 
are not available to create minutes for the Committee to approve, then Committee members must 
take on this responsibility to ensure compliance with the law. See OML 2019-72; OML 2017- 
115. We note that the Committee has since approved the minutes in question. Therefore, we 
order no additional relief.
Complaint 9
In complaint 9, Mr. Dias alleges that the phrase “vote to approve” listed in relation to 
eight topics under the heading “Discussion” on the School Committee’s December 10 meeting 
notice violates the Open Meeting Law as it constitutes the chair improperly giving “his opinion 
on what should be approved or not.” We disagree. The phrase “vote to approve” is commonly 
used on meeting notices to indicate that a vote will be taken on a particular matter; we do not 
construe it to mean that a chair or a public body has already decided how it will vote. We also 
have stated that the Open Meeting Law does not generally require that a meeting notice include 
an indication that a vote will be taken at all, although including that notation provides greater 
clarity to the public. See OML 2019-95; OML 2017-186; OML 2014-112; OML 2014-44. 
Therefore, we find no violation.
Complaint 10
In complaint 10, Mr. Dias alleges that an Addendum to the Executive Session at the 
Committee’s December 10 meeting “was never read,” “was never listed on the agenda” and “it 
[was] unclear what the addendum was.” The Open Meeting Law requires that “a public body 
shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, 
Sundays and legal holidays,” and include in that notice “a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.” G.L. c. 30A, §20(b). A public body 
may discuss a topic that was not included in the meeting notice if the chair did not reasonably 
anticipate the discussion of that topic 48 hours in advance of the meeting. See OML 2015-73; 
OML 2012-3; OML 2011-13.

Here, the Addendum included an Executive Session topic regarding a complaint that Mr. 
Dias had filed with the Committee on Friday, December 7, less than 48 hours, not including 
weekends, before the Committee’s meeting on Monday, December 10. Thus, the Chair could not 
have reasonably anticipated the topic at the time the meeting notice was posted. Additionally, 
contrary to Mr. Dias’s assertion that the “[AJddendum was never read,” the video and minutes of 
the December 10 meeting show that the Committee’s attorney indeed read the executive session 
topic listed on the Addendum into the record, prior to entering executive session. Therefore, the 
Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged.
Complaint 11
In complaint 11, Mr. Dias alleges that that the Committee did not address its response to 
one of his Open Meeting Law complaints to him, as the complainant, but rather addressed its 
response to the Attorney General’s Office. Under the Open Meeting Law, a “public body has 14 
business days from the date of receipt to meet to review the complainant’s allegations, to take 
remedial action if appropriate, notify the complainant of the remedial action, and forward a copy 
of the complaint and description of the remedial action taken to the complainant.” 940 CMR 
29.05(5). Here, Mr. Dias admits that the Committee notified him, simultaneously with the 
Attorney General’s Office, of its response to his Open Meeting Law complaint. Thus, we find 
no violation.
Complaint 12
In complaint 12, Mr. Dias alleges that the Chair, Mayor Jasiel F. Correia II, cannot be a 
member of the Committee as he is an individual “facing felonies.” Even if true, this allegation 
would not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2019-85. As such, we 
decline to review this allegation. See OML Declination 7-23-18 (Norwell Board of Selectmen); 
OML Declination 1-25-16 (Nahant Housing Authority Board of Commissioners). We offer no 
opinion on whether any actions taken by the Committee or its Chair could be a violation of some 
other law or regulation outside the scope of the Division’s review.
As explained above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law as 
alleged in complaint 7 by failing to approve meeting minutes in a timely manner but did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged in any of the other nine complaints addressed by this 
determination. We order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s 
requirements and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent 
to violate the law. We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be 
resolved. This determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with 
the Board or with our office. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you 
have any questions.
