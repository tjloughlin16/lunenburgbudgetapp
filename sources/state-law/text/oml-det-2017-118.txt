This office received a complaint from Robert Strayton, dated March 1, 2017, alleging that 
the Chappaquiddick Cellular Committee (the "Committee") violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on February 1, 
2017, and the Committee responded by letter dated February 28, 2017. In his complaint, Mr. 
Strayton alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by: (i) deliberating outside 
of a noticed meeting; (ii) failing to create meeting minutes; and (iii) failing to provide requested 
meeting minutes within the time frame required by law. In its response, the Committee argues 
that it is not a public body subject to the Open Meeting Law. 
Following our review, we find that the Committee is a public body subject to the Open 
Meeting Law. Further, we find that the Committee failed to post notice of one meeting. 
However, we find that the Committee did not violate the law in the other ways alleged. In 
reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Committee's response, and the 
request for further review filed with our office. We reviewed a supplemental e-mail from the 
Committee's legal counsel dated June 1, 2017. Finally, we spoke the following parties by 
telephone: the complainant on May 10, 2017; the Committee's legal counsel on April 26 and 
June 8, 2017; and Edgartown Town Clerk Wanda Williams on June 9, 2017. 
FACTS 
We find the facts as follows. During a meeting held on June 8, 2015, the Edgartown 
Board of Selectmen created the Committee, a six-member body charged with researching and 
making recommendations on ways to improve cellular service on Chappaquiddick. As part of its

approximately 22 times following its creation during this June 8, 2015 meeting.1 
Edgartown has not adopted a municipal website for notice posting purposes, see 940

The Edgartown Planning Board held a meeting on April 19, 2016. Following this 
meeting, the complainant claims to have encountered Mr. Filley outside Edgartown Town Hall. 
At that time, the complainant alleges that he requested from Mr. Filley "all notes and meeting 
minutes of the [Committee]." Mr. Filley does not recall this request. No minutes, either in draft 
or approved form, were provided. 
DISCUSSION 
I. The Committee is a Public Body Subject to the Open Meeting Law. 
The Open Meeting Law defines a "public body" in relevant part as any "multiple-
member board, commission, committee or subcommittee .. . however created, elected, appointed 
or otherwise constituted, established to serve a public purpose." Id. at § 18. To determine 
whether a multi-member entity is a public body subject to the Open Meeting Law, there are three 
factors we consider which apply to all public bodies: 
1. The entity must be "within" government and not excluded from the definition of 
"public body"; 
2. The entity must be a "body" empowered to act collectively; and 
3. The entity must serve a "public purpose." 
See OML 2012-20; OML 2011-42; OML 2010-1.2 
Here, the Committee is within government because it was created by action of the 
Edgartown Board of Selectmen. It also serves a public purpose: gathering and analyzing 
information with the goal of improving cellular service. The Committee is tasked with 
compiling and analyzing information and providing recommendations. Such conduct constitutes 
collective action by a public body. See OML 2015-46. The Committee therefore satisfies all 
three factors. 
The Committee also contends that it is not a public body because it is a body that reports 
to the IT Manager. We note the existence of a judicially-recognized exception to the definition

of "public body" under the Open Meeting Law. The Supreme Judicial Court held in Connelly v. 
School Committee of Hanover, 409 Mass. 232, 235 (1991), that where an individual public 
official creates a committee to advise that person on a decision that the official has the sole 
authority to make, that committee is not subject to the Open Meeting Law. Had the IT Manager 
created the Committee of his own volition, then the body would likely have fit within the 
Connelly exception because it would have been created by an individual public official to advise 
him in a decision that he retained the sole authority to make. See OML 2015-93; OML 2014-56. 
The distinction here, however, is that the IT Manager did not create the Committee of his own 
volition. Rather, the Committee was created by vote of the Edgartown Board of Selectmen. We 
there find that the Committee does not fit within the Connelly exception. 
II. The Committee Failed to Post Notice of One Meeting. 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of all meetings. G.L. c. 
30A, § 20(b). The complaint alleges that Committee failed to post notice of its meetings. While 
many notices did not appear on the Edgartown municipal website, the Committee posted notices 
for the majority of its meetings on the bulletin board in the Edgartown Town Hall. Because 
Edgartown has not adopted the municipal website for notice posting, this satisfied the 
Committee's notice posting obligations under the Open Meeting Law. 940 CMR 29.03(2)(a). 
While the Committee did not post notice of the August 12, 2015 telephone call with AT&T 
representatives, less than a quorum of the Committee took part in that call. Therefore, it was not 
a "meeting" subject to the Open Meeting Law. G.L. c. 30A, § 18. However, a quorum of the 
Committee participated in the August 25, 2015 meeting without posting notice. This failure 
violated the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2017-57. 
III. The Committee Created Minutes of its Meetings. 
Public bodies are required to create minutes of all meetings. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The 
complaint also alleges that the Committee failed to create minutes of its meetings. However, 
counsel for the Committee provided our office with draft copies of certain meeting minutes, and 
we understand from our conversations with the Committee's legal counsel that draft minutes 
were created for all Committee meetings. Those minutes were approved at a Committee meeting 
held on June 15, 2017. Because the Committee created draft minutes of its meetings, we find no 
violation with respect to this allegation.3 
IV. The Allegation that the Committee Failed to Provide Meeting Minutes is 
Untimely. 
Complaints alleging violations of the Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public 
body within 30 days of the alleged violation. G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b). If the alleged violation could 
not reasonably have been known at the time it occurred, then the complaint must be filed within 
30 days of the date it should reasonably have been discovered. 940 CMR 29.05(3). Here, based 
on his April 19, 2016 interaction with Mr. Filley, the complainant alleges that the Committee 
failed to provide draft minutes within 10 days of a request, as required by G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). 
Any complaint alleging a failure to provide minutes in a timely manner would need to be filed

CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee failed to post notice for a 
meeting. We order the Committee's immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting 
Law, and we caution that similar future violations may be consider evidence of intent to violate 
the law.
