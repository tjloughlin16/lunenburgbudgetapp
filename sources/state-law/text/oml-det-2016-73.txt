RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Chair Viana: 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on April 1, 2016, alleging that 
the Fall River Contributory Retirement Board (the Board) violated the Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about March 
7, 2016, and you responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated March 28, 2016. In his 
complaint, Mr. Higgins alleges that the notice for the Board's February 26, 2016 meeting 
failed to include sufficient detail about an anticipated executive session. 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law. 
In reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board's response to the 
complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further review. We also 
reviewed the notice and minutes from the Board's February 26, 2016 meeting. 
FACTS 
We find the facts as follows. On February 19, 2016, the Board posted notice of a 
meeting scheduled for February 26, 2016. The Board listed 7 enumerated topics for open 
session, followed by an executive session topic listed as follows: "The Board may convene in 
executive session pursuant to M.G.L. c. 30A, s.21 (a)(1) to discuss/review disability retirement 
applications, conduct evidentiary hearings, confer with legal counsel on litigation matters 
and./or conduct negotiations with nonunion personnel." The Board includes this executive 
session topic on each meeting notice to notify the public that it may convene in executive 
session, if necessary. The Board did not convene in executive session during this meeting, 
however.

DISCUSSION 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted "to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberation and decisions on which public policy is based." Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge. 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Open Meeting Law requires a public body to post 
notice 48 hours in advance of a meeting and include a "listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting." G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Public bodies 
may enter a closed, executive session for any of ten purposes enumerated in the Open 
Meeting Law, provided that the chair of the public body first announces in open session the 
purpose for the executive session, "stating all subjects that may be revealed without 
compromising the purpose for which the executive session was called." G.L. c. 3OA, 
§§ 21(a), 21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. Of Wavland. 455 
Mass. 561, 567 ("[a] precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is 
necessary under the open meeting law because that is the only notification given to the public 
that the school committee would conduct business in private, and the only way the public 
would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper."). This level of detail about 
the executive session topic should also be included in the meeting notice. See G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 20(b). 
The complainant alleges that the February 26, 2016 meeting notice lacked sufficient 
detail about the anticipated executive session. The Board did not actually convene in 
executive session during this meeting. When a public body does not actually engage in 
deliberation on a noticed topic, the Open Meeting Law is not violated with respect to the 
sufficiency of the meeting notice. See OML 2015-193; OML 2015-63; OML 2014-81. 
Therefore, we find no violation of the law. Because the Board specifically asked our office 
for guidance on the issue, we provide the following examples regarding the sufficiency of 
executive session topics. When entering executive session pursuant to Purpose 3 to discuss an 
ongoing litigation matter, a public body must identify the particular litigation matter that it 
intends to discuss, both in the written notice and verbal statement made before entering 
executive session, if doing so will not compromise the executive session purpose. See G.L. 
c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); OML 2016-12. The same level of detail should be provided for a 
discussion of strategy and/or negotiation with non-union personnel. See OML 2016-43. While 
we generally defer to a public body's assessment of whether the inclusion of such infonnation 
would compromise the purpose for an executive session, a public body must be able to 
demonstrate a reasonable basis for that claim if challenged. See OML 2015-14. 
CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law. We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or 
the Board. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions
