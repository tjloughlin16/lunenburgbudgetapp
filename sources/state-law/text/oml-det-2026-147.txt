FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On or about December 5, 2025, the union for faculty and 
staff at STCC (the “Union”) took a vote of no confidence in the Board, as well as STCC 
President John Cook and his cabinet.  The Board held a posted open meeting on February 5, 
2026.  During that meeting, Member Tonia Butler Pérez raised a “point of order” regarding the 
vote of no confidence.5  She noted that the Board had not yet addressed the vote and expressed 
concern that “it’s acting like the union does not matter to what they’re saying to us.”  She asked 
what the Board was going to do about the vote: “Are we just going to keep ignoring it?”  Chair 
Knapik responded that the Board was not going to discuss that issue at the February 5 meeting, 
but that he would have some remarks at the conclusion of the meeting.  He noted that he would 
be “happy to take this conversation offline.”  Member Butler Pérez stated that she “could not do 
offline” anymore because it goes “in one ear and out the other.”  After more back and forth, 
Chair Knapik again offered to “speak with you offline.”  He explained that “as a public body I 
think I worry a little bit about the conversations that we sometimes engage in.”  He noted that the 
vote of no confidence was not listed as a topic on the meeting notice and suggested that the 
matter should not be discussed in the absence of President Cook.

expect any further action from the Board relative to the no confidence vote. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  Except when convened in executive session, “all 
meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a).  A “meeting” is

defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the 
body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The Open Meeting Law defines “deliberation” broadly 
as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including electronic mail, between or 
among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction; provided, 
however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of a meeting agenda, scheduling 
information or distribution of other procedural meeting [information] or the distribution of 
reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided that no opinion of a member 
is expressed.”  Id.  A one-way communication from one public body member to a quorum on 
matters within the body’s jurisdiction constitutes deliberation for purposes of the Open Meeting 
Law, even if no other public body member responds.  See OML 2026-21; OML 2021-178; OML 
2012-73.7  Moreover, the Open Meeting Law does not carve out an exception to the definition of 
“deliberation” for discussions that do not result in a decision or vote.  See OML 2021-178; OML 
2020-93.  
 
A public body may not engage in serial communication whereby a quorum communicates 
in a non-contemporaneous manner outside of a meeting on a particular subject matter within the 
public body’s jurisdiction.  See McCrea v. Flaherty, 71 Mass. App. Ct. 637 (2008) (finding that 
private serial communications violate the spirit of the Open Meeting Law and may not be used to 
circumvent the intent of the law); see also OML 2024-223; OML 2020-160; OML 2012-84.
4 
Relaying the same information through separate telephone calls or separate text messages to 
individual members of a public body does not insulate communication from the requirements of 
the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2026-76 (finding that city councilor’s communications with 
quorum of fellow councilors via individual telephone calls, text messages and an in-person 
conversation constituted deliberation in violation of Open Meeting Law); OML 2024-157 
(finding separate, serial communication of overlay district map violated Law when it reached a 
quorum); OML 2023-224 (finding planning board violated Law when member telephoned each 
of his fellow members separately to gauge interest in amendment of proposal to be discussed at 
next meeting); OML 2020-160 (finding board violated Law when chair communicated to a 
quorum of the board by separate telephone calls). 
 
Prior to the Board’s February 19 meeting, Chair Knapik spoke with each of the Board 
members outside a meeting regarding the Union’s no confidence vote.  He did so after declining 
Board Member Butler Pérez’s suggestion on February 5 to discuss the topic during the meeting.  
When he refused to take up the topic at the February 5 meeting, Chair Knapik cited his concern 
that the Union’s no confidence vote was not listed on the notice of the meeting, and that STCC 
President Cook was not in attendance.  More telling, however, was his statement that he knows 
“where to place certain matters that sometimes come before this very public board that can 
sometimes make our business uncomfortable.”  He then pledged to speak with each member 
“offline,” a promise that he kept.  Keeping the discussion off the agenda for a public meeting 
would enable the Board to remain “laser focused on the presidential search,” according to Chair 
Knapik. 
 
To remain in compliance with the Open Meeting Law, a public body may need to have 
uncomfortable discussions during an open meeting.  A discussion may not be taken “offline”

simply because it may be uncomfortable, may draw unwanted attention to the public body, or 
may detract from other pressing business before the body.  The purpose of the Open Meeting 
Law is to ensure transparency in the deliberations on which public policy is based.  See OML 
2013-160.  That Chair Knapik did not elicit input from members or engage in any decision-
making process with them by telephone did not insulate the Board from the requirement that all 
deliberation occur in a posted meeting.  By telling each Board member outside a meeting of his 
intended response to the no confidence vote, Chair Knapik violated the Open Meeting Law.   
 
The Board is cautioned that the Open Meeting Law’s definition of “deliberation” is very 
broad, and therefore public body members should use telephone calls, emails or text messaging 
to communicate among a quorum only for the limited purposes that are excluded from the Law’s 
definition of “deliberation,” such as distribution of a meeting agenda, scheduling information, or 
distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided that no opinion 
of a member is expressed.  See OML 2026-76; OML 2021-61.  We acknowledge the Board’s 
vote at its March 30 meeting to seek annual Open Meeting Law training. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board, and specifically, Chair Knapik, 
violated the Open Meeting Law when the Chair discussed the no confidence vote with members 
outside of a meeting.  We order immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, 
and we caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the 
law. 
 
We also order the release within forty-five (45) days of the date of this letter of any 
written communication, including emails and text messages, exchanged by Board members 
regarding the Union’s December 5, 2025, no confidence vote, or President Cook’s response 
thereto.  If members communicated verbally about these subjects outside a meeting, we order 
that those communications be summarized and included in the minutes of the February 19 
meeting, said amended minutes to be approved by the Board within forty-five (45) days of the 
date of this letter.  We also order that the Board certify to our office that it has released the 
communications as ordered.  See 940 C.M.R. 29.07(4).
