This office received a complaint from Maryjane Churchville on December 5, 2025, 
alleging that the Townsend Selectboard (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on October 24, 2025, and 
former Town Administrator Nelson Mui responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by 
letter dated November 13, 2025.  The complaint alleges that the notice of the Board meeting held 
on October 21, 2025, was insufficiently specific. 
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter.  Following our 
review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in the way alleged.  In 
reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s response to the 
complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we 
reviewed the notice and minutes of the Board meeting held on October 21, 2025, as well as a 
video recording of that meeting.1  
  
FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On October 17, 2025, at 11:38 A.M., the Board posted 
notice of a meeting to be held on October 21, 2025, at 5:00 P.M.  The notice listed seven main

public records portal to manage the large number of requests the town had received.  The Board 
raised concerns about potential liability to the town for publicly displaying the requests.  For 
approximately 40 minutes, the Board discussed charging for records, whether information on the 
portal should be public, and the creation of a policy to establish what information on the portal 
should remain private.  Ultimately, Ms. Kell made a motion, which was seconded by Ms. 
Goodrich, that information in the public records portal remain private until the Board created a 
policy.  The motion failed by a majority vote.

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies must post notice of each meeting “at 
least 48 hours prior to such meeting” and that every notice include “the date, time and place of 
such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the 
meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The list of topics shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably 
advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We 
generally consider a topic to include sufficient specificity when a reasonable member of the 
public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  
See OML 2015-35; OML 2012-71; OML 2011-44.   
 
The complaint alleges that the Board posted an insufficient notice for the meeting held on 
October 21, 2025, where the notice included broad topics without including additional item 
descriptions that further advised the public of the topics to be discussed.  First, the complaint 
alleges that the Board did not include on the notice the specific businesses seeking renewal of 
entertainment or Class I, II and III licenses under the topics “Approve and sign out of session all 
2026 renewals for Entertainment Licenses contingent upon receipt of required documentation 
and payment” and “Approve and sign out of session all 2026 renewals for Class I, II, III licenses 
contingent upon receipt of required documentation and payment.”  Here, the Board did not 
actually discuss any entertainment or auto sales licenses, nor did it approve or deny any licenses.  
Rather, it simply moved to sign license renewals outside of a meeting.  When reviewing a 
meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a 
meeting notice standing alone.  See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what 
was actually discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to make 
the public aware of the discussion topics.  See OML 2015-101; OML 2015-21.  Because the 
Board did not actually discuss any specific businesses that were seeking renewal of licenses, we 
find that the notice was sufficiently specific and reflected the actual discussions held during the 
meeting.  See OML 2018-22.  As such, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law by not listing on the notice the businesses seeking renewal of licenses.  See OML 2023-104; 
2017-164.  Although we find no violation with respect to these topics, we remind the Board that, 
to be sufficiently specific, a meeting notice must identify the specific applicants or license

Second, the complaint alleges that the Board did not identify the departments it planned 
to restructure under the topic “Restructuring of Departments” or the policies it was working on 
under the topic “Updates of policies being worked on.”  We find that the Board did not discuss 
the restructuring of any specific departments, although the Board mentioned the Department of 
Public Works as an example of a department that could be restructured.  Rather, the Board heard 
from town legal counsel about the procedure for restructuring town departments and that any 
restructuring would need to comply with the town charter and state laws, and then heard from 
labor counsel about how restructuring might affect union contracts, the budget and job 
descriptions.  Moreover, the Board did not discuss any specific policies being worked on but 
rather received an update from the Town Administrator that several policies were still under 
review by town legal counsel.  The Board then expressed the need to begin reviewing the 
Personnel Policies and Procedures at its next meeting in November.  We have previously 
explained, for example, that when a public body anticipates reviewing all town meeting warrant 
articles or an entire budget, it is not required to list all the warrant articles or all budgetary items 
on its meeting notice.  See OML 2026-30; OML 2017-185; OML 2012-83.  However, when a 
public body anticipates discussing particular Town Meeting warrant articles or budgetary items, 
it must specifically list them in its meeting notice.  See OML 2024-27; OML 2013-8; OML 
2011-7.  Similarly, here, where the Board did not discuss any particular policies or the 
restructuring of any particular town departments but rather all policies under review and the 
general restructuring of town departments, we find that the notice was sufficiently specific with 
respect to these two topics.   
 
Third, the complaint alleges that the Board “strayed far beyond” the topic noticed as 
“Presentation of Public Records Portal by Town Clerk.”  During this part of the meeting, the 
Board heard from the Town Clerk about the creation of a new online portal to track public 
records requests submitted to the Town and discussed protecting the privacy of individuals who 
submitted the requests.  The Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to list all 
tangential topics related to a discussion nor does the law require a public body to anticipate the 
course of deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only that it identify the discussion topic 
with sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2015-73; OML 2014-127; OML 
2014-58; OML 2014-38.  We are satisfied that the topic as listed on the notice for the public 
records portal was sufficient to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting and further that the discussions about whether the portal should be public and the 
creation of a policy naturally flowed from the Board’s consideration of the posted agenda topic.  
See OML 2023-114; OML 2020-99.

Finally, with respect to the topic “Reports from Board liaisons” that was listed on the 
meeting notice, we find that during this part of the meeting Board members generally reported on 
town activities and events attended, announced grants received by the town, and mentioned an 
issue with a town property.  Such announcements are generally spontaneous and not pieces of 
business that Board members anticipate discussing or are otherwise within the Board’s 
jurisdiction.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b) (notices must contain “a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”).  As such, we find that these types of 
announcements do not need to be listed on the meeting notice.  See OML 2017-33.   
 
CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Board or with 
our office.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
