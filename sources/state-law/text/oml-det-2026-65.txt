the Hanover Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about October 14, 2025, and you 
responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Board, by email dated October 28, 2025.
1  The 
complaint alleges that the Board posted a notice with insufficient specificity for a meeting held 
on October 6, 2025, where the notice included an executive session to discuss collective 
bargaining agreements without identifying the bargaining unit(s) that would be discussed.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  On October 1, 2025, at 12:05 P.M., the Board posted notice 
of a meeting to be held on October 6, 2025, at 6:30 P.M.  The notice listed 15 open session topics 
and one executive session topic to “discuss strategy with respect to contract negotiations with 
nonunion personnel, namely Police Lieutenant and Deputy Fire Chief, and Collective Bargaining 
Agreements (CBA)” under Purpose 2. 
 
The October 6, 2025, meeting was held as planned.  During the meeting, the Board first 
discussed the open session topics listed on the notice.  After discussing the open session topics, 
then Chair Rhonda Nyman
3 announced that the Board would convene in executive session 
pursuant to Purpose 2 “to discuss strategy with respect to contract negotiations with nonunion 
personnel, namely Police Lieutenant and Deputy Fire Chief, and collective bargaining 
agreements” and that the Board would not be returning to open session.  Although Chair Nyman 
was reminded that a roll call vote was necessary to convene in executive session, she simply 
asked for “a motion for a roll call vote.”  A motion was made by Board member Steve Louko, 
which was seconded by Board Vice Chair Greg Sattewhite, and Chair Nyman then asked, “All in 
favor?”  Board members responded by saying “Aye.”
4 
 
During the executive session, the Board conducted contract negotiations with a Police 
Lieutenant and Deputy Fire Chief.  The Board then discussed negotiation strategy with respect to 
four collective bargaining units – Police, Fire AFSCME and HMEL.

took place, it is inappropriate to include information in meeting minutes that was not discussed or did not occur at a 
meeting.  See OML 2023-39; OML 2021-128.  We recommend the Board revise the open session minutes to reflect 
the vote to convene in executive session as it actually occurred during the October  6, 2025, meeting.

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a 
public body be conducted in an open session, with some exceptions.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 
21(a).  Public bodies may enter a closed, executive session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session; the 
chair has announced the purpose for the executive session, stating all subjects that may be 
revealed without compromising the purpose for the executive session; a majority of members of 
the body have voted to go into executive session; the vote of each member is recorded by roll 
call and entered into the minutes; and the chair has publicly announced whether the open session 
will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also 
OML 2014-94.5  Executive session topics must be described, both in the meeting notice and in 
an announcement during open session, in as much detail as possible without compromising the 
purpose for which the executive session was called.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also 
District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. School Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] 
precise statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary . . . because that 
is the only notification given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, 
and the only way the public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper.”). 
 
A public body may enter executive session “[t]o conduct strategy sessions in preparation 
for negotiations with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective bargaining sessions or contract 
negotiations with nonunion personnel.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2) (“Purpose 2”).  When convening 
in executive session pursuant to Purpose 2, a public body must identify the nonunion personnel 
or collective bargaining unit with whom it will be negotiating, if doing so will not compromise 
the lawful purpose for secrecy.  See OML 2017-53; OML 2016-43; OML 2013-151.   
 
Another permissible reason to convene in executive session is to “discuss strategy with 
respect to collective bargaining or litigation if an open meeting may have a detrimental effect on 
the bargaining or litigating position of the public body and the chair so declares.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 
21(a)(3) (“Purpose 3”).  When convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 3, a public 
body must identify the collective bargaining unit with whom it will be negotiating, if doing so 
will not compromise the lawful purpose for secrecy.  See OML 2019-37; 2018-139; OML 2016-
43; OML 2015-87. 
 
The complaint alleges that the executive session topic listed on the October 6, 2025, 
meeting notice lacked sufficient specificity where it did not identify the collective bargaining 
units with whom the Board would be negotiating in executive session.  The Board maintains that 
“the agenda provided notice the executive session was to discuss strategy relevant to all four (4) 
CBAs so no further qualifier was required.”  We disagree.  Here, the notice simply indicated that 
the Board would discuss “Collective Bargaining Agreements (CBA)” without indicating the 
specific collective bargaining unit or that it would discuss strategy with respect to all unions.

See OML 2019-114 (meeting notice should list the specific unions to be discussed or specify that 
all unions would be discussed); see also OML 2025-199 (finding notice was sufficiently specific 
where it specified that all unions and nonunion personnel would be discussed in executive 
session); OML 2020-137 and OML 2020-129 (finding notice sufficiently specific where it 
specified that Board would discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining with “all 
bargaining units”).  As such, we find that the notice of the October 6, 2025, meeting was 
insufficiently specific and the Board violated the Open Meeting Law. 
 
We must determine whether this violation was an intentional one.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public body or a member thereof, in 
knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  An intentional violation may 
be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of the law’s requirement or has 
previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  On May 13, 
2021, the Attorney General found that the Board violated the Open Law by posting notice that 
failed to sufficiently identify the specific bargaining units to be discussed in executive session.  
See OML 2021-69.  Moreover, we specifically advised the Board that similar future violations 
could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  Because the Board had previously 
been advised by the Attorney General that posting a meeting notice without identifying the 
specific bargaining units to be discussed in executive session violates the Open Meeting Law, we 
find that this violation was intentional.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 18; 940 CMR 29.02; OML 2021-135; 
OML 2016-69.   
 
Finally, we find that the Board should have cited to Purpose 3, not Purpose 2, when it 
convened in executive session to discuss the collective bargaining agreements.  In past 
determinations, we have distinguished situations where a public body enters executive session to 
conduct actual collective bargaining negotiations under Purpose 2 with ones in which it enters 
executive session to discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining negotiations under 
Purpose 3.  See OML 2025-1; OML 2022-21; OML 2018-19.  Here, the Board discussed strategy 
with respect to collective bargaining while in executive session on October 6, 2025.  Although 
the Board cited the wrong purpose for entering into executive session, it was legally entitled to 
discuss strategy with respect to collective bargaining negotiations behind closed doors.  See, e.g., 
OML 2024-127 and OML 2023-39 (finding notice sufficient even though it described the 
executive session discussions as falling under Purpose 3 instead of Purpose 1 where the notice 
specified that the discussions pertained to an Open Meeting Law complaint).  However, when 
convening in executive session pursuant to Purpose 3, the Chair is required to declare that an 
open meeting would have a detrimental effect on the bargaining position of the public body.  See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(3).  This requirement is meant to engender confidence that the secrecy 
inherent in executive session is truly necessary.  See OML 2011-30.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
posting an insufficiently specific notice for the October 6, 2025, meeting where the notice failed 
to identify the collective bargaining units that were the subject of the executive session.  We 
order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution 
that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
