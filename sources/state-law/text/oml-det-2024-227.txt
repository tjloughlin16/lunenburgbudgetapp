Dear Attorney Austin: 
 
This office received a complaint from George Harris on June 25, 2024,
1 alleging that the 
Wayland Zoning Board of Appeals (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about April 29, and, after an 
extension of time granted by our office, you responded on behalf of the Board by letter dated 
May 23. The complaint alleges that Board failed to timely create and approve minutes for its 
meetings held on January 16 and February 13. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to timely approve meeting minutes. Furthermore, we find the violation to be intentional. In 
reaching this determination, we reviewed the Open Meeting Law complaint, the Board’s 
response, the request for further review, and a December 28, 2023, email that the individual 
contracted to create the Board’s meeting minutes sent to the Board’s assistant.  
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions . . . .” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). The Law 
further requires that minutes “of all open sessions shall be created and approved in a timely 
manner.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). The Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations define 
“timely manner” as being “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, 
whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.” 940 CMR

29.11(2); see OML 2018-48.2 Whenever possible, we encourage “public bodies to approve 
minutes at the next meeting.” 940 CMR 29.11(2). 
 
Ther
e is no dispute that the Board failed to approve the minutes of its January 16 and 
February 13 meetings within the timeframe set forth in 940 CMR 29.11(2). The parties agree that 
the Board held meetings on the following dates in 2024: January 16, February 13, February 20, 
March 12, April 9, and May 21. The parties further agree that during the Board’s May 21 
meeting it approve the minutes of its January 16 and February 13 meetings. 
 
 The issue here is whether the Board had good cause for the delay in approving the 
January 16 and February 13 meeting minutes. We find that it did not. The Board asserts that we 
should find good cause for the delay for the following reasons:  
 
[T]he Board utilizes a third -party contractor to record its meetings and prepare 
meeting minutes for its approval. At the end of December, 2023, the Board was 
informed that a death in the family of the contractor had occurred and that this had 
caused a temporary backlog in the contractor’s preparation of Board minutes. The 
Board was unable, due to these unforeseen circumstances, to move forward with 
approving the meeting minutes in question within the proper timeframe.  
 
We find the explanation unpersuasive. On December 28, 2023, the contractor emailed the 
a
ssistant for the Board, explaining that a death in her family a couple of months prior had caused 
a backlog of meeting minutes. However, the contractor also explained that “everything is caught 
up now,” and she attached to the email all outstanding meeting minutes as of that date. 
Moreover, our office has consistently explained that the responsibility for complying with the 
Open Meeting Law rests with public bodies and members of such bodies, not with individual 
public employees or contractors, and that if staff resources are not available to create minutes for 
the public body to approve, then the public body members must take on this responsibility to 
ensure compliance with the Law. See OML 2017-115; OML 2018-105; OML 2021-134; OML 
2022-224. Indeed, in 2019, after finding that the Board had violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely create and approve meeting minutes, we warned the Board that if staffing 
resources were not available to create meeting minutes, then its members must create the minutes 
to ensure compliance with the Open Meeting Law. OML 2019-21.  
 
We f
ind here that the Board’s failure to timely create and approve the meeting minutes 
did not stem from a “particularized, abrupt, and unforeseen circumstance that directly affected its 
ability to timely approve the minutes in question,” as the Board asserts, but instead stemmed 
from the Board’s failure to sufficiently oversee and manage the creation of its meeting minutes. 
Therefore, we find that the Board has not shown good cause for delay in approving the minutes 
of its January 16 and February 13 meetings and that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
by failing to timely approve the same. We understand and appreciate the demands on public 
bodies, many of which consist of volunteer members; however, public bodies and their members 
are required to comply with the requirements of the Open Meeting Law, including the 
requirement to timely create and approve accurate meeting minutes.

Next, we consider whether the violation found here was, as the Com plainant urges, 
intentional. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c). An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public 
body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].” 940 CMR 29.02. 
An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of 
the Law’s requirements or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open 
Meeting Law. Id. Evidence of an intentional violation includes that a public body was previously 
advised by the Attorney General that the conduct violates the Open Meeting Law. See OML 
2019-114 (violation intentional where Board was warned that it must include identity of parties 
about whom it is conducting strategy discussions); OML 2017-109 (repeated failure to respond 
to complaint within 14 days constitutes intentional violation); OML 2017-23 (repeated 
discussion of personnel matters in executive session constitutes intentional violation). 
 
In 2019 we
 found that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to approve 
meeting minutes within the later of the Board’s next three meetings or 30 days. OML 2019-21. 
In OML 2019-21 we specifically explained to the Board that a staffing shortage and the 
frequency and complexity of its meetings did not constitute good cause for delay in the approval 
of minutes. Additionally, we made clear that “[i]f staff resources are not available to create 
minutes for the Board to approve, then Board members must take on this responsibility to ensure 
compliance with the law.” OML 2019-21. Finally, we warned the Board “that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.” OML 2019-21. In OML 
2019-21 we also noted a 2017 determination from our office, OML 2017-71, finding a prior 
violation for failure to timely approve meeting minutes. 
 
In 2022 we again found that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
timely approve meeting minutes. OML 2022-120. In OML 2022-120 we declined to find an 
intentional violation based on the circumstances at that time, even though we had by then twice 
warned the Board of its obligation to timely approve meeting minutes. However, in OML 2022-
120 we explained “that this is at least the third time we have warned the Board regarding timely 
approval of meeting minutes.” Furthermore, we cautioned the Board “that it should take more 
care to approve minutes in a timely manner and that a determination by our office of a similar 
violation in the future may be considered evidence of intent to violate the Open Meeting Law 
and could result in the imposition of a civil penalty.” In light of the Board’s disregard for our 
previous warnings and guidance regarding its obligation to ensure the timely creation and 
approval of its meeting minutes, we find this violation intentional. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to timely approve the minutes of its January 16 and February 13 meetings. Furthermore, 
we find this violation to be intentional. We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance 
with the Open Meeting Law. Additionally, we refer this matter for a hearing pursuant to 940 
CMR 29.07(3) and recommend that the Board be assessed a fine of $200.00 pursuant to G.L. 
c. 30A, § 23(c) (“Upon the finding of a violation, the attorney general may issue an order to . . . 
impose a civil penalty upon the public body of not more than $1,000 for each intentional 
violation”). We invite the Board to contact this office to discuss the hearing process or a 
resolution of this matter.
