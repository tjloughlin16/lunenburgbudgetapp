This office received a complaint from John McDonnell on June 18, 2025, and two 
complaints on July 10, 2025, alleging that the Andover School Committee (the “Committee”) 
violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaints were originally filed 
with the Committee between April 14, 2025, and June 3, 2025, and you responded to all three 
complaints, on behalf of the Committee, by separate letters dated May 2, May 16, and June 13, 
2025.  The complaints allege that the Committee failed to follow its established practice for 
public comment, failed to post sufficiently specific meeting notices, failed to create sufficiently 
detailed meeting minutes, failed to timely approve meeting minutes, deliberated outside of a 
properly posted meeting, and created a subcommittee that failed to post notice or create minutes.  
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed these matters.  Following 
our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by posting a notice of 
the March 20, 2025, meeting with insufficient specificity and failing to approve the minutes of 
the March 12 and March 20, 2025, meetings in a timely manner.  We find that the Committee did 
not violate the Open Meeting Law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the original complaints, the Committee’s responses to the complaints, and the 
complaints filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, we reviewed the notices

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Town of Andover has one preschool, five elementary 
schools that serve students from kindergarten through fifth grade, three middle schools that serve 
students from sixth to eighth grade, and a high school.  In 2018, Andover Public Schools 
submitted an application to the Massachusetts School Building Authority seeking funding to 
build a new West Elementary School.  As part of the application, Andover Public Schools noted 
that if a new West Elementary School was built, the district would redistrict the other four 
elementary schools so that each school’s enrollment was most effectively aligned with its 
capacity. 
 
In July 2024, the Town issued a request for proposals seeking a consultant to lead a 
comprehensive kindergarten through grade eight redistricting and rezoning process, which would 
be based on school capacity, student enrollment, and student demographics, and involve a high 
level of community engagement.  In October 2024, the Town hired Dillinger Research and 
Applied Data (Dillinger RAD) to analyze data and propose various options for realigning 
elementary and middle school district lines.   
 
During a November 21, 2024, workshop meeting, the Committee received an update 
from Nick Stellitano of Dillinger RAD, who advised that two community meetings had been 
held and surveys sent out.  Mr. Stellitano explained that redistricting scenarios would be 
developed based on collected data and feedback from the community.  
 
The Committee posted notices of meetings to be held on January 9, February 6, and 
February 27, 2025.  The January 9, 2025, meeting notice included the topic “Continuing 
Business: Presentation by Dillinger RAD on Redistricting Update”; the February 6, 2025, 
meeting notice included the topic “Continuing Business: Redistricting Update”; and the February 
27, 2025, meeting notice include the topic “SC Workshop: Virtual Presentation on Redistricting 
– Nick Stellitano – Dillinger RAD.”   
 
Each of these three meetings was held as planned.  During each meeting, Nick Stellitano 
of Dillinger RAD presented the Committee with redistricting scenarios for the five elementary 
schools and three middle schools.  The Committee discussed the advantages and disadvantages 
of the proposed redistricting scenarios during each of the three meetings but did not vote on any 
of the proposed scenarios.  After each presentation, Dillinger RAD accepted public comment for 
a period of seven to 10 days on the redistricting scenarios that had been proposed during 
Committee meetings.  Each of the subsequently proposed redistricting scenarios were compiled 
based on feedback received from school staff and the community and were intended to balance 
social-emotional learning, school building capacity, and safe walking routes.  By the conclusion

DISCUSSION 
 
I. One of the Allegations Raised in the Complaints Does Not Constitute a 
Violation of the Open Meeting Law. 
  
The complaints allege that the Committee failed to follow its “established practice” of 
eliciting public comment prior to discussing a redistricting scenario during the meeting held on 
March 20, 2025.  Even assuming the allegation raised in the complaints was true, it would not 
constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law.  The Open Meeting Law does not require that a 
public body allow public participation, but rather gives the chair of the public body discretion to 
decide whether to permit public comment during a meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A § 20(g) (“[n]o 
person shall address a meeting of a public body without permission of the chair, and all persons 
shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.”); see also OML 2020-77; OML 2017-189.
3  If public 
participation will be permitted, the chair may determine the method for receiving public 
comment, who may speak or otherwise participate during a meeting, and may set limitations on 
participation.
4  See OML 2020-114; OML 2017-189.  Even though it was the Committee’s usual 
practice to allow for public participation either prior to or during meetings, it was not required to 
continue to do so.  Moreover, it is not a violation of the Open Meeting Law for a public body to 
vote on a topic without first taking comments from the public.  See OML 2019-19; OML 2019-2.  
We do, however, encourage public bodies to allow for as much public participation as time and 
circumstances permit, and note that the Committee allowed for public comment during the 
March 20, 2025, meeting.  See OML 2019-120; OML 2015-12; OML 2014-129; OML 2012-59.   
 
II. We Find that the Notice of the March 20, 2025, Meeting Contained an 
Insufficiently Specific Topic. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  
The notice must be printed in a legible, easily understandable format and must contain the date, 
time and place of the meeting, as well as a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates 
will be discussed.  Id.  The list of topics shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally 
consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of the public could read 
the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-
35.    
 
The complaint alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law because it 
voted to adopt a specific redistricting scenario but the notice of the March 20, 2025, meeting 
indicated that the Committee planned to merely continue discussing redistricting.  Here, the 
notice of the March 20, 2025, meeting included the topic “Continuing Business: Redistricting

Presentation; Public Input re-Redistricting Pres; Committee Redistricting Discussion.”  During 
the meeting, the Committee reviewed previous redistricting scenarios (E6, M2, M3 and M4) and 
one new scenario (M5) presented by Dillinger RAD.  Ultimately the Committee voted by 
majority vote “to adopt the redistricting proposal titled Scenario E6/M5 for implementation at

The Open Meeting Law does not require a public body to anticipate the course of 
deliberation or the outcome of a discussion, only that it identify the discussion topic with 
sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  
See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b); OML 2014-127; OML 2014-58; OML 2014-38.  
We have previously said that it is reasonably foreseeable that a public body may take action and 
hold a vote following discussion of a topic listed on a meeting notice, even where the notice 
topic does not specifically indicate the possibility of a vote.  See OML 2015-66; OML 2012-75.  
However, we have also explained that there may be a context in which a public body has 
repeatedly discussed a posted topic over the course of several meetings without ever taking 
action on the topic.  In such circumstances, the public may not reasonably foresee a vote by the 
public body on the posted topic at any given meeting without additional detail.  In that context, it 
may be necessary for a public body to specifically indicate on its notice that it plans to take 
action and vote on a particular topic.  See OML 2017-186. 
 
Here, the Committee had been discussing redistricting scenarios since January 2025.  For 
five of twelve meetings, the Committee posted notices that included a topic related to 
redistricting discussions.  Prior to the March 20, 2025, meeting, the Committee had not made any 
decisions or taken any votes regarding the seven proposed elementary school redistricting 
scenarios (E1 through E7) and the five middle school redistricting scenarios (M1 through M5).  
Then, during the March 20, 2025, meeting, Committee Member DiCesaro moved to “adopt the 
redistricting proposal titled Scenario E6/M5” and the Committee agreed by a majority vote.  
Moreover, we find that the Committee knew during its March 12, 2025, workshop meeting that it 
was expected to vote for a redistricting scenario during its next meeting on March 20, 2025.  As 
such, where the Committee anticipated voting for a redistricting scenario during the March 20, 
2025, meeting, it should have indicated on its notice that it planned to vote on this matter since 
the Committee had discussed this topic over numerous meetings without ever taking action.  See 
OML 2019-111; OML 2017-195; OML 2017-186.  We find therefore that the Committee 
violated the Open Meeting Law by posting an insufficiently specific notice for its March 20, 
2025, meeting.  
 
III. We Find that the Committee Created Sufficiently Detailed Minutes but 
Violated the Open Meeting Law by Failing to Approve Minutes in a 
Timely Manner.  
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made, and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  See OML 2016-105; OML 2013-64.  By substantial compliance, we mean that the 
===PAGE 6===
6 
 
minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did not 
attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred.  See 
OML 2012-106.  While minutes must include a summary of the discussion on each topic, a 
transcript is not required, and the minutes do not need to include every remark or opinion 
presented.  See OML 2012-29; OML 2011-55. 
 
The Open Meeting Law further requires that open session minutes be created and 
approved in a “timely manner.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  The Attorney General’s Open Meeting 
Law Regulations define “timely manner” as “within the next three public body meetings or 
within 30 days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further 
delay.”  940 CMR 29.11(2).  Whenever possible, we recommend that the minutes be approved at 
the next meeting.  940 CMR 29.11(2); see also OML 2019-20.  In addition, the Open Meeting 
Law requires that “the minutes of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft 
form, shall be made available upon request by any person within 10 days.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). 
 
The complaints allege that the Board failed to create sufficiently detailed minutes and 
failed to timely “issue” minutes of meetings.  First, we note that the Open Meeting Law does not 
require that public bodies “issue” or post minutes to a website or that minutes be submitted to the 
Town Clerk.  Rather, public bodies must create and approve open session minutes within a 
timely manner and make minutes available within 10 days of a request.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(c); OML 2024-149; OML 2013-103.  There is no suggestion in the complaints that the 
minutes were requested and not provided to the complainant. 
 
Second, complainants must allege violations with a degree of specificity, as our office 
will not conduct broad audits of public bodies based on generalized allegations.  See OML 2023-
135; OML Declination 7-19-23 (Fairhaven Historical Commission).  Here, the complaints 
suggest that minutes of meetings held between October 2024, and March 2025 do not contain 
sufficient detail and were not approved in a timely manner.  We decline to review six months’ 
worth of meetings, during which time the Committee held 10 meetings and nine workshops, to 
determine whether the minutes of each of these meetings are sufficiently detailed or were timely 
approved.   
 
Nonetheless, we have reviewed the minutes of the Committee meetings held on March 12 
and March 20, 2025, and find that these minutes contain sufficient detail.  Both sets of minutes 
contain the date, time and place of the meetings, as well as the members who were present.  In 
addition, after watching video recordings of these two meetings, we find that the minutes contain 
an accurate summary of the discussions that occurred during each of the meetings.  Although the 
complaints allege that the minutes fail to contain the “basis” for the Committee’s decisions to 
accept or reject a particular redistricting scenario, we note that the Committee did not 
specifically discuss in either the March 12 or March 20, 2025, meetings the basis for its 
decisions.  To be “accurate,” meeting minutes must accurately reflect the discussions that took 
place during a meeting, including a record of all votes.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); OML 2020-62.  
Because the minutes must accurately capture the discussions that actually took place, it is 
inappropriate to include information in meeting minutes that was not discussed or did not occur 
at a meeting.  See OML 2021-128.

Although we find that the Committee created sufficiently detailed minutes of the March 
12 and March 20, 2025, meetings, we find that the Committee failed to timely approve these two 
sets of meetings minutes.  Here, the Committee did not approve the minutes of its March 12, 
2025, meeting until June 12, 2025, and did not approve the minutes of its March 20, 2025, 
meeting until May 5, 2025, which was not within the later of the next three meetings or 30 days 
following each meeting.  See OML 2022-16; OML 2021-155.  As such, we find that the 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to approve these minutes in a timely 
manner.  
 
IV. We Find Insufficient Evidence that a Quorum of the Committee 
Deliberated Outside of a Properly Posted Meeting.  
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an 
emergency, “a public body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such 
meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  A “meeting” 
is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within 
the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The law defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written 
communication through any medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of 
a public body on any public business within its jurisdiction.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open 
Meeting Law, a “quorum” is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id. 
 
The complaints allege that the Committee must have discussed certain redistricting 
scenarios and delegated certain decision-making authority to Dillinger RAD outside of a meeting 
since the meeting minutes do not reflect any discussions or delegations.5  We have explained on 
many occasions that mere speculation or conjecture regarding deliberation outside of a posted 
meeting, without factual support, is insufficient to support a complaint’s allegations.  See, e.g., 
OML 2025-15; OML 2023-242; OML 2012-59.  Here, the complainant does not support his 
allegation with anything other than his impression that because meeting minutes do not reflect 
the Committee’s acceptance or rejection of redistricting scenarios, the Committee must have 
discussed the scenarios outside of a properly posted meeting.  A suggestion of deliberation 
outside a meeting, without factual support, does not establish a violation of the Open Meeting 
Law.  See OML 2025-160.  Moreover, the Committee denies that it discussed redistricting 
scenarios outside of a properly posted meeting.  Absent specific evidence to the contrary, we 
credit a public body’s account of the facts.  See OML 2025-15; 2023-121; OML 2016-141; see 
also LaPointe v. License Bd. of Worcester, 389 Mass. 454 (1983) (“There is every presumption 
in favor of the honesty and sufficiency of the motives actuating public officers in actions 
 
5 The complaints further allege that a quorum of the Committee may have attended a community forum on March 8, 
2025, without posting notice or creating minutes.  The Open Meeting Law does not prohibit a quorum of members 
of a public body merely from being in the same place at the same time; indeed, the Open Meeting Law’s definition 
of “meeting” specifically excludes “attendance by a quorum of a public body at a public or private gathering, 
including a conference or training program or a media, social or other event, so long as the members do not 
deliberate.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  Therefore, where a community forum does not include any deliberation by members 
of a public body, it does not constitute a “meeting” for purposes of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2020-19.  As 
stated above, mere speculation or conjecture regarding deliberation outside of a posted meeting, without factual 
support, is insufficient to support a complaint’s allegations.

To the extent the complaints allege that the Committee did not have the authority to 
delegate decision-making authority to Dillinger RAD, the Division of Open Government only 
enforces and interprets the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  The Open Meeting Law

V. We Find that the Redistricting Advisory Council is not a Subcommittee of 
the Committee.  Rather, it was Created by the Superintendent and 
Therefore is Not Subject to the Open Meeting Law.  
 
A “public body” subject to the Open Meeting Law is any “multiple-member board, 
commission, committee or subcommittee within the executive or legislative branch or within any 
county, district, city, region or town, however created, elected, appointed or otherwise 
constituted, established to serve a public purpose.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The Supreme Judicial 
Court recognized an exemption to the Open Meeting Law in Connelly v. School Committee of 
Hanover, 409 Mass. 232 (1991).  In Connelly, the Court held that where an individual public 
official creates a committee to advise that person on a decision that they have the sole authority 
to make, the committee is not subject to the Open Meeting Law.  Connelly, 409 Mass. at 235. 
 
The complaints allege that the Council is a subcommittee of the Committee and therefore 
subject to the Open Meeting Law.  As such, the complaint alleges, the Council violated the Law 
by failing to post notices of meetings and maintain meeting minutes.  The Committee contends 
that the Council falls within the Connelly exception where it was created by the Superintendent.   
 
Generally, to determine whether a multiple-member entity is a public body subject to the 
Open Meeting Law, there are three factors we consider: 1) the entity must be “within” 
government and not excluded from the definition of “public body”; 2) the entity must be a 
“body” empowered to act collectively; and 3) the entity must serve a “public purpose.”  See 
OML 2017-118; OML 2016-102; OML 2012-20; OML 2011-42; OML 2010-1.  Here, the 
Committee acknowledges that the Council satisfies all three factors, i.e., it is within government 
because it was created by the Superintendent; it is empowered to act collectively; and it was 
established to serve a public purpose.   
 
 However, we agree with the Committee and find that the Council falls within the 
Connelly exception where the Superintendent assembled the Council on her own, she was not 
directed to do so by the Committee, and the Committee never voted to create the Council and 
was not otherwise involved in creating the Council.  See OML 2019-149; OML 2018-62.  
Although the Superintendent appeared before the Committee on several occasions and provided 
updates from and recommendations by the Council, neither the Council nor the Superintendent 
was specifically tasked by the Committee with bringing any recommendations before the 
Committee.  See OML 2015-93; OML 2014-56.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by posting a notice for a meeting held on March 20, 2025, that included insufficient specificity 
and by failing to timely approve the minutes of the meetings held on March 12 and March 20, 
2025.  We order immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution 
that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
 
We now consider the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions
