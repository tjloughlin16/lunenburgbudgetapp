DISCUSSION
I. The Board Failed to Timely Respond to a Request for Executive Session
Meeting Minutes and Failed to Conduct a Review of those Minutes at its
Next Meeting.
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). Approval of 
executive session minutes is a separate and unrelated obligation from the requirement that 
executive session minutes be periodically reviewed to determine whether they should be released 
to the public. See OML 2019-115.6 This initial approval of executive session minutes is to 
approve the minutes as an accurate record of what occurred at a particular executive session and 
does not mean that the minutes are approved for release to the public.
A public body must respond to a request for executive session minutes within 10 days. 
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). If the public body or a designee has determined, prior to the request, 
that certain executive session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester 
directly, notifying him or her of the availability of those minutes. See OML 2013-105; OML 
2013-99. And if, at the time of a request, the public body has not conducted a review of the 
minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure is warranted, the body must perform such 
a review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no later than its next meeting or within 30 days, 
whichever occurs first. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). In such circumstances, the body must still

respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her that it is conducting this review.
See OML 2013-99.
The first complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely respond to a request for 
executive session meeting minutes. On November 17, 2018, four residents requested from the 
Board 24 sets of executive session minutes. Attorney Batt responded on December 3, 2018, 
advising that the Board would meet to review, finalize and approve the minutes as well as 
determine whether the minutes can be released. While we acknowledge that the Board initially 
responded to the request advising that it would be conducting a review of minutes, this response 
was provided in 10 business days, not 10 calendar days as the Open Meeting Law requires. See 
OML 2019-20; OML 2017-50; OML 2016-71; OML 2015-173. Because the Board failed to 
respond within 10 calendar days, we find that it violated the Open Meeting Law by not properly 
responding to a request for minutes. The Board, however, now understands its obligation to 
respond to a request for meeting minutes within 10 calendar days, and we commend the Board’s 
commitment to ensure future compliance with the law.
We also find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by not reviewing the 
requested executive session minutes at its next meeting. See OML 2016-135. Even though the 
Board’s next meeting following the November 17, 2018, request was on December 3, 2018, it 
did not conduct a review of any executive session minutes at that meeting. Rather, the Board 
reviewed seven sets of executive session minutes at its subsequent meeting on December 17, 
2018; reviewed five sets of executive session minutes during a January 7, 2019, meeting; and 
reviewed seven sets of executive session minutes during a March 11, 2019, meeting. Thereafter, 
the Board reviewed individual sets of minutes during its March 18, 2019; April 10, 2019; and 
April 22, 2019, meetings. While we commend the Board for continuing to review the requested 
minutes to determine whether nondisclosure was still warranted, the Open Meeting Law 
specifically requires the Board to perform a review of the requested executive session minutes no 
later than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first. See G.L. c. 30A, §
22(g)(2). Because the Board failed to review the minutes at its December 3, 2018, meeting, it 
violated the Open Meeting Law.
We also take this opportunity to remind the Board that public bodies have an obligation 
to review the minutes of executive sessions at reasonable intervals to determine if continued non­
disclosure of minutes is warranted, and to announce that determination at the next meeting 
following its review. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); OML 2019-35; OML 2015-94; OML 2013- 
56. Although the Open Meeting Law does not define ‘‘reasonable intervals,” we have found that 
quarterly reviews or reviews every six months satisfy the law. See OML 2017-104; OML 2015- 
166; OML 2013-180.
II. The Board Committed a Technical Violation of the Open Meeting Law 
When a Board Member Other than the Chair Announced the Purpose for
the December 17, 2018. Executive Session.
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an 
open session, with some exceptions. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). Public bodies may enter a 
closed, executive session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law. G.L.

c. 30A, § 21(a). Before entering the executive session, the chair must state the purpose for the 
executive session, stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose 
for which the executive session was called. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also District 
Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm, of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise 
statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only 
notification given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only 
way the public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”). This level of 
detail about the executive session topic must also be included in the meeting notice. See OML 
2016-72. A public body need not specifically identify the statutory purpose for the executive 
session, as long as the topic makes clear which of the ten executive session purposes the public 
body is invoking. See OML 2017-49, n.6.
The second complaint alleges that the Board failed to follow proper procedure to enter 
into executive session because a Board member other than the chair stated the purpose for the 
executive session held on December 17, 2018. The Open Meeting Law specifically provides that 
the chair must state the purposes for executive session. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3). Here,
Board Member Kaufman, rather than Chair McKean, read the purpose for the executive session, 
which was to discuss litigation involving Estabrook Road, and stated that discussions in open 
session may be detrimental to the Board’s litigating position. Because a Board member other 
than the chair made these statements, we are constrained to find a violation of the Open Meeting 
Law. See OML 2016-75. We note, however, that it would have been permissible for the chair to 
explicitly delegate the responsibility for making the required statements to another individual.
See OML 2016-124 (it is not a violation where the chair specifically asked legal counsel to make 
the statement on his behalf). Where, as here, the statements were made in open session, but by a 
Board member other than the chair, and complied with the Open Meeting Law’s spirit of 
transparency, the violation is de minimis and we do not order release of the executive session 
meeting minutes. See OML 2019-34; OML 2016-75.
The complaint also suggests that the December 17, 2018, meeting notice concerning the 
executive session topic lacked sufficient detail. Here, the notice stated that the Board would 
discuss strategy with respect to the Estabrook Road litigation and the verbal statement made 
prior to entering executive session identified this specific litigation matter as well. We find that 
the notice was sufficiently detailed where the Board identified the specific litigation matter it 
planned to discuss during the executive session. The Board need not list each and every detail 
about a topic in order to comply with the law’s requirements. See OML 2014-122.
CONCLUSION
We find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to properly respond to 
a request for executive session minutes within 10 days and by failing to have the chair make the 
statement of the executive session purpose. We order immediate and future compliance with the 
law’s requirements and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.
