RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints
Dear Attorney Carboni:
This office received three complaints from Peter Douglas on October 22, alleging that 
the Marion Zoning Board of Appeals (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.1 The complaints were originally filed with the Board on or about August 8 
and August 14, respectively, and you responded to the complaints, on behalf of the Board, in 
two letters each dated September 7. In his complaints, Mr. Douglas alleges that a Board 
member spoke with Town Counsel in a hushed tone during the Board’s July 12 meeting. Mr. 
Douglas also alleges that a quorum of the Board constitutes a “voting bloc,” which 
demonstrates that they deliberate outside of noticed meetings,2 and that, immediately prior to 
its July 19 meeting, a quorum of the Board deliberated in a manner that was inaudible to the 
public. Finally, Mr. Douglas alleges that the Board failed to “publish” certain meeting 
minutes.3
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law. 
In reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaints, the Board’s responses to 
the complaints, and the complaints filed with our office requesting further review. We also 
spoke with you by telephone on May 2, 2019.

FACTS
We find the facts as follows. During the July 12 meeting, the Board chair and the 
Board’s legal counsel discussed a matter outside of the earshot of the audience. Prior to the 
start of the July 19 meeting, the Board chair and legal counsel discussed the signing of a 
document. This discussion was not within earshot of the other Board members, who engaged 
in personal conversation before the meeting began. At the time of its response to the 
complaint on September 7, 2018, the Board’s had not yet created and approved meeting 
minutes for meetings held since February 2018.
DISCUSSION
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School 
Commission of Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive 
session is convened. See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21. The law defines a “meeting” as “a 
deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.” G.L. 
c. 30A, § 18. A “deliberation” is defined as “an oral or written communication through any 
medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any 
public business within its jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include 
the distribution of other procedural meeting or the distribution of reports or documents that 
may be discussed at a meeting, provided that no opinion of a member is expressed.” Id. For 
the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” is a simple majority of the members of 
the public body. Id.
The complainant alleges that the Board improperly deliberated on two occasions: (1) 
during the July 12 meeting, when the Board Chair and legal counsel engaged in a whispered 
conversation; and (2) just prior to the start of the July 19 meeting, when Board members 
engaged in a discussion that was inaudible to the public. First, because the Open Meeting 
Law only applies to members of a public body, any communication that may have occurred 
on July 12 between Board counsel, who is not a member of the Board, and a single Board 
member does not constitute deliberation. See G.L. c. 30A, § 18; OML 2017-69; OML 2015- 
77.4 Second, we find no evidence that the Board discussed Board business prior to the July 
19 meeting, but rather exchanged personal pleasantries while waiting for the meeting to begin, 
See OML 2018-1. Accordingly, the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law.
With respect to the allegation that the Board failed to publish or disseminate its 
minutes from meetings held in 2018, we note that the Open Meeting Law requires public 
bodies to create and approve meetings minutes in a timely manner, and to provide them 
within 10 days of a request, if they exist in either draft or final form. See G.L. c. 30A, §
22(c). “Timely manner” means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30

days, whichever is later, unless the public body can show good cause for further delay.” See 
940 CMR 29.11; OML 2018-48. Whenever possible, we recommend that minutes of a

minutes of meetings held since February 2018 had not yet been created at the time the Board 
responded to this complaint on September 7, 2018, the Board has failed to timely create and 
approve its meeting minutes; an issue the Board acknowledged in its response. See OML 
2019-53 (in which we find that the Board violated the law in this manner). We advise the 
Board to promptly approve those minutes.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law as alleged in the complaints. We now consider the complaints addressed by this 
determination to be resolved. This determination does not address any other complaints that 
may be pending with our office or the Board. Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 
963-2540 if you have any questions regarding this letter.
