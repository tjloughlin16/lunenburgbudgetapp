This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on March 18, 2026, alleging that 
the Randolph Licensing Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25.
1  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on February 23, and you responded 
on behalf of the Board by letter dated March 17.2  The complaint alleges that the Board failed to

Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way 
alleged.  In reaching this determination we reviewed the original Open Meeting Law complaint, 
the Board’s response, and the complainant’s request for review.  We also communicated with 
you and with the complainant by telephone and email.  
 
FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  On February 1, 2026, the complainant sent an email to 
Board Chair Ronald Lum, “[p]ursuant to the open meeting law” “requesting a .pdf file copy of 
all meeting minutes of the Town of Randolph Licensing Board for the calendar years 2024, 2025 
and 2026.” 
 
 The explicit language of the request notwithstanding, it was read as being made pursuant 
to the Public Records Law.  On February 3, the Town Clerk sent an email to the complainant, 
acknowledging receipt of “your public records request” and stating that a response would be

DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires public bodies to create 
and approve minutes of all open sessions in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, §22(c).  Pursuant to 
the Attorney General’s regulations, “timely manner” generally means within the next three 
public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later.  940 CMR 29.11(2). 
 
Requests for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law or the Open 
Meeting Law, depending on how the request is made.  The requirements for responding differ. 
A request made to a municipal employee, such as a Town Clerk, falls under the Public Records 
Law and requires a response within 10 business days.  See G.L. c. 66, § 10; OML 2019-163; 
OML 2019-126.3  A request for meeting minutes made directly to the public body generally falls 
under the Open Meeting Law.  Under the Open Meeting Law, the minutes of an open session, if 
they exist and whether approved or in draft form, must be made available upon request by any 
person within 10 days.  G.L. c. 30A, §22(c).  If such minutes do not yet exist at the time of a 
request, the Open Meeting Law requires that the public body respond to the request within 10 
days with an explanation that the minutes do not exist in either approved or draft form.  See 
OML 2026-69; OML 2024-153; 2020-164.  Such a response must state unequivocally whether 
the minutes exist in approved or draft form.  See OML 2018-98. 
 
 On February 1, Board Chair Lum received a request for minutes  of meetings held in 
2024, 2025, and 2026.  The request was made under the Open Meeting Law, not the Public 
Records Law, as it was sent to the Board Chair and explicitly referenced the Open Meeting Law.  
Under the Open Meeting Law, the Board was required to respond to the request by February 11, 
either providing the requested minutes or explicitly stating that the minutes did not exist in either 
approved or draft form.  Here, the requestor received a response within 10 days, on February 3.  
However, that response did not make the requested minutes available or state unequivocally that 
the minutes did not exist.  Instead, the response of February 3 misstated the timeline for 
providing a response (referencing the 10 business days allowed under the Public Records Law) 
and referenced a possible fee, which is not provided for under the Open Meeting Law.  The 
Board then provided the requested minutes after 10 calendar days, on February 13.  In its 
response, the Board acknowledged that it was late in making the minutes available but stated that 
the “violation was a result of logistical challenges in preparing and processing a high volume of 
detailed minutes within the required timeframe. . . .”  Because the Board did not respond to the

CONCLUSION 
 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board  violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to timely respond to a request for meeting minutes.  We order the Board’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and caution that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the Law.
