RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint  
 
Dear Attorney Castro:  
 
This office received a complaint from Stephen Morgan on March 21, 2025, alleging that 
the Ashland Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint originally was filed with the Board on March 3, 2025.  You responded on behalf 
of the Board by letter dated March 21, 2025.  The complaint alleges that the Board violated the 
Open Meeting Law when it (1) failed to create and maintain five sets of minutes of executive 
sessions held between January 3, 2024, and April 3, 2024 (“the 2024 Executive Sessions”);1 and 
(2) failed to release executive session minutes after the purpose for those executive sessions had 
expired. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to create and maintain minutes of the 2024 Executive Sessions until March 5, 2025.  We decline 
to review the allegation that the Board failed to release minutes of the 2024 Executive Sessions 
after they had expired.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the complaint, the Board’s 
response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  We also reviewed 
notices of the meetings of January 3, 2024, January 17, 2024, January 31, 2024, February 7, 
2024 and April 3, 2024.

DISCUSSION 
   
I. The Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it created and then reviewed 
minutes of executive sessions 11 to 14 months after they took place. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the 
members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents 
and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, 
including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The Law further states that “[m]inutes 
of all open sessions shall be created and approved in a timely manner,” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c), 
while requiring that minutes of executive sessions be “create[d] and maintain[ed]” and 
“review[ed]” “at reasonable intervals” to determine whether continued nondisclosure is 
warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a), (g)(1).  See also OML 2024-158; OML 2024-29;2 Swansea 
Board of Selectmen v. Attorney General, Civil Action No. 2173CV00906 (Bristol Sup. Ct. 
December 27, 2023).  Executive session minutes may be withheld for “as long as publication 
may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer; provided, however, that 
the executive session was held in compliance with section 21.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).  Although 
“reasonable interval” is not defined by the Law, our office has found a quarterly review or a 
review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2020-101; OML 2019-45; OML 2017-104.   
 
That executive session minutes are not required to be “approved” in a timely manner does 
not leave their creation to the whim of the public body.  It is axiomatic that if executive session 
minutes are to be reviewed at reasonable intervals, they must be in existence at the time of 
review.  Thus, the Open Meeting Law requires that executive session minutes be created with 
such promptness as to allow for review at reasonable intervals.3  Pursuant to the information 
before us, the Board did not create minutes of the 2024 Executive Sessions until around March 5, 
2025.  By that time, it had been 11 to 14 months since the subject meetings had taken place.  
That timing did not allow for review at reasonable intervals to determine whether continued 
nondisclosure was warranted.  By failing to create and maintain executive session minutes 
capable of being reviewed at reasonable intervals (intervals of three to six months, starting on the 
meeting date), the Board violated the Open Meeting Law. 
 
II. We decline to review the allegation that the Board failed to release minutes of the 
2024 Executive Sessions after the purpose had expired.

maintaining without elaboration that we will find that the purpose for those executive sessions

with the public body.  See Foudy v. Amherst-Pelham Regional Sch. Comm., 402 Mass. 179, 184

burden on the Board will be to prove that the executive session purpose remains.  See OML 
2024-219; OML 2012-38; OML 2011-19. 
 
III. We decline to find the Board’s violation intentional. 
 
Finally, we must determine whether the Board’s violation was intentional, as the 
complainant urges.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by 
a public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 
CMR 29.02.  An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate 
ignorance of the law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates 
the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  The complainant points out that our office previously has found the 
Board in violation of the Open Meeting Law, for failure to approve minutes in a timely manner.  
See, e.g., OML 2023-216.  However, neither this determination, nor any other addressed to the 
Board, put it on notice that the conduct at issue here violates the Open Meeting Law.  OML 
2023-216 concerned the approval of open session minutes in a timely manner.  As explained 
above, executive session minutes do not need to be approved in a timely manner.4  Because the 
Board has not previously been advised that the conduct at issue here violated the Open Meeting 
Law, we decline to find its violation intentional. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
waiting until March 5, 2025 to create and maintain minutes of executive sessions held between 
January 3, 2024 and April 3, 2024.  We order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with 
the Open Meeting Law’s requirements and caution that future similar violations may be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law. 
 
  
 
4 Additionally, OML 2023-216 was issued prior to the Superior Court decision issued in Swansea, supra.  Prior to 
Swansea, we advised public bodies that they must create and approve both open and executive session minutes in a 
timely manner.  We have since amended our guidance in compliance with the ruling in Swansea that the Open 
Meeting Law does not require approval of executive session minutes in a timely manner.  See OML 2024-29; 
Swansea Board of Selectmen, Civil Action No. 2173CV00906 (Bristol Sup. Ct.).
