 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Hucksam: 
 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on November 20, 2025, alleging 
that the Halifax Board of Selectmen (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on October 30, 2025, and you 
responded, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated November 19, 2025.  The complaint alleges 
that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by i) failing to post a sufficiently specific notice 
of the meeting held on October 21, 2025; ii) failing to timely approve minutes of the meeting 
held on June 17, 2025; and iii) failing to announce that the October 21, 2025, meeting was being 
recorded.  
 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing 
to identify on the October 21, 2025, meeting notice the nonunion personnel with whom the 
Board would be negotiating in executive session.  We find that the Board did not violate the law 
in the other ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, 
the Board’s response to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further 
review.  In addition, we reviewed the notice, minutes and video recording of the Board meeting 
held on October 21, 2025.

FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows.  On October 16, 2025, the Board posted notice of a meeting 
to be held on October 21, 2025, at 6:00 P.M.  The notice listed the location of the meeting as the 
Selectmen Meeting Room.  The notice included seven open session topics as well as executive 
session topics.  The executive session topics were listed as follows: “to conduct strategy session 
in preparation for negotiations with non-union personnel” under Purpose 2, “to discuss the 
discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or charges against, a public officer, employee, staff 
member or individual” under Purpose 1, and the approval of minutes of executive sessions held 
on June 17, 2025, and August 26, 2025.  On October 21, 2025, at 10:29 A.M., the Committee 
posted an amended notice changing the location of the meeting to the Great Hall. 
 
 Both the original and amended notice included the following language: “ This meeting has 
been posted in accordance with the provisions of M.G.L. Chapter 30A, section 20 (b), and 
pursuant to Chapter 30A, section 20 (f), notice is being made that this meeting is being recorded 
and will be posted on “Area 58” Community Access Channel.” 
 
The October 21, 2025, meeting was held as planned.  The Board first convened in open 
session and Chair Jonathan Selig read the language listed on the notice conveying that the 
meeting was being recorded.  Chair Selig then announced that the Board would convene in 
executive session stating the purposes as they were listed on the notice.  He further advised that 
the Board would return to open session following the conclusion of the executive session.  The 
Board then unanimously voted by roll call to convene in executive session.  The Board returned 
to open session approximately 15 minutes later and discussed the remaining topics listed on the 
notice.  
 
DISCUSSION 
 
I. One Executive Session Topic Listed on the October 21, 2025, Meeting Notice 
Was Insufficient but We Find the Notice to be Otherwise Sufficient. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires that meetings of a public body be 
noticed and open to the public, unless an executive session is convened.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-
(b), 21.  A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that 
a majority of members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each 
member is recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly 
announced whether the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.

The chair of the public body must announce in open session the purpose for the executive 
session, “stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the purpose for which 
the executive session was called.”  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b)(3); see also District Attorney 
for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of 
the reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification 
given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the 
public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  This level of detail 
about the executive session topic must also be included in the meeting notice.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); OML 2016-72.  
 
The complaint alleges that the notice of the October 21, 2025, meeting was insufficient in 
several ways.  First, the complaint alleges that one executive session topic listed on the notice - 
“to conduct strategy session in preparation for negotiations with non-union personnel” - did not 
include the specific nonunion personnel with whom the Board would be negotiating.  A public 
body may convene in executive session to “conduct strategy sessions in preparation for 
negotiations with nonunion personnel or to conduct collective bargaining sessions or contract 
negotiations with nonunion personnel.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2) (“Purpose 2”).  We have 
previously stated that a public body must identify in its meeting notice the name or position of 
the specific nonunion personnel with whom it will be negotiating, if doing so will not 
compromise the lawful purpose for secrecy.  See OML 2020-67; OML 2015-63; OML 2013-97.  
The Board does not suggest that inclusion of the nonunion personnel would have compromised 
the purpose for the executive session.  See OML 2016-90; OML 2011-11.  Therefore, we find 
that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to identify on the October 21, 2025, 
meeting notice the specific nonunion personnel with whom the Board would be negotiating in 
executive session.    
 
Second, the complaint alleges that the notice failed to provide the address of the meeting 
location.  The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies post notice of every meeting “at 
least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays” and 
include “the date, time and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably 
anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).   
 
The October 21, 2025, meeting notice indicated that the meeting was to be held at “Great 
Hall.”  The Board stated in its response that it “is widely understood by the citizens of the Town” 
that “Great Hall” refers to the Town Hall located at 499 Plymouth Street in Halifax.  We have 
stated in past determinations that a public body should provide the street address of a meeting 
location, where possible.  See OML 2017-33; OML 2016-38.  However, we have also concluded 
that where a complaint does not allege that any member of the public was actually mislead by the 
meeting notice at issue, any error that may exist is harmless.  See OML 2016-77.  Here, the 
complaint does not allege that any members of the public were confused by the notice at issue.  
Accordingly, we remind the Board of its obligations under the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 
2016-146. 
 
 Finally, the complaint alleges that the amended notice failed to include the date and time 
that the notice was originally posted.  The Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations 
require that the date and time that a meeting notice is posted be conspicuously recorded on or

with the notice.  See 940 CMR 29.03(l)(d).  In addition, notices that are revised within the 48 
hours before the meeting must conspicuously record both the date and time that the amended 
notice was posted and the date and time that the notice was originally posted.  See 940 CMR 
29.03(l)(d); OML 2020-49; OML 2017-183; OML 2013-44.   
 
Here, the Board posted notice of the October 21, 2025, meeting on October 16, 2025.  
The Board then amended the notice on the day of the meeting.  The original and amended notices 
include, on the face of the notices, the date and time that each notice was posted, and the 
amended notice notes that it was “revised.”  In addition, the municipal website, which is the 
official method of posting notice for the Town of Halifax, includes both the original and 
amended notices.  Recording the date and time enables the public to observe that public bodies 
are complying with the Open Meeting Law’s notice requirements without requiring constant 
vigilance.  See OML 2013-44.  Additionally, it provides this office with evidence of compliance 
with the Open Meeting Law’s notice requirements.  See OML 2014-9.  We find that where both 
the original and amended notices conspicuously recorded thereon the date and time of posting 
and where the original notice remained posted to the same location on the municipal website as 
the amended notice, the Board effectively conveyed to the public the date and time that each 
notice was posted and fulfilled the transparency goals of the Open Meeting Law.  See, e.g., OML 
2024-6; OML 2019-102; OML 2018-53 (explaining that the date and time of posting may be 
listed either on the meeting notice itself or, when a meeting notice is posted to a website pursuant 
to 940 CMR 29.03(2)(b), on the website next to the link to the meeting notice).  Therefore, we do 
not find a violation of the Open Meeting Law in this regard.  We note, however, that best 
practice is for an amended notice to include both the date and time that the amended notice was 
posted and the date and time that the notice was originally posted.   
 
II. We F
ind that the Board Did Not Fail to Timely Approve Minutes of the Meeting 
Held on June 17, 2025. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely approve the minutes of the meeting 
held on June 17, 2025.  The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain 
accurate minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and 
place, the members present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of 
documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at 
each meeting, including the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  The Law further states 
that “[m]inutes of all open sessions shall be created and approved in a timely manner,” while 
requiring that minutes of executive sessions be “review[ed]” “at reasonable intervals” to 
determine whether continued nondisclosure is warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c); G.L. c. 30A, § 
22(g)(1).   
 
In other words, the Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body approve 
executive session minutes in a timely manner, rather it requires that they be “create[d] and 
maintain[ed]” and “review[ed]” “at reasonable intervals” to determine whether continued 
nondisclosure is warranted.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a), (g)(1).  See also OML 2024-158; OML 2024-
29; Swansea Board of Selectmen v. Attorney General, Civil Action No. 2173CV00906 (Bristol 
Sup. Ct. December 27, 2023).  Executive session minutes may be withheld for as long as 
publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer.  G.L. c. 30A, 
===PAGE 5===
5 
 
§ 22(f). Although “reasonable intervals” is not defined by the Law, our office has consistently 
found a quarterly review or a review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2023-233; 
OML 2020-101; OML 2019-45; OML 2017-104; OML 2015-166.  Here, the Board met in 
executive session on June 17, 2025, and reviewed the minutes of that executive session during a 
meeting held on October 21, 2025.  Because the Board met in executive session on June 17, 
2025, it was not required to approve those executive session minutes in a timely manner but 
rather to review them at a reasonable interval.  Where the Board reviewed the June 17, 2025, 
executive session minutes within four months, we find that it did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law.  
 
We remind the Board that a public body’s obligation to review executive session minutes 
for possible release is ongoing.  If a public body reviews executive session minutes and 
determines that the purpose for the executive session remains such that the minutes should 
continue to be withheld from the public, it must continue to review those same executive session 
minutes at reasonable intervals until it determines that the reason for secrecy has expired.  See 
OML 2023-242; OML 2022- 155; OML 2021-129.  Moreover, a public body must announce the 
result of its review at the next meeting following its review.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); OML 
2024-168; OML 2015-94; OML 2013-56.  
 
III. The Board Chair Announced that the Meeting Was Being Recorded. 
 
Finally, the complaint alleges that the Chair failed to announce that the October 21, 2025, 
meeting was being recorded.  The Open Meeting Law requires that “[a]fter notifying the chair of 
the public body, any person may make a video or audio recording of an open session of a 
meeting of a public body, or may transmit the meeting through any medium, subject to 
reasonable requirements of the chair as to the number, placement and operation of equipment 
used so as not to interfere with the conduct of the meeting.  At the beginning of the meeting, the 
chair shall inform other attendees of any recordings.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(f).  The requirement that 
the chair inform attendees of any recording includes any recording made by members of the 
public body itself, as well as recordings made for public broadcasting or administrative purposes, 
such as assisting in the drafting of meeting minutes.  See OML 2019-129; OML 2016-155; OML 
2013-136; OML 2012-24. 
 
The October 21, 2025, meeting was recorded by Area 58 Community Access Media.  
After watching a video recording of the meeting, we find that the Chair announced at the 
beginning of the meeting that the meeting was being recorded.  As such, we find that the Board 
did not violate the Open Meeting Law.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to identify on the October 21, 2025, meeting notice the nonunion personnel with whom 
the Board would be negotiating in executive session.  We order immediate and future 
compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be 
considered evidence of intent to violate the law.
