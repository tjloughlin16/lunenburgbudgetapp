Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
improperly discussing matters that were not appropriate for executive session behind closed 
doors; by failing to include sufficient detail in its meeting notice and prior to entering into 
executive session; and by failing to review Open Meeting Law complaints before sending its 
response. We do not find that these were intentional violations. 
FACTS 
Based upon our review of the material listed above, the facts are as follows. Notice 
for the Committee's December 6, 2012 meeting, posted on December 3, 2012, listed among 
the items for discussion the following topic: "Executive Session: Contract negotiations for 
non-union employee." During the December 6, 2012 meeting, the Committee approved by 
roll call a vote "to go into executive session for a non-union employee." The Committee did 
not announce whether it would reconvene in open session following the closed door 
discussion. During the executive session, the Committee engaged in a discussion with the 
Committee's only employee. Cable Coordinator Chris Dancause. The Committee expressed 
several concerns about Mr. Dancause's performance before outlining its expectations and 
means of gauging his performance in the coming months leading up to his contract renewal in 
June 2013. 
Upon the filing of Mr. Higgins' December 13, 2012 and March 19, 2013 complaints. 
Attorney Frank sent a response on behalf of the Committee. The Committee did not discuss 
the complaints until it convened a meeting on June 27, 2013, at which time the Committee 
voted to refer both complaints to Town Counsel. 
DISCUSSION 
The Open Meeting Law requires that all meetings of a public body be conducted in an 
open session, with some exceptions. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a). Public bodies may enter a 
closed, executive session for any of ten purposes enumerated in the Open Meeting Law, 
provided that the chair of the public body first announces in open session the purpose for the 
executive session, "stating all subjects that may be revealed without compromising the 
purpose for which the executive session was called." G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), 21(b)(3). Before 
a public body convenes a meeting, however, it must post notice 48 hours in advance and 
include a "listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the 
meeting." G.L. c. 3OA, § 20(b). Public bodies must list the topics for discussion with 
"sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting." 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). 
While it did not cite the statutory purpose, it is clear that the Committee intended to 
meet in executive session pursuant to Purpose 2, to conduct contract negotiations with its 
nonunion personnel, namely the Cable Coordinator. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(2). The Board 
should have stated in the meeting notice the name of the specific non-union personnel with 
whom it was negotiating. Including this detail in the notice for the meeting would have put 
any interested member of the public on notice that the Committee was discussing strategy

Likewise, this detail about the purpose for the executive session should have been included in

Notwithstanding the Law's requirement for additional detail about the executive 
session purpose, the Committee's December 6, 2012 executive session discussion was not 
directly related to a contract negotiation. 5 Rather, it was an evaluation of the Cable 
Coordinator's performance prior to the commencement of a negotiation. The discussion that 
occurred was therefore inappropriate under Purpose 2, or any other executive session purpose, 
and should have occurred in open session. See District Attorney for Northern Dist. v. School 
Committee of Wavland. 455 Mass. 561, 568 (Mass. 2009) ("[wjhile professional competence 
must first be discussed in an open session, how that evaluation will factor into a contract or 
salary negotiation strategy may be a suitable discussion for an executive session"). 
Finally, the Committee did not convene a meeting to issue or authorize a response to 
either Open Meeting Law complaint, as required by 940 CMR 29.05(5) and G.L. c. 30A, § 
23(b). See also OML 2012-95; OML 2012-90. As the Committee acknowledged during its 
June 27, 2013 meeting, while a public body may authorize an individual such as the public 
body's Administrator or Town Counsel to respond on its behalf, the Open Meeting Law and 
the Attorney General's regulations require that the Board first review the Open Meeting Law 
complaint, though it may request an extension from this office if it is unable to respond within 
14 business days. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5). 
CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting 
Law by improperly discussing matters that were not appropriate for executive session behind 
closed doors; by failing to include sufficient detail in its meeting notice and prior to entering 
into executive session; and by failing to review Open Meeting Law complaints before sending 
its response. Accordingly, we order immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting 
Law, and caution that future similar conduct may be considered evidence of intent to violate 
the Open Meeting Law. In addition, we order the Committee, within thirty (30) days of 
receiving this determination, to review the minutes from its December 6, 2012 executive 
session, as well as any documents used therein, and release those minutes or documents, 
unless the attorney-client privilege or an exemption to the Public Records Law applies.
