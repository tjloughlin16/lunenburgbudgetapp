RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaints 
 
Dear Attorney D’Ortenzio:  
 
This office received two complaints from Mark Brooks on April 2, 2025, alleging that the 
Worthington Selectboard (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaints were originally filed with the Board on February 25, 2025, and you responded to 
both complaints, on behalf of the Board, by letter dated March 28, 2024, following an extension 
of time granted by this office.  The complaints allege that the Board failed to provide minutes of 
executive sessions held on October 1 and December 17, 2024, and continues to withhold the 
minutes past the time that publication would defeat the lawful purpose for the executive sessions.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaints, the 
Board’s response to the complaints, the complaint filed with our office requesting further review, 
and the notices and open and executive session minutes of the Board meetings held on October 1 
and December 17, 2024.   
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberation and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The law requires that all meetings of a public body be 
conducted in an open session, with some exceptions.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  A public 
body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the 
Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that a majority of

members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each member is 
recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly announced whether 
the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.
1   
 
One permissible reason to convene in executive session is to “discuss the reputation, 
character, physical condition or mental health, rather than professional competence, of an 
individual, or to discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or complaints or charges brought against, 
a public officer, employee, staff member or individual.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1) (“Purpose1”).  
The individual to be discussed in such executive session must be notified in writing by the public 
body at least 48 hours prior to the proposed executive session, and be afforded the right to be 
present during deliberations that involve that individual, the right to speak on his or her own 
behalf, the right to have an attorney present for the purpose of advising but not participating in 
the meeting, and the right to cause an independent record to be created of said executive session 
by audio-recording or transcription, at the individual's expense.  Id.   
 
Executive session minutes may be withheld from disclosure to the public “as long as 
publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no longer; provided, 
however, that the executive session was held in compliance with” the Open Meeting Law.  G.L. 
c. 30A, § 22(f).  When the purpose for a valid executive session has been served, the minutes and 
any documents or exhibits used at the session must be disclosed, unless the attorney-client 
privilege or an exemption to the Public Records Law applies.  Id.  The burden of justifying 
continued nondisclosure of executive session minutes lies with the public body.  Foudy v. 
Amherst-Pelham Regional School Committee, 402 Mass. 179, 184 (1988). 
 
The Board met in executive session on both October 1 and December 17, 2024, under 
Purpose 1 to discuss a complaint brought against a Highway Department employee.  On January 
21, 2025, the complainant submitted a public records request to the Town Clerk requesting 
executive session minutes of Board meetings held on October 1 and December 17, 2024.  On 
January 28, 2025, Town Clerk Katrin Kaminsky responded to the request stating that “Neither is 
available at this time.”  Thereafter, the complainant filed the Open Meeting Law complaints at 
issue here.  On March 7, 2025, Margaret O'Neal, Executive Assistant to the Board, sent an email 
to the complainant explaining that the “same issue was discussed in both sessions and a 
resolution has not been reached.  Until that time, we cannot release the minutes.”  The Board’s 
response to the Open Meeting Law complaints maintained that “the topic of the October 1, 2024 
and December 17, 2024 executive sessions is ongoing, and as such the Board will not be able to 
release those minutes.” 
 
When a public body convenes an executive session under Purpose 1, the purpose for the 
session generally concludes when the public body resolves the matter with the person who is the 
subject of the executive session.  See OML 2020-120; OML 2017-143.  Here, at the time of the 
request for minutes, the Board was still engaged in resolving the complaint.  Our role is not to 
supplant the Board’s judgment that disclosure of the minutes would defeat the lawful purpose of 
the executive sessions but rather to determine whether the Board has a reasonable basis for

continuing to withhold the executive session minutes.  We find that the Board has made a 
sufficient showing to justify its nondisclosure of the minutes of October 1 and December 17, 
2024, executive sessions.   
 
We remind the Board that executive session minutes may not be withheld under the Open 
Meeting Law indefinitely.  Indeed, if the purpose for the executive session has concluded, then 
the Open Meeting Law would no longer provide a basis for withholding the executive session 
minutes.  Generally, with respect to Purpose 1 discussions, if the Board has taken all the action it 
was authorized to take with respect to an employee, then the Open Meeting Law would no longer 
provide a basis for withholding the executive session minutes.  See OML 2021-143.  However, 
even if the Open Meeting Law no longer authorizes the withholding of minutes or documents, 
the materials may be exempt from production, in whole or in part, under the Public Records 
Law.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f); OML 2020-142; OML 2015-170.  We further remind the Board 
of its continuing obligation to review, at reasonable intervals, its executive session minutes to 
determine if continued nondisclosure of those minutes is warranted.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); 
OML 2024-24.  Although “reasonable interval” is not defined by the law, our office has found a 
quarterly review or a review every six months to be appropriate.  See OML 2019-45; OML 
2017-104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180. 
 
We find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law by withholding the 
executive session minutes of the October 1 and December 17, 2024, meetings.  We now consider 
the complaints addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This determination does not 
address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the Board.  Please feel free 
to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions regarding this letter.
