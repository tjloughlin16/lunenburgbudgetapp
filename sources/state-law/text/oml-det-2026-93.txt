 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Delmar: 
 
This office received a complaint from Patrick Higgins on February 6, 2026,
1 alleging that 
the Taunton School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on or about January 13, and 
you
2 responded, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated February 6.3 The complaint alleges 
that the Committee failed to respond to a request for meeting minutes within 10 calendar days. 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law in the 
way alleged. In reaching this determination we reviewed the original Open Meeting Law 
complaint and attachments, the Committee’s response, and the complainant’s request for review.

Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Open Meeting Law requires public bodies to create 
and approve minutes of all open sessions in a timely manner. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c). The minutes 
of an open session, if they exist and whether approved or in draft form, shall be made available 
upon request by any person within 10 days. Id. When a public body receives a request for 
executive session minutes, the body must respond to the request within 10 days. See G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 22(g)(2). If at the time of the request the public body has not yet performed a review of the 
requested minutes, as provided by G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1), the body must perform the review and 
release the non-exempt minutes, or any portion thereof, not later than the body’s next meeting or 
30 days, whichever occurs first. Id. If this review is required, the public body must still respond 
to the requester within 10 days of receiving the request, informing the requester that the public 
body will conduct a review to determine whether the minutes may be withheld or must be 
released. See OML 2023-62; OML Declination 10-15-2025 (Hubbardston Select Board).

Requests for meeting minutes may fall under the Public Records Law or the Open 
Meeting Law, depending on how the request is made, and the requirements for responding differ. 
A request for meeting minutes made directly to the public body falls under the Open Meeting 
Law and requires a response within 10 calendar days. See OML 2019-163. However, a request 
made to a municipal employee, such as a Town Clerk, would likely trigger the Public Records 
Law and requires a response within 10 business days. See G.L. c. 66, § 10;
 see also OML 2019-
126. 
 
Here, on January 2, the complainant sent an email to then-Committee Chair Gregory 
DeMelo, requesting copies of “both open and executive session minutes [of the Committee], for 
calendar years 2024 and 2025.”
5 Because the request for meeting minutes was directed to the 
Committee Chair, the request fell under the Open Meeting Law and required a response within 
10 calendar days. See OML 2019-163. After receiving no response, the complainant filed an 
Open Meeting Law complaint with the Committee on January 13. On January 16, Attorney 
Delmar responded to the request for minutes and directed the complainant to the Committee’s 
website where the minutes were posted.6 Our office has previously said directing a requester to a 
website where the requested minutes are posted and accessible does satisfy the requirement to 
make minutes “available.” See OML 2024-202. Nonetheless, where the Committee failed to 
respond to the January 2 request for minutes within 10 days, we find that the Committee violated 
the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2026-74; OML 2026-73. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to respond to a request for meeting minutes within 10 days. We order the 
Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, and caution that 
similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the Law.
