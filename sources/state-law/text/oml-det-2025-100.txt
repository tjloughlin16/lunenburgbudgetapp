RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints  
 
Dear Assistant City Solicitor Degnan:  
 
This office received a complaint from Linda Vacon on April 18, 2025, alleging that the 
Holyoke City Council (the “Council”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  
The complaint was originally filed with the Council on February 22 and you responded on behalf 
of the Council by letter dated March 7.1  The complaint alleges that the notice of the Council’s 
February 4 meeting was not sufficiently specific.   
 
Foll
owing our review, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting Law in 
the way alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Council’s response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  In 
addition, we viewed recordings of meetings the Council held on February 4 and February 18.2  
We also reviewed the notices and minutes of those two meetings.

DISCUSSION 
 
 The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public body must post notice of every meeting at least 
48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). 
Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed at 
the meeting.”  Id.  The list of topics must have sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the 
public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).   
 
We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member of 
the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s 
discussion.  See OML 2023-3; OML 2019-134; OML 2015- 35.5  The Open Meeting Law

requires that the notice describe topics with sufficient specificity so that the public can make an 
informed decision whether to attend the meeting to observe the discussion regarding a topic of 
interest.  See OML 2023-86; OML 2019-95; OML 2018-22.   
 
The Open Meeting Law regulations contemplate that a meeting notice may be amended, 
even within 48 hours of a meeting.  See 940 CMR 29.03(1)(d).  Specifically, a public body may 
amend a meeting notice within 48 hours of a meeting to include an unanticipated topic and then 
discuss that topic during the meeting.  See OML 2024-160; OML 2023-173; OML 2019-131. 
Indeed, a public body may discuss and even take action on a truly unanticipated topic during a 
meeting despite the topic not appearing on the meeting notice at all.  See OML 2024-57; OML 
2020-172; OML 2019-131.  Nonetheless, the Attorney General strongly recommends that public 
bodies, as a matter of best practice, table discussion of unanticipated topics that might be of 
significant interest to the public so that the topics can be included on the notice for a future 
meeting.  See OML 2019-131; OML 2018-119; OML 2013-87.  When reviewing whether a topic 
was anticipated, we look to whether it was reasonably anticipated for discussion at the particular 
meeting at issue.  See OML 2024-6; OML 2023-101; OML 2022-203.   
 
The complaint alleges that the six topics that were included in the amended notice of the 
February 4 meeting as late filed orders and communications were anticipated 48 hours prior to 
the February 4 meeting, and therefore, they were required to be included in the notice that was 
posted 48 hours prior to the meeting in order for the Council to discuss those items on February 
4.  When reviewing a meeting notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, 
we do not review a meeting notice standing alone. See OML 2022-148; OML 2015-109; OML 
2014-155.  Rather, we review what the public body actually discussed at the meeting to 
determine whether the notice was sufficiently specific to reasonably inform the public of the 
topic to be discussed.  See OML 2015-109.  When a public body does not actually deliberate on 
a noticed topic, the public body cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with respect to that topic, 
even if it would have been insufficiently specific had the public body proceeded with its 
deliberation.  See OML 2020-53; OML 2015-63; OML 2014-81.   
 Assuming arguendo that the late filed orders and communications were anticipated 48 
hours prior to the February 4 meeting, they would need to be included on the notice only if they 
were actually discussed at the February 4 meeting.  As a preliminary matter, we note that the 
vote to lay on the table the six late items did not constitute discussion or action requiring 
inclusion on the notice.  See OML 2022-159 (finding that where quorum of Committee appeared 
for Board meeting but voted to table and adjourn Committee meeting because members were 
unprepared, there was no “meeting” for purposes of Open Meeting Law); OML 2017-197 
(concluding that no deliberation occurred and no Open Meeting Law violation could be found 
where public body tabled discussion of topics that were alleged to be insufficiently noticed); 
OML 2017-171 (finding that postponement of discussion of topic that was insufficiently listed in 
notice complied with Open Meeting Law). 
Although there was no substantive discussion of the late filed items at the February 4 
meeting, the complainant argues that deliberation for purposes of the Open Meeting Law did 
occur about at least one of the late items because a first reading occurred at the February 4 
meeting.  In other words, the Council did not engage in a discussion in the traditional sense but,

according to the complainant, the first reading that occurred on February 4 required inclusion on 
the notice, as it is a necessary step in the approval of that proposed order.6 
 
It does not appear that we have previously determined whether the reading of a proposed 
order (as “reading” is understood in the legislative context) constitutes a topic of discussion for 
purposes of notice under the Open Meeting Law.  We need not decide the issue here.  At the 
February 18 meeting, Councilors disagreed whether a first reading occurred at the February 4 
meeting and therefore held a vote to pass the first reading at the February 18 meeting.  It is not 
our role to opine on the effect of certain actions of the Council.  Where a majority of the Council 
apparently believed it did not pass a first reading at the February 4 meeting and thus did it at the 
February 18 meeting, we are constrained to find that a first reading did not occur on February 4.  
Therefore, the February 4 meeting did not include any discussion of the late filed items, even if 
we were to adopt the broad interpretation that the complainant ascribes to the word “discussion” 
under the Open Meeting Law.  Because there was no discussion of the late filed orders at the 
February 4 meeting, we find no insufficiency in the notice. 
 
Although we do not find a violation, we take this opportunity to remind the Council that 
designation of an item of discussion as “late filed” does not remove it from the requirements of 
the Open Meeting Law if the item is reasonably anticipated by the chair or others to whom the 
responsibility of preparing the notice is delegated.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); OML 2022-67; 
OML 2020-80; OML 2015-190.7  Moreover, any suspension of the rules concerns rules 
particular to the Council and does not affect the requirements of the Open Meeting Law.8  When 
the Council voted in favor of “suspending the rules,” it did not, and could not, suspend the 
application of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2024-197; OML 2022-135.   
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Council did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law in the way alleged.
