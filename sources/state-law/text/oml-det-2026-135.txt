 RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Attorney Randazzo: 
 
This office received a complaint from Ronald Beaty on September 12, 2025,
1 alleging 
that the Provincetown Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on August 18, and you responded 
on behalf of the Board by letter dated September 10. The complaint alleges that the notice for the 
Board’s July 28 meeting included three topics that were not sufficiently specific. 
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter. Following our 
review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law with respect to one of the three 
notice topics alleged to have been insufficient. We find that the Board did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law with respect to the other two topics. In reaching this determination, we reviewed 
the Open Meeting Law complaint, the Board’s response, the request for further review, and the 
notice for and video recording of the July 28 meeting.
2 
 
FACTS 
 
We find the facts to be as follows. On Tuesday, July 22, the Board posted notice of a 
meeting to be held on Monday, July 28. Relevant here, the notice included, among others, the 
following topics: “Town Manager’s Report”; “Select Board Closing Matters - Topics to Include 
but not limited to: (Votes May be taken on the following items)”; and, for an executive session,

The Town Manager wrote a memo to the Board dated July 22 and entitled “Department

The Town Manager’s memo was included in the meeting packet for the Board’s July 28 
meeting. The page immediately preceding the memo in the meeting packet was titled “Town 
Manager’s Report.” The page also noted that the “Action Sought” by the Town Manager with 
respect to the report was a “Discussion.”    
 
The July 28 meeting was held as scheduled. When the Board reached the topic “Town 
Manager’s Report,” the Manager began by recognizing and thanking the Fire Department and the 
Police Department. The Town Manager also reported on a few administrative matters, 
announced public events, and discussed activities of the Finance Committee and matters related 
to Fiscal Year 2025. After this initial presentation, the Town Manager noted that there were 
many more topics included in his written report and gestured in a manner that appeared to 
welcome discussion on those topics. Board members then asked questions, engaged in 
discussions, and interviewed department heads and staff regarding various topics included in the 
Town Manager’s report. For example, the Board spent a significant amount of time questioning 
the chair of the Airport Commission, who the Town Manager had invited to the meeting in order 
to answer the Board’s questions. In total, the discussion under the topic “Town Manager’s 
Report” lasted approximately 30 minutes.  
 
When the Board reached the notice topic “Select Board Closing Matters - Topics to 
Include but not limited to: (Votes May be taken on the following items),” there was no 
discussion. Finally, prior to convening in executive session, a member of the Board read aloud 
the reasons for which the Board would convene in executive session that evening, including 
“MGL c30A Sec 21(a) Clause 1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9 or 10, to consider and approve previous 
executive session minutes from May 27, 2025.” Thereafter, the Board voted to convene in 
executive session and the open session portion of the meeting ended. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. Sch. Comm. of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Open Meeting Law requires that, “[e]xcept in an 
emergency, . . . a public body shall post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to the 
meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Meeting 
notices must include, among other things, a list of the topics that the chair, and any other 
individual authorized to create the notice, reasonably anticipates 48 hours in advance (excluding 
===PAGE 3===
3 
 
weekends and legal holidays) will be discussed at the meeting. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); OML 
2022-206; OML 2020-152.3 The topics for discussion must be listed on the notice with 
“sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the 
meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). We generally consider a topic to include sufficient specificity 
when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated 
nature of the public body’s discussion. See OML 2019-70. When reviewing a meeting notice for 
sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review the notice standing 
alone. See OML 2015-21; OML 2014-155. Rather, we review what was discussed at the meeting 
to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed. See OML 2015-101.  
 
I. The notice topic “Town Manager’s Report” was insufficiently specific, in violation of 
the Open Meeting Law. 
 
In the past, when a meeting notice included as a topic a report or presentation by 
somebody who is not a member of the public body, such as “Superintendent’s Report” or 
“Administrator’s Report,” we have declined to find a violation of the Open Meeting Law for lack 
of specificity where the chair did not anticipate the specific nature of the deliberations or where 
the individual presenting the report simply provided status updates that were not discussed by the 
public body. See OML 2025-3 (finding no violation where neither the chair nor the individual 
responsible for creating the meeting notice knew what the water superintendent would discuss 
during his report); OML 2020-87 (finding no violation where “the Chair was not aware of what 
the Superintendent intended to report to the Committee ahead of the meeting”); OML 2019-94 
(finding no violation when a meeting notice included the topic “Discussion of Legal 
Settlements” without identifying any particular legal matters, but the committee merely heard a 
report from the superintendent and did not engage in any discussion); OML 2013-111 (finding 
no violation where the chair “did not reasonably anticipate, more than 48 hours before the 
meeting, the specific topics that the Town Administrator would address during his report”); 
OML 2011-26 (finding that the board could not have reasonably anticipated discussion of a topic 
raised during the town manager’s report where the memo outlining the issue had been prepared 
the same day as the meeting and the town manager raised the issue with the chair just prior to the 
meeting). Nonetheless, we encourage chairs, as a best practice, to solicit from the presenter 
details regarding the precise matters to be discussed and to include such details on the meeting 
notice. See OML 2018-33; OML 2012-19 (explaining that the standing notice topic 
“superintendent’s report” followed by a statement that the superintendent had his own items to 
be discussed was insufficient).  
 
On the other hand, if a public body chair actually knows in advance the specific nature of 
the topics to be discussed during a report, that information must be included on the meeting 
notice. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b) (meeting notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair 
reasonably anticipates will be discussed at the meeting.”); 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b) (notice topics 
must be listed with “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be 
discussed at the meeting.”); see also OML 2021-142(finding that discussion of returning a school 
building to the town was reasonably anticipated where the chair received a letter from the 
superintendent regarding this issue and the chair then raised the issue at a meeting); OML 2015-

54 (finding a violation where public body discussed reports from legal counsel that the chair 
distributed during a meeting where the chair anticipated that the reports would generate 
discussion).4 Finally, the meeting notice itself must include the required level of specificity; it is 
insufficient to require members of the public to search through other documents or sources to 
learn information that should have been included on the notice. See OML 2024-9. 
 
The complaint alleges that the notice topic “Town Manager Report” was insufficiently 
specific. In its response, the Board does not assert that the topics discussed under this notice 
topic were unexpected; nor would the evidence before us support such an assertion. Instead, the 
Board explains that “[t]he Town typically posts the agenda for Monday meetings on the 
preceding Tuesday, and the meeting packets on the preceding Wednesday,” noting that “[t]his is 
well in advance of the 48 hour posting deadline.” The Board further asserts that “[t]he ‘Town 
Manager’s Report’ agenda item is supported by a lengthy written report that is included in the 
publicly posted meeting packet, which is posted to the Town’s website the day after the agenda 
[is] posted.” Finally, the Board seems to suggest that it held no discussion under the topic “Town 
Manager’s Report.” The video evidence before us flatly contradicts this assertion.   
 
Based on the evidence before us, we find that the topics discussed during the “Town 
Manager’s Report” were reasonably anticipated for discussion during the July 28 meeting. First, 
the Board was aware of the topics the Town Manager might discuss during his July 28 report to 
the Board where the Town Manager wrote a memo regarding these topics addressed to the 
Board, dated July 22, and titled “Department Update Report for the July 28, 2025 Select Board 
Meeting.” Second, the Board anticipated discussing matters raised in the memo. The page 
preceding the memo in the meeting packet indicated that the “action sought” was a discussion, 
which is exactly what occurred, and Board members arrived at the July 28 meeting prepared to 
discuss, ask questions, and interview town officials regarding various matters raised in the 
memo. As such, the Board was obligated to provide the public notice that it anticipated 
discussing these matters. Its failure to do so violated the Open Meeting Law. As noted above, 
requiring members of the public to search through other documents or sources, such as the Town 
Manager’s memo, to learn of the specific topics that might be discussed at the meeting does not 
comply with the Open Meeting Law’s requirement that meeting notices include a list of the 
topics anticipated for discussion, described with sufficient specificity.  
 
II. Because the Board did not hold any discussion under the topic “Select Board Closing 
Matters - Topics to Include but not limited to: (Votes May be taken on the following 
items),” the notice cannot have been insufficient with respect to that topic. 
 
When a public body does not actually engage in any deliberation on a particular topic, it 
cannot violate the Open Meeting Law with respect to the sufficiency of the notice topic. See 
OML 2022-148; OML 2024-241; OML 2021-43; OML 2019-94. The complaint alleges that the 
topic “Select Board Closing Matters - Topics to Include but not limited to: (Votes May be taken 
on the following items)” was insufficiently specific. Because the Board did not hold any 
discussions under this topic, we find no violation with respect to this allegation.

III. The executive session topic regarding review of executive session minutes was 
sufficient to advise the public of the anticipated nature of the executive session 
discussion.  
 
A public body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes 
enumerated in the Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session; 
announced the purpose for the executive session, stating all subjects that may be revealed 
without compromising the purpose for the executive session; a majority of members have voted 
by roll call to convene in executive session; and the chair has announced whether the open 
session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session. G.L. c. 30A, §§ 21(a), (b); see 
also OML 2014-94. Executive session topics must be described, both in the meeting notice and 
in the open session announcement, in as much detail as possible without compromising the 
purpose for which the executive session was called. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also District 
Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“A precise 
statement of the reason for convening in executive session is necessary . . . because that is the 
only notification given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the 
only way the public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper.”). A public 
body need not specifically identify the statutory purpose for the executive session, as long as the 
topic makes clear which of the ten executive session purposes the public body is invoking. See 
OML 2021-169; OML 2017-7. 
 
We have explained that a public body may convene in executive session to review and 
approve executive session minutes under either the original purpose for the executive session, or 
under G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(7) (“Purpose 7”), citing to the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c.30A, §§ 
22(f), (g).
5 
 
The complaint alleges that the topic “MGL c30A Sec 21(a) Clause 1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9 or 10, 
to consider and approve previous executive session minutes from May 27, 2025” is insufficient 
because it “fails to identify the exact purpose(s) applicable to the discussion.” We agree that 
citing all ten executive session purposes, without additional detail, would be insufficient. 
However, we find that the additional detail “to consider and approve previous executive session 
minutes from May 27, 2025” provides sufficient notice to the public of the anticipated nature of 
the executive session discussion such that the public would know if the reason for convening in 
executive session was proper. See District Attorney for the N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 
455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009). 
  
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
where the notice topic “Town Manager’s Report” was insufficiently specific. We find that the 
Board did not otherwise violate the Open Meeting Law as alleged. We order the Board’s 
immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law and caution that similar future 
violations may be considered evidence of an intent to violate the Law. 
 
5 Purpose 7 allows a public body to convene in executive session “[t]o comply with, or act under the authority of, 
any general or special law or federal grant-in-aid requirements.” G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(7). When convening in 
executive session under Purpose 7, a public body must identify the specific law that it is relying on unless doing so 
would compromise the purpose for the executive session.
