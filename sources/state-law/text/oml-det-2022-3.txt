RE: Open Meeting Law Complaint 
Dear Attorney Bryant: 
This office received a complaint from Deborah Colón on December 20, 2021, alleging 
that the Carver School Committee (the “Committee”), and specifically Vice Chair Andrew 
Cardarelli, violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. The complaint was originally 
filed with the Committee on or about September 9, 2021, and you responded on behalf of the 
Committee by letter dated September 27, 2021. The complaint alleges that Mr. Cardarelli 1) 
deliberated via email; 2) “conspired to prevent an individual from exercising their right to speak 
at public comment”; and 3) made negative comments about students and others.   
Following our review, we resolve this complaint by informal action in accordance with 
940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), and find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by 
deliberating via email. We decline to review the remaining allegations. At the outset, we note 
that we do not review, and offer no opinion on, the substance of any Committee member’s public 
comments, but rather review only whether the Committee communicated in a manner that 
violated the Open Meeting Law. In reaching this determination we reviewed the original Open 
Meeting Law complaint, the Committee’s response, the request for further review, and the three 
emails that form the basis of the complaint.  
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.” Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978). The Law requires that all meetings of a public body be 
noticed and that meetings be open to the public, unless an executive session is convened. G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21. A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a public 
body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.” G.L. c 30A, § 18. The Law

defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including 
electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its 
jurisdiction . . . .” G.L. c. 30A, § 18. For purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a quorum is “a 
simple majority of the members of the public body.” G.L. c. 30A, § 18. A public body may not 
use a non-member, such as a staff member, to facilitate communication on matters that the public 
body should otherwise save for discussion at an open meeting. See District Attorney for the 
Northern District v. School Committee of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 569-71 (2009); OML 2013-
76; OML 2018-43.1 Finally, the expression of an opinion on matters within a body’s jurisdiction 
by a public body member to a quorum of the public body is deliberation, even if no other public 
body member responds. See OML 2019-57; OML 2012-15; OML 2012-8; OML 2011-37. 
  
 The Committee concedes, and we find, that on September 26, 2020; May 19, 2021; and 
July 26, 2021,2 Mr. Cardarelli improperly deliberated via email by either emailing a quorum of 
the Committee directly regarding public business within the Committee’s jurisdiction or causing 
such emails to be forwarded to a quorum of the Committee. Therefore, we find that the 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by deliberating outside of a properly noticed 
meeting.  
 
 The Committee contends that it cured the violation with respect to the September 26, 
2020, email when it voted to release the minutes of its October 1, 2020, executive session in 
which it discussed the September 26 email. The Committee explains that along with the release 
of those executive session minutes, it also released the September 26 email, subject to redactions. 
Although we commend the Committee for taking this remedial action, we find that this action 
was insufficient to fully remedy the violation.  
 
The Open Meeting Law provides that minutes of an executive session meeting, as well as 
the materials used to prepare the minutes, “may be withheld from disclosure to the public in their 
entirety . . . [for] as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, 
but no longer.” G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f). Similarly, all documents and exhibits used at the executive 
session may be withheld for as long as publication would defeat the lawful purpose of the 
executive session. Id. However, a public body may only rely on section 22(f) of the Open 
Meeting Law to withhold executive session minutes and records if “the executive session was 
held in compliance with section 21” of the Open Meeting Law. Id.; see also OML 2019-140. 
Even if the October 1, 2020, executive session was held in compliance with section 21 of the 
Open Meeting Law, the September 26, 2020, email deliberation itself was not. To the extent the 
Committee asserts that the contents of the September 26, 2020, email are protected by the 
attorney-client privilege, we note that the Supreme Judicial Court has held that the Open Meeting 
Law constitutes a “statutory public waiver of any possible privilege of the public client in 
meetings of governmental bodies except in the narrow circumstances stated in the [Open 
Meeting Law].” District Attorney for the Plymouth Dist. v. Selectmen of Middleborough, 395

At its September 27, 2021, meeting the Committee publicly disclosed the May 19 and 
July 26, 2021, emails and attached those emails to the minutes of the September 27, 2021, 
meeting. We find this to be sufficient remedial action and order no further action with respect to 
the May 19 and July 26, 2021, emails. 
 
Next, we must determine whether the violation found here was, as the Complainant 
urges, intentional. See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c). An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a 
public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].” 940 CMR 
29.02. An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate 
ignorance of the Law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates 
the Open Meeting Law. Id. Here, we have not previously warned the Committee against the 
violation found in this determination, nor do we find other evidence that would support a finding 
of an intentional violation. We therefore decline to find that the violation was intentional.  
 
Finally, we do not review the allegations that Mr. Cardarelli “conspired to prevent an 
individual from exercising their right to speak at public comment” and made negative comments 
about students and others. The Division of Open Government’s review concerns compliance 
with the Open Meeting Law. The Open Meeting Law does not require that a public body allow 
public participation in a meeting. See OML 2014-23; OML 2012-23. To the contrary, the Open 
Meeting Law provides that “[n]o person shall address a meeting of a public body without 
permission of the chair, and all persons shall, at the request of the chair, be silent.” G.L. c. 30A, 
§ 20(g). Nonetheless, we encourage public bodies to allow for as much public participation as 
time and circumstances permit. Likewise, although we certainly discourage uncivil and 
unprofessional behavior, an allegation of unprofessional conduct or incivility does not constitute 
a violation of the Open Meeting Law. See OML 2020-36, n. 5; OML 2019-166; OML 
Declination 6-28-16 (Kingston Board of Selectmen). Because neither allegation, even if true, 
would constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law, we decline to review these allegations. 
We offer no opinion as to whether any actions taken by the Committee or its members might 
violate of the First Amendment or any other law or regulation outside the scope of our review. 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by deliberating via email. We order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the 
Open Meeting Law, and caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the Law. Additionally, we order the Committee to release to the public the 
September 26, 2020, email in its entirety within 30 days of the date of this letter.  
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved. This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee. Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions.
