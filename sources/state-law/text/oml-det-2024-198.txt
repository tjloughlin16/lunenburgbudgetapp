RE: Open Meeting Law Complaints  
 
Dear Attorney North:  
 
On August 21, 2024, this office received a complaint from Owen Smith, alleging that the 
Newburyport City Council Budget and Finance Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25.  Mr. Smith filed the complaint with the Committee on July 
17, and you responded on behalf of the Committee by letter dated July 26.1  The complaint 
alleges that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by: (1) failing to timely review and 
approve meeting minutes; (2) creating and approving minutes of the June 20 meeting which were 
not sufficiently detailed, were inaccurate, and did not contain a list of documents used at the 
meeting; and (3) allowing public comment at its June 20 meeting when public comment was not 
listed on the notice.2 
 
Following our review, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law when 
it failed to timely approve minutes and when it created and approved minutes of the June 20 
meeting which were not sufficiently detailed.  We find that it did not violate the Law in the other 
ways alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the 
Committee’s response to the complaint, and the complainant’s request for further review.  We 
also viewed a recording of the Committee’s June 20 meeting, reviewed minutes of the June 20 
meeting, and reviewed an email from Committee Chair Sharif Zeid, dated June 17.

FACTS  
We find the facts as follows.  The Committee posted notice for and held an open meeting 
on June 20.  At the commencement of the meeting, Chair Zeid made introductory remarks about 
the budget process and previewed the order of topics to be discussed at the meeting.  He 
commented that the agenda was outlined in an email.  Although Chair Zeid did not specify the 
date or recipients of the email, the Committee has identified and produced the email, which 
Chair Zeid sent to Richard Jones, Ethan Manning and Andrew Levine on June 17.  The email 
lists a general “high-level” discussion of the budget, followed by a discussion of each 
department’s budget.  The June 20 meeting lasted approximately one hour and forty-five 
minutes.  A member of the public, Jane Snow, made several comments during the meeting.  
Minutes of the June 20 meeting are one page long and contain no list of documents used at the 
meeting.    
DISCUSSION  
 
I. The Committee failed to approve minutes in a timely manner. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Board of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Law requires public bodies to create and approve 
minutes of open session meetings in a timely manner.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(c).  “Timely manner” 
means “within the next three public body meetings or within 30 days, whichever is later, unless 
the public body can show good cause for further delay.”  940 CMR 29.11.  Whenever possible, 
we recommend that minutes of a meeting be approved at the next meeting.  See OML 2018-67; 
OML 2017-133.4  The Committee has acknowledged that although it has created minutes of its 
meetings in a timely manner, the minutes “may not have been formally approved by the 
Committee.”  Because the Committee does not dispute that at least some open session minutes 
have not been timely approved, we find a violation as to this allegation.  We take this 
opportunity to remind the Committee that the Open Meeting Law does not govern the method for 
approving minutes.  As such, public bodies may choose the method for approving their minutes, 
including approval by the public body chair or another designee alone, by majority vote of the 
public body, or by consent of the body (approved unless there is an objection).  See OML 2024-
186; OML 2023-204; OML 2019-25.  When a quorum of a public body approves minutes, 
however, it must do so during a noticed meeting, and the notice must include the specific 
meeting minutes to be approved.  See OML 2022-173; OML 2019-25; OML 2017-92. 
 
II. Minutes of the June 20 meeting are not sufficiently detailed. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that public bodies “create and maintain accurate minutes 
of all meetings, including executive sessions, setting forth the date, time and place, the members 
present or absent, a summary of the discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other 
exhibits used at the meeting, the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including 
the record of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a); 940 CMR 29.11(1).  When reviewing minutes for

compliance with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy 
requirement.  Minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public 
who did not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what 
occurred.  See OML 2024-174; OML 2023-35; OML 2021-140.  The Open Meeting Law 
requires that meeting minutes include more than a statement that a public body held a discussion 
about a specified topic; the Law requires that the minutes summarize the substance of the public 
body’s discussion.  See OML 2020-53; OML 2019-167; OML 2018-8.  We encourage public 
bodies to include dissenting or minority opinions whenever feasible.  See OML 2023-9; OML 
2019-163.  While it is not necessary to record the comments of every speaker, and we 
acknowledge the difficulty of doing so where there are many speakers whose remarks may even 
overlap, if a particular individual speaks at some length or is the only one to offer an argument 
for or against a proposal, that person and his or her comments should be identified in the 
minutes.  See OML 2023-9; OML 2019-163; OML 2012-29. 
 
The complainant alleges that minutes of the June 20 meeting are deficient in several 
ways.  First, he maintains that the minutes do not provide a sufficiently detailed summary of 
discussions held during the June 20 meeting.  We agree.  The June 20 meeting was over 100 
minutes long and included extended discussions of “free cash” accounts, tax rates, collective 
bargaining units and salaries, among other topics.  However, minutes of the June 20 meeting are 
only one page long and consist almost entirely of a list of proposed budget orders, identified by 
numbers and acronyms, with a vote of “no recommendation” following each.  The only summary 
of any discussion is the brief synopsis of public comment: “Jane Snow . . . Question about 
schools, making commitment to school choice, tax rate increases are tough.”  There is no 
summary of any of the Committee members’ remarks or discussions.  The minutes fall far short 
of the level of detail required by the Open Meeting Law, and we find a violation of the Law as to 
this allegation.  As ordered below, the substance of the Committee’s discussions must be 
summarized in the minutes. 
   
The second deficiency in the minutes alleged by the complainant is the omission of a list 
of documents “used” in the meeting.  Specifically, the complainant alleges that the June 17 email 
that was referenced by Chair Zeid should have been listed in the minutes.  The Open Meeting 
Law does not define what it means for a document to be “used” at a meeting, such that it needs 
to be listed in the minutes.  We have explained that a document is “used” at a meeting when it is, 
at a minimum, physically present, verbally identified, and its contents are discussed by members 
of the public body.  See OML 2014-146; OML 2019-57; OML 2012-42.  Merely mentioning that 
correspondence had taken place or referencing information from within a document does not 
render the document “used” such that it needs to be listed in the meeting minutes.  See OML 
2024-75; OML 2023-145; OML 2014-129.  At the Committee’s June 20 meeting, Chair Zeid 
referenced his June 17 email in passing as he outlined how he expected to conduct the evening’s 
meeting.  The email was only cryptically identified and was not at all discussed.  Therefore, the 
Committee was not required to list it in the minutes of the June 20 meeting. 
 
Finally, the complainant alleges that the minutes inaccurately reflect two motions to 
approve the budget and “CIP” order, where both were the subject of a single motion.  Minutes 
must document “the decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record 
of all votes.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Although the minutes are not as clear as they could be in

documenting votes taken at the meeting, they do reflect that no recommendation was made as to 
any of the matters before the Committee on June 20.  Therefore, we do not find a violation as to  
this allegation.  Although we find no violation, we recommend that when the Committee revises 
the minutes to include further detail about discussions, it also clarify the details about the votes 
taken on June 20. 
 
III. The Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it omitted public 
comment from the notice of the June 20 meeting. 
   
Pursuant to the Open Meeting Law, a public body must post notice of every meeting at 
least 48 hours in advance, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays.  G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b).  Notices must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed at the meeting.”  Id.  The list of topics must have sufficient specificity to reasonably 
advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  The Open 
Meeting Law does not require that a public body offer an opportunity for public comment on 
matters on the meeting’s agenda, nor does the Open Meeting Law require that a meeting notice 
specify that there will be an opportunity for public comment if one is anticipated.  See G.L. c. 
30A § 20(g); OML 2017-189; OML 2014-31; OML 2014-23.  Public comment is not itself a 
topic for the public body’s discussion that needs to be listed on the meeting notice.  See OML 
2023-205; OML 2022-62.  It is reasonably inferable that a public body may allow the public to 
comment on items listed on its posted meeting notice, even if public comment is not specifically 
listed on the meeting notice.  See OML 2023-67; OML 2022-62; OML 2014-31.  The Committee 
was not required to specifically list public comment on the notice for the June 20 meeting, even 
if public comment was anticipated, and did not violate the Open Meeting Law in not doing so. 
 
CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law 
when it failed to timely approve minutes and when it created and approved minutes of the June 
20 meeting which were not sufficiently detailed.  We order the Committee to amend its June 20 
meeting minutes in accordance with the above guidance and to certify to this office within sixty 
(60) days of the date of this letter that it has done so.  See 940 CMR 29.07(4).  We further order 
the Committee to approve all open session minutes that have not yet been approved in a timely 
manner in accordance with the above guidance and to certify to this office within sixty (60) days 
of the date of this letter that it has done so.  We order immediate and future compliance with the 
Law’s requirements and we caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.
