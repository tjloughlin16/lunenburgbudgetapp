Northborough Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-

not listing the establishments to be considered for license renewal, as required by the Open

We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter. Following our 
review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law with respect to the first 
allegation; however, it subsequently cured that violation. We find that the Board did not violate 
the Open Meeting Law with respect to the second allegation. In reaching this determination, we 
reviewed the complaint, the Board’s response to the complaint, and the request for review. In 
addition, we reviewed the meeting notice, the meeting packet, the meeting minutes, the event

FACTS 
 
 We find the facts as follows. The town of Northborough’s official notice posting location 
is its municipal website.3 The Board posted notice on the municipal website for a meeting to be 
held on December 15, 2025. The website indicates that the notice was originally posted on 
December 11, 2025, at 4:37 p.m. It further indicates that there were revisions to the notice on 
December 11 and December 12 of 2025. The notice itself is stamped with a tag that indicates it 
was “reviewed” on December 12, 2025, at 4:18 p.m.  
 
 The meeting notice for the December 15, 2025, meeting included, among others, the 
topic “Consideration of Renewal of Selectmen’s Licenses for Calendar Year 2026, as outlined in 
the memorandum included in the meeting packet; discussion and possible vote.” The notice itself 
did not include a list of the establishments to be considered for license renewal. The town 
website contains a separate link to the meeting packet for the December 15, 2025, meeting. The 
meeting packet is 131 pages long and includes, among other things, the list of establishments that 
were to be considered for license renewal at the meeting.  
 
 The meeting was held as scheduled on December 15, 2025.When the Board reached the 
topic of license renewals, Chair Julianne Hirsch stated that the Board had a list of establishments 
that were to be considered to have their licenses renewed. Chair Hirsch stated that there were no 
problems with the businesses noted by the building inspector, fire department, or police 
department. The Chair then stated that the motion would read that license renewal would be 
contingent upon a passing inspection from all three entities, as there were a couple of minor 
issues noted by the building inspector. Examples of the minor issues were the need for new light 
switch covers and replacing cracked ceiling tiles at some businesses. There were no issues that 
were life-endangering. Board member Jacob Jones stated that the assumption would be that the 
building inspector, fire, and police, would do a proper job with their inspections. Board member 
Jones alluded to complaints regarding the building department but declined to go into detail.  
 
 During the discussion of license renewals, an individual identified as “Angie,” believed 
to be Executive Assistant Angie Sowden, joined the meeting online (the meeting was held in-
person). Executive Assistant Sowden stated that there were no issues with any of the 
establishments that were life- or safety-related. Executive Assistant Sowden stated that there was 
need for updated signage, replacing light switch plates, and replacing cracked ceiling tiles at a 
few establishments. The members thanked Executive Assistant Sowden for joining the meeting 
and approved the motion to renew the licenses subject to passing inspections.  
 
 The Board met virtually  on January 26, 2026. The meeting was properly noticed and the

entities to be considered for license renewal. When the Board reached the topic of the Open 
Meeting Law complaint, Assistant Town Administrator Diane Wackell discussed the specifics of 
the complaint as well as advice from town counsel regarding the complaint. Board member

DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Board Violated the Open Meeting Law When it Failed to List the Establishments 
to be Considered for License Renewals on the Notice of the December 15, 2025, 
Meeting; However, the Board Cured the Violation During Its January 26, 2026, 
Meeting 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that, “[e]xcept in an emergency, . . . a public body shall 
post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to the meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays 
and legal holidays.” G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b). Meeting notices must include, among other things, a 
list of the topics that the chair, and any other individual authorized to create the notice, 
reasonably anticipates 48 hours (excluding weekends and legal holidays) in advance of the 
meeting will be discussed at the meeting. See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b); OML 2022-206; OML 2020-
152.4 The topics for discussion must be listed on the notice with “sufficient specificity to 
reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(b). 
We generally consider a topic to include sufficient specificity when a reasonable member of the 
public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s discussion. 
See OML 2019-70. Since 2018, we have consistently required that, to be sufficiently specific, a 
meeting notice must identify the specific applicants or license holders that will be considered 
during the meeting, as well as the specific type of license renewal to be reviewed. See OML 
2026-59; 2020-112; OML 2020-20; OML 2017-33; OML 2016-90. Where a topic requires a 
public body’s approval or other specific decision, such as annual license renewals or 
appointment of candidates to public bodies, the specific applicants or candidates should be listed 
on the notice itself; it is not sufficient to reference a separate list that is available elsewhere. See 
OML 2026-59; OML 2024-236; OML 2022-68; OML 2020-20; OML 2019-22.

The complaint alleges that the notice for the Board’s December 15, 2025, meeting was 
insufficient because it did not contain a list of establishments to be considered for license 
renewals. We find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law where the notice for the 
December 15, 2025, meeting did not include a list of the licenses to be considered for renewal at 
the meeting.    
 
In its response, the Board argues that any violation of the Open Meeting Law regarding 
the not
ice for the December 15, 2025, meeting was cured by the notice and conduct of the 
January 26, 2026, meeting. We agree with the Board. Violations of the Open Meeting Law may 
be cured by independent, deliberative action that is not merely a ceremonial acceptance and 
perfunctory ratification of action taken in violation of the law.  See Pearson v. Bd. of Selectmen 
of Longmeadow, 49 Mass. App. Ct. 119, 125 (2000); OML 2020-7; OML 2016-49. Generally, 
that means conducting deliberations anew at a subsequent meeting that is accessible to the public 
and for which proper notice is provided. See OML 2026-87; OML 2023-15; OML 2021-115 
(finding violation was cured when public body members engaged in discussion about specific 
matters that had been discussed at prior meeting).  
 
The January 26, 2026, meeting was properly noticed and the notice included a list of the 
businesses to be considered for license renewal. Although the January 26, 2026, discussion 
regarding the license renewals was nearly as brief as the discussion at the December 15, 2025, 
meeting, the Board did discuss pending inspectional issues as well as issues that had been 
addressed since the December meeting. The Board also discussed whether it was necessary to 
include the earlier caveats for approval of the renewals regarding satisfactory inspection by the 
police and fire departments since their concerns had been addressed. Finally, the Chair was 
deliberate in making sure that there was time to discuss additional issues if needed. We find that 
during its January 26, 2026, meeting the Board took independent, deliberative action that was not 
merely a ceremonial acceptance and perfunctory ratification of the action taken on December 15, 
2025, and therefore we find that the Board cured the violation.  
 
II. The Board Did Not Violate the Open Meeting Law Where the Date and Time of 
Posting Were Included With the December 15, 2025, Amended Meeting Notice. 
 
The Attorney General’s Open Meeting Law regulations require that, “the date and time that a 
meeting notice is posted shall be conspicuously recorded thereon or therewith. If an amendment 
occurs within 48 hours of a meeting, not including Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays, then 
the date and time that the meeting notice is amended shall also be conspicuously recorded 
thereon or therewith.” 940 CMR 29.03(l)(d).  
 
The complaint alleges that the amended notice for the December 15, 2025, meeting did 
not indicate the date and time the amended meeting notice was posted. The complaint also 
alleged that the notice stated it was “reviewed” by the municipal clerk but not when it was posted 
by the municipal clerk.  
 
In its response, the Board acknowledged that it accidentally selected “Reviewed” on the 
online posting program and that it typically uses the verbiage “Received” or “Revised” in its 
posting language. The Board argues that it did not violate the Open Meeting Law because the

meeting notice was already posted to the town’s website and the website included the date and 
time the notice was posted. We agree with the Board. The website where the meeting notice was 
posted indicated the date and time the original meeting notice was posted and the date and time 
each amended notice was posted. The Open Meeting Law requires the date and time of posting 
to be included on or with the meeting notice. We have advised in the past that the date and time 
of posting may be listed on the meeting notice itself or, when a meeting notice is posted to a 
website pursuant to 940 CMR 29.03(2)(b), on the website next to the meeting notice. See OML 
2019-102; OML 2018-53. Because the Board satisfied these requirements, no violation 
occurred.5 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law with 
respect to the first allegation by failing to list on its notice for the December 15, 2025, meeting 
the establishments to be considered for license renewal. The Board subsequently cured this 
violation. We find that the Board did not commit a violation with respect to the second 
allegation. We commend the Board for the remedial action taken to correct the violation.
