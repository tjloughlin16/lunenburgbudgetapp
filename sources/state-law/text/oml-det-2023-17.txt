This office received a complaint from Michael Donahue on June 8, 2022, alleging that 
the Georgetown School Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 
30A, §§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on or about May 12, 
2022, and you responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Committee, by letter dated June 1, 
2022.  The complaint alleges that the Committee i) convened in executive session on October 7, 
2021, without listing that session as a topic on the notice; ii) failed to discuss whether to 
investigate an incident that occurred during a September 17, 2021, football game; iii) improperly 
met in executive session on April 14, 2022; iv) deliberated outside of a properly posted meeting; 
and v) failed to include sufficient detail on the notice for the meeting held on April 14, 2022. 
 
We appreciate the patience of the parties while we reviewed this matter.  Following our 
review, we decline to review the first two allegations for the reasons discussed below.  We find 
that the Committee did not violate the Open Meeting Law in the other ways alleged.  In reaching 
this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Committee’s response to the 
complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further review.  We also reviewed 
the notices, and open and executive session minutes of the Committee meetings held on October 
7, 2021, and April 14, 2022.  In addition, we reviewed numerous emails and text messages sent 
or received by Committee members which were provided to this office by the complainant.  
Finally, we communicated with you on July 13, 2022, and September 16, 2022; and with the 
complainant on September 9, 2022, September 29, 2022, November 18, 2022, November 22, 
2022, and December 20, 2022.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Committee is a five-member public body; thus, three 
members constitute a quorum. 
 
On October 1, 2021, at 6:44 P.M., the Committee posted notice for a meeting to be held 
on October 7, 2021, at 6:30 P.M.  The notice included ten topics.  On October 5, 2021, at 2:01 
P.M., the Committee posted an amended notice for the October 7, 2021, meeting.  This notice 
included one additional topic – “Executive Session Chapter 30A s21.” 
 
The October 7, 2021, meeting was held as planned and the Committee discussed the 
noticed open session topics.  According to the open session minutes, after discussing the open 
session topics, Chair Barbara Linares
1 “made a motion to adjourn the public meeting at 7:45PM 
to enter into Executive Session.”  The Board then approved a unanimous vote to convene in 
executive session.  During the executive session, the Board discussed with the Superintendent 
her potential retirement.  
 
During an October 21, 2021, meeting, the Committee convened in executive session to 
discuss retaining the law firm Foley Hoag LLP to investigate allegations that racial slurs had 
been used during a September 17, 2021, football game between Georgetown High School and 
Roxbury Prep High School.  At the conclusion of this discussion, the Committee reconvened in 
open session and voted to retain Foley Hoag LLP to conduct the investigation.  Prior to this 
meeting, members of the Committee individually emailed the Superintendent and shared their 
thoughts on how best to investigate the incident. 
 
On or about March 27, 2022, the complainant submitted a letter to the Committee 
expressing concerns about the actions of Ms. Linares during and outside of Committee meetings 
and raised allegations that she violated the Open Meeting Law.  Specifically, the letter raised 
concerns that the Committee had imposed a mask mandate for students but that some members 
did not wear masks themselves during several Committee meetings.  The letter also alleged that 
Ms. Linares violated the Open Meeting Law by convening in executive session for an improper 
purpose and deliberating outside of a properly posted meeting. 
 
On March 28, Ms. Linares sent an email to all members of the Committee stating “I 
wanted to let you know that I spoke with Attorney Pagnini regarding Mr. Donahue’s email he 
sent you.  I wanted you to know that you have no obligation to respond to his request.  It is 
completely up to you as yo [sic] how you proceed!  Have a great night!”

Committee posted a separate notice for a meeting to be held on April 14, 2022, at 6:30 P.M.  
This notice listed 11 topics, including “Acceptance of Warrants: #39V22 & #40P22” and

DISCUSSION 
 
I. We Decli ne to Review the First Allegation as It is Untimely and Decline 
to Review the Second Allegation as It Has Been Previously Addressed. 
 
 The complaint alleges that the Committee convened in executive session on October 7, 
2021, without listing that session as a topic on the notice.  Complaints alleging violations of the 
Open Meeting Law must be filed with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b).  If the alleged violation could not reasonably have been known at the time 
it occurred, then the complaint must be filed within 30 days of the date it should reasonably have 
been discovered.  940 CMR 29.05(3).  When an alleged violation occurs during an open meeting, 
the alleged violation is reasonably discoverable at the time it occurs.  See OML 2014-85; OML 
2012-52.2 
 
On October 5, 2021, the Committee posted notice for an executive session to be held on 
October 7, 2021, thus putting the public on notice that the Committee intended to convene in 
executive session on that date.  Any allegations regarding the sufficiency of the notice with 
respect to the executive session topic were discoverable on October 7, 2021, and therefore any 
complaint regarding these procedures should have been filed within 30 days of October 7, 2021.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b).  Because the complaint was not filed with the Committee until May 12, 
2022, seven months later, we find that the complaint is untimely with respect to this allegation 
and decline to review it.  See OML 2019-92. 
 
While we do not review the merits of this allegation, we remind the Committee that 
execut
ive session topics must be described, both in the meeting notice and in an announcement 
during open session, in as much detail as possible without compromising the purpose for which 
the executive session was called.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b)(3); see also District Attorney for the 
N. Dist. v. Sch. Comm. of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 567 (2009) (“[a] precise statement of the 
reason for convening in executive session is necessary ... because that is the only notification 
given the public that a [public body] would conduct business in private, and the only way the 
public would know if the reason for doing so was proper or improper”).  While a public body is

The complaint also alleges that the Committee failed to discuss whether to investigate an

Committee discussed in executive session, on October 21, 2021, whether to retain a specific law

2022-29.  Nonetheless, because we have already addressed this issue, we decline to further 
review the allegation here.   
 
With respect to the complaint’s concern that additional discussions regarding an 
inves
tigation may have occurred among Committee members outside of the posted executive 
session meeting, we have reviewed numerous emails between the Superintendent and Committee 
members sent shortly after the incident occurred.  Certain of these emails were either sent by the 
Superintendent to a quorum of the Committee or were sent by a Committee member to the 
Superintendent and/or members of the Committee.  The Superintendent is not a member of the 
Committee or otherwise subject to the Open Meeting Law, and thus, any emails sent by her to a 
quorum of the Committee do not constitute improper deliberation.  See OML 2020-53; OML 
2014-80.  Further, even though certain of the emails from Committee members contained his or 
her opinion on the investigation, a matter within the jurisdiction of the Committee, those 
opinions were shared only with the Superintendent or with a subquorum of the Committee and 
therefore did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-132; OML 2017-199; OML 
2017-69; OML 2015-77; OML 2011-52.   
 
II. We Find that the Committee Properly Met in Executive Session on April 14, 
2022. 
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding 
delibe
rations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The law requires that all meetings of a public body be 
conducted in an open session, with some exceptions.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a), 21(a).  A public 
body may enter an executive, or closed, session for any of the ten purposes enumerated in the 
Open Meeting Law provided that it has first convened in an open session, that a majority of 
members of the body have voted to go into executive session, that the vote of each member is 
recorded by roll call and entered into the minutes, and the chair has publicly announced whether 
the open session will reconvene at the conclusion of the executive session.  G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
21(a), (b); see also OML 2014-94.  One permissible purpose for convening in executive session 
is “to discuss the reputation, character, physical condition or mental health, rather than 
professional competence of an individual, or to discuss the discipline or dismissal of, or 
complaints or charges brought against, a public officer, employee, staff member or individual.”  
G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1).

OML 2012-119; OML 2011-6.  Therefore, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law when it discussed the complainant’s letter in executive session.

III. We Find that the Committee Did Not Communicate via Email or Text 
Message in Violation of the Open Meeting Law. 
 
The Open Meeting Law defines a “meeting,” in relevant part, as “a deliberation by a 
public
 body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  The 
law defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any medium, including 
electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public business within its 
jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the distribution of a meeting 
agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other procedural meeting [sic] or the 
distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided than no 
opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” 
is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id.  A public body may not engage in 
serial communication whereby a quorum communicates in a non-contemporaneous manner 
outside of a meeting on a subject within the public body’s jurisdiction.  See OML 2021-100; 
OML 2015-3; OML 2012-84; McCrea v. Flaherty, 71 Mass. App. Ct. 637 (2008) (holding that 
private serial communications violate the spirit of the Open Meeting Law and may not be used to 
circumvent the intent of the law). 
 
The
 complaint alleges that Ms. Linares communicated with a quorum of the Committee 
outside of a properly posted meeting via text messages and email.4  In late March 2022, after 
receiving the complainant’s letter, Ms. Linares separately texted two members of the Committee, 
Cheryl Lachendro and Suzanne MacDonald.  The communications between Ms. Linares and Ms. 
Lachendro were essentially criticisms of the complainant and expressions of sympathy toward 
Ms. Linares.  We have found in previous determination letters that a discussion was outside a 
body's jurisdiction where it concerned purely interpersonal dynamics, see OML 2011-38; a 
completed decision over which the body had no further authority, see OML 2017-88; and

The communications between Ms. Linares and Ms. MacDonald present a closer question.  
Whil
e these communications similarly criticized the complainant, they also discussed the 
contents of his March letter and whether the letter needed to be addressed by the Committee.  As 
such, we find that these communications relate to matters within the Committee’s jurisdiction.  
However, because the communications were only between two members of the Committee, a 
subquorum, and did not otherwise reach a quorum of the Committee, we find that such 
communications do not constitute deliberation and therefore the Committee did not violate the 
Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2018-132; OML 2017-199; OML 2017-69.   
 
We turn next to whether the Committee communicated via email outside of a properly 
poste
d meeting.  We find that only one email, dated March 28, 2022, reached a quorum of the 
Committee.  Although a close call, we find that this email, which was sent by Ms. Linares to the 
Committee and shared her conversation with Attorney Pagnini about responding to the 
complainant’s March letter, was administrative in nature where it simply relayed information 
from Attorney Pagnini that Attorney Pagnini permissibly could have sent to the full Committee 
himself and Ms. Linares did not express an opinion in the email.  See OML 2021-112; OML 
2013-130.  Thus, this email was exempt from the definition of deliberation under the law.  See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 18; OML 2017-85; OML 2017-28; OML 2015-69.  We caution the Committee, 
however, that determining which tasks are merely administrative, and therefore appropriate for 
email, can be challenging, and that email communication between a quorum of public body 
members - however innocent - creates at least the appearance of an Open Meeting Law violation.  
As such, we caution public bodies on the use of electronic communications.  See OML 2017-88; 
OML 2014-80. 
 
The remaining emails, which were exchanged between March 30, 2022, and April 11, 
2022, wer
e sent by Ms. Linares to either the Superintendent, the Superintendent’s Administrative 
Assistant, or Attorney Pagnini.  Individual public officials are not public bodies and therefore are 
not subject to the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2021-3.  Therefore, any emails sent by Ms. 
Linares to individual public officials do not constitute improper deliberation.  Any emails sent by 
the Superintendent, who is not a member of the Committee or otherwise subject to the Open 
Meeting Law, to a quorum of the Committee also do not constitute improper deliberation.  See 
OML 2020-53; OML 2014-80.  As such, we find that the Committee did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law with respect to these communications.  See OML 2020-71.

IV. The Committee Posted a Suf
 ficiently Specific Notice for its Meeting Held 
on April 14, 2022. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that, except in an emergency, “a public body shall post 
notic
e of every meeting at least 48 hours prior to such meeting, excluding Saturdays, Sundays 
and legal holidays.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The notice must be printed in a legible, easily 
understandable format and must contain the date, time and place of the meeting, as well as a 
listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be discussed.  Id.  The list of topics 
shall have “sufficient specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at 
the meeting.”  940 CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific 
when a reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated 
nature of the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35.  When reviewing a meeting notice 
for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting notice 
standing alone.  See OML 2015-109; OML 2014-155.  Rather, we review what was actually 
discussed at the meeting to determine if the notice was sufficiently detailed to make the public 
aware of the discussion topics.  See OML 2016-149. 
 
The complaint alleges that the Committee failed to include sufficient specificity with 
res
pect to the acceptance of two warrants listed on the notice for the April 14, 2022, meeting.  
Here, the item, “Acceptance of Warrants: #39V22 & #40P22,” was listed on the notice under 
“Consent Agenda.”  We have previously stated that consent agenda topics, which do not require 
the public body’s discussion, but instead are listed as perfunctory administrative tasks, do not 
need to include details such as the specific bill to be approved or the names of specific donors.  
See OML 2019-161; OML 2018-22.  Here, the Committee did not discuss the warrants but 
simply moved to approve, which motion carried unanimously.  Therefore, we find that this 
consent agenda topic did not violate the Open Meeting Law.  See 2022-206.  However, whenever 
possible, we encourage the Committee to include a further description to identify the specific 
warrants to be approved.
