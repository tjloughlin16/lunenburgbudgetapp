FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Board is a five member public body; thus, three 
members constitute a quorum. 
 
On December 6, 2019, the Board posted notice of a meeting to be held on Monday,

meeting . . .”  During the December 9, 2019, meeting, the Board reviewed and discussed the

DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Board Intentionally Violated the Open Meeting Law By D istributing a 
Composite Evaluation of the Town Administrator to All Members Prior to 
a Meeting. 
 
The Open Meeting Law seeks to balance the public’s interest in witnessing the 
deliberations of public officials with the government’s need to manage its operations efficiently. 
To that end, the law defines deliberation as “an oral or written communication through any 
medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public 
business within its jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the 
distribution of a meeting agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other procedural 
meeting or the distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, provided 
that no opinion of a member is expressed.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 18.  All deliberations must occur 
during a
 meeting.  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting Law, a “quorum” is a simple 
majority of the members of a public body.  Id.    
 
The first complaint alleges that the Board deliberated outside of a meeting when the 
Tow
n Administrator’s composite evaluation was emailed to all members prior to both the 
December 9, 2019, and March 29, 2021, meetings.  Performance evaluations and discussions of 
an employee’s professional competence must occur in open session.  G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a)(1); 
District Attorney for the Northern District v. School Committee of Wayland, 455 Mass. 561, 569 
(2009).  While members of a public body may individually create evaluations, and then submit 
them to an individual to aggregate into a master evaluation document to be discussed at an open 
meeting, once the individual evaluations are submitted for aggregation there should be no 
deliberation among members of the public body regarding the content of the evaluations outside 
of an open meeting, whether in person or over email.  See OML 2021-122.
3   
 
The very practice utilized by the Board here, where public body members submitted 
performance evaluations to the chair, who then aggregated the individual evaluations into a 
single document, and then distributed that composite evaluation in advance of the noticed 
meeting, was found to be a violation of the Open Meeting Law by the Supreme Judicial Court in 
Boelter v. Board of Selectmen of Wayland, 479 Mass. 233 (2018).  In holding that this practice 
violated the Open Meeting Law, the Supreme Judicial Court stated that “the effect of the 
circulation of the individual and composite evaluations was that all five board members were 
aware of the opinions of four of the members in advance of the open meeting; thus, the 
circulation, in effect, constituted a deliberation, or a meeting, to which the public did not have

access.”  Boelter, 479 Mass. at 243.  As such, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting 
Law when a composite evaluation was sent to all Board members via email prior to the 
December 9, 2019, and March 29, 2021, meetings. 
 
We must now determine whether this violation was, as
  the complainant urges, an 
intentional one.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a 
public body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 
29.02.  An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate 
ignorance of the law’s requirement or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates 
the Open Meeting Law.  Id.  On April 5, 2018, the Supreme Judicial Court issued a decision 
finding that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by circulating individual and composite 
evaluations, which expressed the opinions of the board members, to a quorum in advance of the 
public meeting.  See Boelter v. Board of Selectmen of Wayland, 479 Mass. 233, 243 (2018).  
Because the Board had previously been advised that this conduct violated the Open Meeting 
Law, we find that this violation was intentional.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 18; 940 CMR 29.02; OML 
2013-63.  However, we credit the Board’s explanation that they “misinterpreted the restrictions 
of Boelter and believed that if the contents of the composite were not attributed to any members 
and the document was distributed by staff rather than the members, this would comply with the 
Law.”  Therefore, we decline to recommend the imposition of a civil penalty in this instance.  
See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c); OML 2018-108; OML 2015-122.  Nonetheless, we now specifically 
instruct the Board that once individual evaluations are compiled, the aggregated evaluation may 
not be circulated to a quorum of members prior to a meeting, such as by email.

II. The Board Failed to Timely Respond to a Request for Executive Session 
Meeting Minutes and Failed to Conduct a Review of those Minutes at its 
Next Meeting. 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minute
s of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  A public body 
must respond to a request for executive session minutes within 10 days.  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  
If the public body or a designee has determined, prior to the request, that certain executive 
session minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester directly, notifying him or her 
of the availability of those minutes.  See OML 2013-105; OML 2013-99.  And if, at the time of a 
request, the public body has not conducted a review of the minutes to determine whether 
continued nondisclosure is warranted, the body must perform such a review and release the 
minutes, if appropriate, no later than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  In such circumstances, the body must still respond to the requestor 
within 10 days, notifying him or her that it is conducting this review.  See OML 2013-99.

The second complaint alleges that the Board failed to timely respond to a request for 
execut
ive session meeting minutes.  On February 3, 2022, the complainant requested from the 
Board executive session minutes that involved discussions of the Galbi litigation matter.  We 
acknowledge that the Board initially responded to the complainant’s request within 10 days.  
However, Board Member Fay’s February 4, 2022, response was ambiguous, only suggesting that 
he would check on the status of the minutes.  The complainant received no further response from 
the Board until March 22, 2022, when he was advised that the minutes had been reviewed at the 
Board’s March 21, 2022, meeting, approved for release, and posted to the town’s website.  A 
public body must respond to a requestor within 10 days and, at the very least, notify him or her 
that it is conducting a review to determine whether minutes can be released.  See OML 2022-
123; OML 2015-86; OML 2013-199; OML 2013-99.  We encourage public bodies to also 
include specific information about when the minutes will be reviewed.  See OML 2015-130.  
Here, even though the Board’s next meeting following the February 3, 2022, request was on 
February 7, 2022, it did not conduct a review of any of the requested executive session minutes 
at that meeting.  Rather, the Board reviewed all six sets of executive session minutes at its 
meeting on March 21, 2022, which was the ninth meeting after receiving the request.  The Open 
Meeting Law specifically requires the Board to perform a review of the requested executive 
session minutes no later than its next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first.  See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2).  We find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law by not properly 
responding to a request for executive session meeting minutes.  See OML 2016-135; OML 2015-
130.  We order no further remedial action as the Board has since reviewed and released the 
requested executive session minutes.  
 
We take this opportunity to remind the Board that public bodies have an obligation to 
revi
ew the minutes of executive sessions at reasonable intervals to determine if continued non-
disclosure of minutes is warranted, even in the absence of a specific request, and to announce 
that determination at the next meeting following its review.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(1); OML 
2019-35; OML 2015-94; OML 2013-56.  Although the Open Meeting Law does not define 
“reasonable intervals,” we have found that quarterly reviews or reviews every six months satisfy 
the law.  See OML 2017-104; OML 2015-166; OML 2013-180.  
 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board intentionally violated the Open 
Meeting Law by deliberating outside of a properly posted meeting when it shared via email the 
composite evaluations of the Town Administrator.  We find that the Board violated the Open 
Meeting Law by failing to properly respond to a request for executive session minutes.  We order 
immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that similar future 
violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  Although we decline to 
recommend the imposition of a civil penalty in this instance for the intentional violation, we 
caution the Board that a future finding of an intentional violation of the Open Meeting Law may 
result in a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per intentional violation.
