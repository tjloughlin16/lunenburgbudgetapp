DISCUSSION 
 
I. The Board violated the Open Meeting Law when members of the public were unable 
to access the remote meeting held August 27, 2025. 
 
At the core of the Open Meeting Law is the requirement that meetings of public bodies be 
open and accessible to the public.  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except when meeting in executive 
session, “all meetings of a public body shall be open to the public.”).  Generally, access to a 
meeting must include the opportunity to be physically present as well as to see and hear what is 
being discussed by the members of the public body.  See OML 2026-38; OML 2020-100; OML 
2016-54.7   To satisfy the Open Meeting Law’s requirement that meetings be open and accessible 
to the public, a meeting notice must inform the public of the “place of the meeting,” whether 
remote or in person.  See G.L. c. 30A § 20(b); OML 2021-28.  See also OML 2021-73 (finding 
that public body “failed to provide real-time access to the meeting ... as the notice failed to 
include sufficient information regarding the location of the remote meeting”); OML 2020-102. 
 
A public body is permitted to meet entirely remotely, so long as it provides adequate, 
alternative means of public access to its deliberations.  “Adequate, alternative means” may

include, without limitation, providing public access through telephone, internet, or satellite 
enabled audio or video conferencing or any other technology that enables the public to clearly 
follow the proceedings of the public body in real time.  G.L. c. 20, § 20(b); Chapter 2 of the Acts 
of 2025; OML 2026-87; OML 2025-179; OML 2021-135.  When all public body members 
participate in a meeting remotely or when a public body meets in person without providing the 
public physical access to the meeting location, the public body must then provide the public with 
adequate, alternative access to the meeting, which requires access to the proceedings in real time. 
See OML 2026-87; OML 2020-111; OML 2020-100; Chapter 20, § 20(b). 
 
The August 27, 2025, meeting proceeded for over twenty minutes during which time 
some members of the Board and of the public were unable to log into the meeting.  We therefore 
find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2026-87 (finding violation of 
Open Meeting Law when at least one member of public was removed from remote meeting and 
was blocked from rejoining meeting); OML 2024-95 (finding violation of Open Meeting Law 
when meeting notice included incorrect link, even though error was accidental and public body 
was unaware that public could not access meeting); OML 2024-63 (finding that public body did 
not provide adequate, alternative means of access where real-time remote access was not 
available to all who wanted to attend meeting).  By deliberating during a purportedly open 
meeting that was not accessible to the public, the Board violated the Open Meeting Law. 
 
Violations of the Open Meeting Law may be cured by independent, deliberative action 
that is not merely a ceremonial acceptance and perfunctory ratification of action taken in 
violation of the law.  See Pearson v. Bd. of Selectmen of Longmeadow, 49 Mass. App. Ct. 119, 
125 (2000); OML 2020-7; OML 2016-49.  Generally, that means conducting deliberations anew 
at a subsequent meeting that is accessible to the public and for which proper notice is provided.  
See OML 2026-87; OML 2023-15; OML 2021-115 (finding violation was cured when public 
body members engaged in discussion about specific matters that had been discussed at prior 
meeting).  
 
Once it learned of the access problem, the Board suspended deliberations and attempted 
to resolve the problem.  Unsuccessful in its attempts, the Board ended the meeting and scheduled 
a new meeting for September 3, 2026.  Although the September 3, 2026, meeting was intended 
to cure the violation of the August 27, 2026, meeting, deliberations were not conducted anew on 
all topics of the August 27, 2026, meeting.  For example, the Board did not discuss the briefing 
that was referenced during the August 27, 2026, meeting.  Therefore, we find that the Board did 
not cure its violation of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2026-87 (finding that violation was 
not cured because public body did not engage in independent deliberation at subsequent 
meeting); OML 2023-115 (finding “that the Board did not cure the December 7 violation where, 
on January 18, 2023, it simply voted to ratify its earlier votes”); OML 2023-71 (finding that 
violation was not cured by subsequent meeting where Commission “merely ratified the June 2 
vote.”)

posted notice listed the location of the meeting as Conference Room A at One Ashburton Place,

the location of the meeting.  Although the public likely could have benefitted from additional 
signage, the meeting was not so difficult to find as to be inaccessible in violation of the Open 
Meeting Law.  We find no violation as to this allegation.   
 
III. The Board interfered with the recording of the December 10, 2025, meeting but not of 
the other meetings referenced in the complaints.  
 
The complainant alleges that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law on several 
occasions when he sought to record Board meetings.  The Open Meeting Law states that “[a]fter 
notifying the chair of the public body, any person may make a video or audio recording of an 
open session of a meeting of a public body, or may transmit the meeting through any medium, 
subject to reasonable requirements of the chair as to the number, placement and operation of 
equipment used so as not to interfere with the conduct of the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(f).  
See, e.g., OML 2019-67 (finding Chair’s order to stop recording because meeting was already 
being live-streamed did not constitute reasonable restriction on right to record meeting); OML 
2018-57 (finding requirement that equipment focus on Chair only did not constitute reasonable 
restriction on recording). 
 
September 3, 2025, meeting 
 
Board Member Calaj’s AI recorder attempted to enter the meeting to record it, although 
Member Calaj was not in attendance.  We have not previously addressed whether section 22(f), 
providing for the recording of meetings subject to the reasonable requirements of the chair, 
allows recording a meeting by use of AI programs.  However, we have stated that “[t]he law 
does not place any restrictions on the type of permissible medium for recording or under what 
circumstances a recording can be broadcast.”  See OML 2018-4.  Although the brief exchange 
immediately prior to the commencement of the September 3 meeting is not entirely clear as to 
the rationale for declining to admit Member Calaj’s AI recorder, the Board has represented in 
conjunction with these Open Meeting Law complaints that its policy is to allow AI programs to 
record a meeting so long as the Chair is notified in conformance with the Open Meeting Law by 
the individual who controls the AI recorder.  We find this to be a reasonable policy.  Member 
Calaj did not notify the chair of his intent to record the September 3, 2025, meeting; therefore, 
the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it declined to admit Member Calaj’s AI

recorder into the meeting.  There is conflicting information in the record as to whether the 
complainant’s AI program was in the waiting room at the September 3, 2025, meeting.8  
However, where the complainant did, in fact, record the meeting, we find no violation as to this 
allegation.  
 
December 10, 2025, meeting 
 
Two days prior to the December 10, 2025, meeting, the complainant emailed the Board 
Chair, stating that he may “opt” to record the upcoming meeting.  At the beginning of the 
December 10, 2025, meeting, the Board Chair announced that after notifying the Chair, any 
person may record the meeting, subject to reasonable requirements.  He added: “If anyone 
intends to record this meeting, please indicate so now.”  No one spoke up.  The complainant 
arrived at the December 10, 2025, meeting after the chair’s announcement, and began to record 
the meeting.  A staff member observed the recording in progress and alerted the Board.  The 
Chair asked the complainant to delete that portion of the recording that was made prior to the 
staff member’s discovery that the meeting was being recorded.   
 
The complainant notified the Board by email that he might record the December 10, 
2025, meeting.  We credit the Board’s statement that it did not know whether the person who 
sent the email was in attendance at the meeting.  Nonetheless, the email satisfied the Open 
Meeting Law’s requirement that a person wishing to record a meeting notify the Chair.  See 
OML 2012-24.  The Chair’s command to delete the recording was, in practical terms, an 
unreasonable restriction on (or prohibition of) recording the beginning of the meeting.  We find, 
therefore, that the request to delete the recording violated the Open Meeting Law.     
 
January 14, 2026, meeting 
 
On January 12, 2026, the complainant again emailed the Board, stating that he may opt to 
record the January 14, 2026, meeting.  The complainant attended the January 14, 2026, meeting.  
As he did at the December 10, 2025, meeting, the complainant began recording the January 14, 
2026, meeting without stating at the meeting that he planned to do so.  However, the Chair did 
not prohibit him from doing so or otherwise interfere with the recording.  The Board did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law on January 14, 2026, when the Chair asked at the beginning of the 
meeting whether anyone planned to record it.  Indeed, we encourage chairs to make reasonable 
efforts to determine whether anyone is recording a meeting, such as by asking at the start of the 
meeting whether anyone intends to do so.  See OML 2013-136. 
 
At 2:00 p.m., the scheduled start time of the January 14, 2026, meeting, MassTech’s 
General Counsel sent an email to the complainant regarding recording the meeting.  She asked 
the complainant to “confirm with the chair when a recording is being commenced, including the 
method of recording.  You should thereafter comply with any instructions on how the recording 
may be conducted, which may be limited to video or audio recording.”  This email overstates the 
restrictions that a chair may impose on recording.  The Open Meeting Law allows only

“reasonable requirements of the chair as to the number, placement and operation of equipment

However, to the extent that the complainant suggests that this email chilled his right to 
record the meeting and therefore constituted a violation of the Open Meeting Law, we do not 
find a violation.  First, the email was sent at exactly the scheduled start time of the meeting; if 
the complainant did not read the email prior to the meeting (and it is unclear if he did), it could 
not have had a chilling effect.  Second, the email was not sent by a member of the Board and 
should not be attributed to it.  Third, and most importantly, the Chair did not restrict or prohibit 
the recording of the January 14, 2026, meeting.  For these reasons, the January 14, 2026, email 
did not infringe on the complainant’s right to record the meeting. 
 
February 11, 2026, meeting 
 
In the March 7 Complaint, the complainant alleges that the Board imposed a “start of 
meeting confirmation” requirement to record the February 11, 2026, meeting.  An individual 
wishing to record a meeting does not forfeit their right to do so by failing to provide notice of 
their intent to record prior to the commencement of the meeting.  See OML 2014-8 (explaining 
that “[t]he failure to provide advance notice . . . may not be used to prohibit a person from 
recording or transmitting a meeting”).  However, the Chair’s announcement at the beginning of 
the February 11, 2026, meeting did not impose an impermissible restriction on recording.  At the 
beginning of the meeting, the Chair asked the complainant whether he was recording the 
meeting.  When the Chair did not receive a response from the complainant, he stated that the 
Board would assume that the complainant was recording the meeting, in light of the email sent 
two days prior to the meeting, stating his intent to record.  The Board’s action relative to 
recording the February 11, 2026, meeting did not violate the Open Meeting Law.   
 
The March 7 Complaint also alleges that the Board’s reference to G.L. c. 272, § 99, the 
Massachusetts Wiretap Statute, in its response to the January 7 Complaint and the Second 
January 14 Complaint,
9 and at the February 11 meeting, is itself a violation of the Open Meeting 
Law, as it intimidates those who wish to record a meeting.  Our office is tasked with enforcement 
of the Open Meeting Law.  We do not review allegations of violations of other statutes.  See, 
e.g., OML 2013-136 (declining to review allegation of violation of G.L. c. 272, § 99).  We offer 
no opinion on the interplay between the Open Meeting Law and the Massachusetts Wiretap 
Statute.  Therefore, we decline to review this allegation.  Nonetheless, we caution the Board 
against making statements that might deter the public from recording a meeting.  See OML 
2012-90 (finding no violation where complainant was allowed to record meeting but expressing 
concern about and cautioning against making remarks that complainant could not use members’ 
images, as such remarks might discourage exercise of right to record meeting).  We also 
encourage the complainant to work with the Board when seeking to record meetings.  Based 
upon our investigation, it appears that the Board consistently attempted to discern whether 
members of the public intended to record a meeting.  The complainant may not have been

IV. Notices of the meetings of February 11, 2026, March 11, 2026, and April 15, 2026, 
were insufficiently specific; notices of the meetings of August 27, 2025, September 3, 
2025, December 10, 2025, and January 14, 2026, were not. 
 
Notice of a meeting must include “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates 
will be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The list of topics must have sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 
29.03(l)(b).   We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member 
of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s 
discussion.  See OML 2023-3; OML 2019-134; OML 2015- 35.  The Open Meeting Law 
requires that the notice describe topics with sufficient specificity so that the public can make an 
informed decision whether to attend the meeting in order to observe the discussion regarding a 
topic of interest.  See OML 2023-86; OML 2019-95; OML 2018-22.  Generic notice topics do 
not satisfy the requirement of specificity.  See OML 2022-179.  When reviewing a meeting 
notice for sufficiency, unless it is clearly insufficient on its face, we do not review a meeting 
notice standing alone.  Rather, we review what was discussed at the meeting to determine if the 
notice was sufficiently specific.  See OML 2025-158; OML 2024-155; OML 2023-61.  Where a 
public body does not deliberate on a particular topic, it cannot violate the Open Meeting Law 
with respect to the sufficiency of the meeting notice as it pertains to that topic. See OML 2019-
94; OML 2016-76.

V. The “chair procedures” referenced by the Chair during meetings were not documents 
“used at the meeting” and therefore were not required to be listed in the minutes.  
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that a public body “create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  Meeting minutes 
must set “forth the date, time and place, the members present or absent, a summary of the 
discussions on each subject, a list of documents and other exhibits used at the meeting, the 
decisions made and the actions taken at each meeting, including the record of all votes.”  Id.  We 
have said that a document is “used,” and must be listed in the minutes, when the document is 1) 
physically present, 2) verbally identified, and 3) its contents discussed by the public body during 
a meeting.  See OML 2026-108; OML 2025-6; OML 2024-217. 
 
During multiple meetings, the Chair followed a document called the “chair procedures” 
to assist him in running the meeting.  MassTech staff created the chair procedures to serve as a 
script for the Chair to follow while conducting the meeting.  The content of the chair procedures 
is meeting-specific and includes, for example, the exact wording of anticipated motions.  
===PAGE 18===
18 
 
Typically, the chair procedures document also indicates that the Chair will “call for a roll call 
vote to approve the motion, which will be displayed on the screen and does not need to be read.”  
The chair procedures document also includes key provisions of the Open Meeting Law.   
 
At the beginning of at least one meeting, the Chair referenced the chair procedures; he 
sought to access it as a reference during the meeting.  Regardless of whether the chair procedures 
were physically present or verbally identified, however, we find that they were not discussed 
during the meeting.  We have not been made aware of any discussion of the chair procedures 
during a meeting.  Rather, it was the topics referenced in the chair procedures that were 
discussed during the meeting.  In that regard, the Chair’s use of the chair procedures is similar to 
a chair’s use of the meeting notice and a copy of the Open Meeting Law, to help guide them 
through the meeting.  The Open Meeting Law would not require that minutes list the notice of a 
meeting on the list of documents used at a meeting if a public body chair used the notice as an 
outline of the discussions to be had.  See OML 2024-75 (noting that mere reference to 
information from within a document does not render the document “used” such that it needed to 
be listed in meeting minutes); OML 2019-28 (finding that although actions referenced in letter 
may have prompted committee to discuss implementation of safety plan, actual content of letter 
was not discussed and therefore letter was not “used” during meeting such that it should have 
been listed in minutes); OML 2012-59 (finding that fact sheet that was physically present and 
verbally identified but was not discussed need not be listed in minutes); OML 2012-42 (“We 
decline to adopt the complainant’s argument that all background material used by members of a 
public body to prepare for a public meeting should be considered ‘used’ at the meeting, 
regardless of whether it is discussed, voted upon, or even brought to the meeting.”).  Because the 
chair procedures were not discussed during a meeting, the chair procedure documents were not 
“used” and therefore were not required to be listed in the meeting minutes. 
 
To the extent that the complainant alleges that he should have been provided with the 
chair procedures in response to a request, we decline to review that allegation.  The Open 
Meeting Law governs requests for minutes only, not requests for any other documents.  See G.L. 
c. 30A, § 22.  Documents used by a public body during an open meeting must be provided in 
response to a public records request but there is no requirement in the Open Meeting Law that 
the public body affirmatively provide copies of those documents to persons in attendance at a 
meeting.  See OML 2016-94; OML 2015-199.  Finally, although we decline to review this 
allegation, we note our concern that the Board regularly discusses and votes on lengthy motions 
that are displayed on a screen but are not read aloud.  We caution the Board to conduct meetings 
in such a way that members of the public are able to follow the proceedings and know what 
topics are the subject of discussions and votes. 
 
VI. Minutes of the December 10, 2025, meeting are insufficiently detailed for failure to 
summarize the exchange about recording the meeting.  
 
===PAGE 19===
19 
 
The complainant alleges that the minutes of the December 10, 2025, meeting are 
insufficiently detailed because they do not include a summary of the Chair’s demand that the 
complainant delete the recording that he was making of the meeting.  We agree.14   
 
As noted above, the Open Meeting Law requires that minutes include a “summary of the 
discussions on each subject.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a).  When reviewing minutes for compliance 
with the Open Meeting Law, we look for substantial compliance with the accuracy requirement. 
See OML 2026-27; OML 2023-56; OML 2016-105.  By substantial compliance, we mean that 
the minutes should contain enough detail and accuracy so that a member of the public who did 
not attend the meeting could read the minutes and have a clear understanding of what occurred. 
See OML 2026-27; OML 2023-204; OML 2022-214; OML 2021-140.  A public body is not 
required to capture every remark, comment or opinion made during a meeting to substantially 
comply with the accuracy requirement of the Open Meeting Law.  See OML 2025-58; 2023-204; 
OML 2021-4; OML 2019-145.  For example, minutes were not required to include members’ 
“disjointed remarks as they attempted to establish an audio connection” between the members 
attending in person and a member participating remotely.  See OML 2026-95. 
 
 The December 10, 2025, meeting was already underway when the complainant entered 
the conference room and began recording.  Staff noticed that the complainant was recording the 
meeting without announcing upon his arrival that he planned to do so.  General counsel and, by 
extension, the Chair were notified of the recording in progress. An exchange ensued during 
which the Chair asked the complainant to delete the recording; the complainant, in turn, 
maintained that he was not required to do so.  The minutes of the December 10, 2025, meeting 
do not include any summary of this exchange.  Although minutes are not required to capture 
every remark, they cannot omit a discussion in its entirety.  We therefore find that the minutes of 
the December 10, 2025, are not sufficiently detailed.  See OML 2025-87 (finding that minutes 
that did not include any summary of public comments were not in compliance with the Open 
Meeting Law).   
 
Citing OML 2023-145, the Board argues that the minutes need not capture matters of a 
“parliamentary” nature, such as the exchange regarding recording the meeting.   In the 
determination cited by the Board, we found that the Open Meeting Law “does not require that 
meeting minutes specifically document the motioning of a vote.”  The discussion at issue here, 
however, is distinguishable from the motion that was omitted from minutes that were the subject 
of OML 2023-145.  Here, there was a discussion, albeit brief, of the Chair’s authority to order 
deletion of a recording.  That this discussion was not on a subject particular to the Board’s 
jurisdiction does not remove it from the minutes.  See, e.g., OML 2026-95 (noting that the Open 
Meeting Law’s requirement that votes be recorded in the minutes does not except votes of a 
housekeeping nature, for example, a vote to take topics out of order).  By failing to include this 
exchange in the minutes, the Board violated the Open Meeting Law.

themselves accurately summarize what was said at the February 11, 2026, meeting.  The

those discussions.  For example, the complaint maintains that a determination previously issued 
by our office was inaccurately summarized by general counsel during the meeting.  The minutes 
of a meeting must accurately summarize that was actually said at the meeting.  See OML 2025-
187; OML 2023-76; OML 2022-189.  The Open Meeting Law does not dictate the content of a 
discussion at a meeting; rather, it requires that meeting minutes accurately summarize what was 
discussed.  See OML 2023-226; OML 2016-155.  Therefore, we find no violation as to these 
remaining allegations.   
 
VIII. The Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it did not respond to the First 
January 14 Complaint in a timely manner. 
 
Within 14 business days of receiving an Open Meeting Law complaint, the chair must 
distribute the complaint to all members of the public body, the public body must meet to review 
the complaint, and the public body must then respond to the complainant in writing and provide 
our office a copy of the complaint and the response.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 
29.05(5).  The Board responded to the First January 14 Complaint on March 11, 2026, well 
beyond the 14 business days allowed by the Law.  The Board “does not disagree” that its 
response was late, explaining that the Chair did not realize that two complaints were filed on 
January 14, 2026.  Because the Board did not file a timely response to the First January 14 
Complaint, we find that it violated the Open Meeting Law.

IX. The Board’s violations were not intentional. 
 
The complainant alleges that multiple violations that are the subject of these complaints 
were intentional.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 23(c) (p roviding for the Attorney General to determine 
whether a violation was intentional).  An intentional violation is an “act or omission by a public 
body or a member thereof, in knowing violation of [the Open Meeting Law].”  940 CMR 29.02.  
An intentional violation may be found where the public body acted with deliberate ignorance of 
the law’s requirements or has previously been advised that certain conduct violates the Open 
Meeting Law.  Id.   
 
The complainant alleges, among other things, that the Board committed intentional 
violations when it listed notice topics with insufficient specificity, when it interfered with his 
right to record meetings, when it approved minutes that were insufficiently detailed, and when it 
failed to respond to an Open Meeting Law complaint within 14 business days, as required by 
statute. 
 
On November 7, 2025, we issued a determination finding that the Board violated the 
Open Meeting Law when it listed a notice topic that included an acronym, NEMC, that was not 
widely understood by the public.  See OML 2025-184.  The complainant alleges that through 
that determination, the Board was advised that the use of acronyms not widely understood by the 
public violates the Open Meeting Law.  Therefore, according to the complainant, the violation of 
insufficiently specific notice, found here, was intentional.   
 
Because we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law when it used the 
acronym CHIPS, we do not find an intentional violation.  Nor do we find an intentional violation 
as to inclusion of notice topics such as “Report from the Mass Tech Collaborative Board Chair.”  
The Board was not previously advised that such a topic was insufficiently specific.  Although a 
similar allegation was made in the complaint that was the subject of OML 2025-184, we declined 
to review that allegation because a quorum was not present during the discussion of that topic 
and, as we explained, “[w]hen a quorum is not present, there is no deliberation requiring 
compliance with the Open Meeting Law.”  Id.  The Board was not previously found in violation 
of the same provisions of the Open Meeting Law as we find here and we find no intentional 
violation of the Open Meeting Law. 
 
CONCLUS
ION 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when its August 27, 2025, meeting was inaccessible.  We also find that the Board violated the 
Open Meeting Law when it interfered with the complainant’s right to record the Board’s meeting 
on December 10, 2025.  We find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it 
discussed topics at meetings held on February 11, 2026, March 11, 2026, and April 15, 2026, 
that were not listed with sufficient specificity in the meeting notices.  We find that minutes of the 
Board’s December 10, 2025, meeting were insufficiently detailed and that minutes of the 
Board’s February 11, 2026, meeting were insufficient as they failed to list all documents used 
during the meeting.  Finally, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law when it 
failed to respond timely to the First January 14 Complaint.
