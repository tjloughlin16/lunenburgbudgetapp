This office received a complaint from George Harris on April 28, 2023, alleging that the 
Wayland Finance Committee (the “Committee”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Committee on or about March 14, 2023, 
and Co-Chair Pamela Roman responded to the complaint, on behalf of the Committee, by letter 
dated March 29, 2023.  The complaint alleges that the Committee posted a notice with 
insufficient specificity with respect to one open session topic for a meeting held on March 6, 
2023, and failed to announce the name of a member who participated remotely during the March 
6, 2023, meeting. 
 
We resolve this matte
 r by informal action, in accordance with 940 CMR 29.07(2)(a), 
after reviewing the original complaint, the Committee’s response to the complaint, the complaint 
filed with our office requesting further review, and the notice and minutes of the Committee 
meeting held on March 6, 2023, as well as a video recording of that meeting.1  We find that the 
Committee violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to announce the name of the member who 
participated remotely during the meeting held on March 6, 2023.  We find that the Committee 
did not violate the Open Meeting Law in the other way alleged.

The Committee posted notice of a meeting to be held on March 6, 2023, at 7:00 P.M.  
The notice listed nine topics.  One of the topics was “Discuss and Vote Transfer from the 
Reserve Fund for Legal Settlement.”   
 
The March 6, 2023, meeting was held as planned and one member participated remotely.  
The 
Committee admits, and we agree, that the Chair did not announce the name of the one 
member who was participating remotely during the March 6, 2023, meeting.  As such, we find 
that the Committee violated the Open Meeting Law.  See 940 CMR 29.10(7)(b) (at start of 
meeting, “the chair shall announce the name of any member who will be participating remotely” 
and such information must be recorded in the meeting minutes).  We note that the minutes 
identify the member who was participating remotely.   
  
The complaint also alleges that a topic listed on the notice was insufficient to advise the 
public
 of the nature of the Committee’s discussion.  The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to 
eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding deliberations and decisions on which public policy is 
based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  A public 
body must post notice of every meeting at least 48 hours in advance, and include the date, time 
and place of the meeting, as well as a “a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will 
be discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The list of topics shall have sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.  940 CMR 
29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to be sufficiently specific when a reasonable member 
of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of the public body’s 
discussion.  See OML 2015-35.
2 
 
Here, the complaint alleges that the topic “Discuss and Vote Transfer from the Reserve 
Fund for Legal Settlement” was insufficient where it did not identify the exact legal settlement 
that the Committee planned to discuss during the meeting held on March 6, 2023.  Towards the 
end of the meeting, the Committee discussed the need to replenish the legal budget with money 
from the Town’s reserve fund and identified approximately $178,000 that had been expended 
from the fund for a legal settlement with the former police chief.
3  The Committee approved by 
majority vote a transfer “from the Reserve Fund to the Legal Budget, in the amount of $178,588, 
to replenish the Legal Budget for settlement payment made to former employee.”  A public body 
need not list each and every detail about a topic in order to comply with the Open Meeting Law’s 
requirements.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-134.  Although listing additional 
detail may have been helpful to the public, we are satisfied that the topic as listed was sufficient 
to reasonably advise the public of the issue to be discussed at the meeting.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 
20(b); see also OML 2020-125; OML 2013-152.  As such, we find that Committee did not 
violate the Open Meeting Law with respect to the sufficiency of the notice of the March 6, 2023, 
meeting.

 For the reasons stated above, we find that the Com mittee violated the Open Meeting Law 
by not announcing the name of the member who participated remotely during the meeting held 
on March 6, 2023.  We order the Committee’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s 
requirements, and we caution that similar future violations could be considered evidence of 
intent to violate the law.   
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with our office or the 
Committee.  Please feel free to contact the Division at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
