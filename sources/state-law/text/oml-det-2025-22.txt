Dear Ms. Stefield: 
 
This office received a complaint from Cindy King on December 7, 2024, alleging that the 
Townsend Affordable Housing Trust (the “Trust”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, 
§§ 18-25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Trust on October 24, 2024, and the Trust 
responded to the complaint by letter on October 28, 2024, following an extension of time granted 
by this office.  The complaint alleges that the Trust posted a notice that was insufficiently 
specific, failed to conduct roll call votes, and did not discuss an Open Meeting Law complaint 
during a meeting held on October 7, 2024.   
 
Following our review, we find that the Trust violated the Open Meeting Law by failing to 
conduct roll call votes during the October 7, 2024, meeting in which members participated 
remotely.  We find that the Trust did not violate the Open Meeting Law in the other ways 
alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Trust’s response 
to the complaint, and the complaint filed with our office requesting further review.  In addition, 
we reviewed the notice, minutes and video recording of the Trust meeting held on October 7, 
2024.

FACTS 
 
We find the facts as follows.  The Trust duly posted notice of a meeting to be held on 
October 7, 2024.  The notice listed the physical location of the meeting as Select Board Room, 
Townsend Town Hall.  In addition, the notice stated that individuals could “Join Zoom Meeting” 
and included a link to the Zoom meeting, several telephone numbers the public could use to dial 
into the meeting, and the Webinar ID number and passcode required to log into the meeting.  The 
notice also listed one main topic: “Review OML Complaint brought against TAHT by Cindy 
King regarding September 4
th, 2024 meeting.” 
 
The October 7, 2024, meeting was held as planned and all Trust members participated 
remotely.  The Trust’s Housing and Grant Administrator was present in the physical meeting 
location in Town Hall and the complainant attended the meeting in person.  During the meeting, 
which lasted approximately five minutes, the Trust discussed a previous Open Meeting Law 
complaint filed by the complainant, which complaint alleged that a quorum of the Trust 
deliberated after the conclusion of a September 12, 2024, meeting.  Vice Chair David Werlin 
confirmed that all members had received the complaint and then suggested a proposed response 
to the complaint.  Trust member Beverly Napior made a motion to accept Mr. Werlin’s proposed 
response, which was seconded by Trust member Cindy Boundy, and the Trust unanimously 
voted to provide the proposed response to the Attorney General’s Office.  Mr. Werlin then asked 
for a motion to adjourn which motion was made by Ms. Boundy and seconded by Ms. Napior.  
The Trust did not vote on this motion; rather, Mr. Werlin simply announced that the meeting was 
adjourned after the motion had been made and seconded. 
 
DISCUSSION 
 
The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of public bodies be open and accessible to 
the publ
ic.  See G.L. c. 30A, § 20(a) (except when meeting in executive session, “all meetings of 
a public body shall be open to the public.”).  Generally, access to a meeting must include the 
opportunity to be physically present as well as to see and hear what is being discussed by the 
members of the public body.  See OML 2020-100; OML 2018-75; OML 2016-54.
2  Section 20 
of Chapter 20 of the Acts of 2021, An Act Relative to Extending Certain COVID19 Measures 
Adopted During the State of Emergency, temporarily suspended the requirement under the Open 
Meeting Law that meetings be held at a physical location that is accessible to the public as long 
as a public body instead provides “adequate, alternative means” of access to meetings through 
technology that enables the public to clearly follow the proceedings of the public body while 
those activities are occurring.
3  Section 20 allows some or all members of a public body to 
participate in a meeting remotely but does not require a public body to provide remote access to 
its meetings if those meetings are instead held in-person at a location that is open and physically 
accessible to the public.  See OML 2023-52; OML 2021-147. 
 
 
2 Open Meeting Law determinations and declination letters may be found at the Attorney General’s website, 
https://www.mass.gov/the-open-meeting-law. 
3 These provisions have been extended several times since their inception, most recently on March 29, 2023, when 
Governor Maura Healey signed into law a supplemental budget bill which further extended the expiration of the 
temporary provisions to March 31, 2025.

The Open Meeting Law further requires that public bodies must post notice of each 
meeti
ng “at least 48 hours prior to such meeting” and that every notice include “the date, time 
and place of such meeting and a listing of topics that the chair reasonably anticipates will be 
discussed at the meeting.”  G.L. c. 30A, § 20(b).  The list of topics shall have “sufficient 
specificity to reasonably advise the public of the issues to be discussed at the meeting.”  940 
CMR 29.03(l)(b).  We generally consider a topic to include sufficient specificity when a 
reasonable member of the public could read the topic and understand the anticipated nature of 
the public body’s discussion.  See OML 2015-35; OML 2012-71; OML 2011-44.   
 
The complaint alleges that the notice of the October 7, 2024, meeting was insufficiently 
spe
cific where it misidentified the Open Meeting Law complaint as pertaining to a Trust meeting 
held on September 4, 2024, rather than a meeting held on September 12, 2024.  While we 
acknowledge that the Open Meeting Law complaint discussed by the Trust pertained to a 
September 12, 2024, meeting, a public body need not list each and every detail about a topic in 
order to comply with the Law’s requirements.  See OML 2022-98; OML 2020-132; OML 2019-
134.  We have previously stated that when a public body will discuss an Open Meeting Law 
complaint, the notice should include some identifying information about the complaint, such as 
the name of the complainant.  See OML 2022-220; OML 2013-82.  Here, the notice specified 
that the Trust would discuss an Open Meeting Law complaint and included the complainant’s 
name.  As such, we are satisfied that the topic as listed on the notice here was sufficient to 
reasonably advise the public of the issue to be discussed at the meeting, that is, an Open Meeting 
Law complaint filed by the complainant.  See OML 2024-229; OML 2023-114; OML 2013-152.   
 
To the extent the complaint alleges that the notice was also insufficiently specific where 
it di
d not state that all members of the Trust would be participating remotely, we do not find a 
violation.  Although public bodies are permitted to meet entirely remotely, the body must 
provide adequate, alternative means of public access to the body’s deliberations, and the notice 
must provide enough detail to reasonably inform the public of the location of the meeting.  See 
OML 2022-173.  Here, the notice included both a physical meeting location and a method of 
alternative remote access to the meeting (and listed information necessary to access the meeting 
such as website link and webinar ID), and the meeting was accessible in-person and through the 
web-based Zoom platform.  The notice did not need to separately note that some members of the 
Trust would be participating remotely.  See OML Declination 4-12-24 (Dedham School 
Committee).  
 
However, because Trust members participated remotely during the October 7, 2024, 
mee
ting, all votes taken during that meeting were required to be by roll call vote, even 
unanimous votes.  See 940 CMR 29.10(7)(c); OML 2016-132; OML 2015-145.  A roll call vote 
is “the act or an instance of calling off a list of names.”  Webster’s Third New International 
Dictionary of the English Language, 683 (unabridged ed. 1961).  We find that the Trust violated 
the Open Meeting Law by failing to conduct a roll call vote when it voted to approve the 
response to the Open Meeting Law complaint.  Further, we remind the Trust that at the start of 
the meeting, “the chair shall announce the name of any member who will be participating 
remotely” and such information must be recorded in the meeting minutes.  See 940 CMR 
29.10(7)(b).

We do not, however, find a violation when the Trust adjourned the October 7, 2024, 
mee
ting without voting by roll call.  Although a motion was made and seconded to adjourn the 
October 7, 2024, meeting, the Trust did not actually vote on that motion.  Rather, the Vice Chair 
simply ended the meeting after the motion was made and seconded.  The Open Meeting Law 
does not require a vote to adjourn the meeting of a public body.  Although a vote provides a 
practical way to collectively end a meeting, it is not a requirement of the Open Meeting Law.  
G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25; see also OML 2019-7.  Therefore, the Trust did not violate the Open 
Meeting Law when it exited the meeting without any vote.  See OML 2024-9. 
 
Finally, with respect to the allegation in the complaint that the Trust did not discuss the 
subst
ance of the Open Meeting Law complaint during the October 7, 2024, meeting, we do not 
find a violation.  The Open Meeting Law requires that within 14 days of receipt of a complaint, 
the public body review the complaint's allegations; take remedial action, if appropriate; and send 
to the Attorney General a copy of the complaint and a description of any remedial action taken.  
G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); 940 CMR 29.05(5).  Upon the filing of an Open Meeting Law complaint 
with a public body, the chair “shall disseminate copies of the complaint to the members of the 
public body,” and “the public body shall review the complaint’s allegations.”  940 CMR 
29.05(3), (5).  Thus, a public body must meet to review the complaint and formulate a response, 
or meet to delegate that authority, and respond to the complaint within 14 business days.  See 
G.L. c. 30A, § 23(b); OML 2017-69; OML 2012-90.  During the meeting, Trust members 
acknowledged receiving the complaint and the Vice Chair then discussed a proposed response to 
the complaint, which was unanimously approved.  The Open Meeting Law does not require a 
public body to engage in a substantive discussion of the merits of the complaint.  See OML 
2024-13.  Indeed, once a public body has reviewed a complaint during a meeting, its decision to 
simply refer the complaint to an individual for response, rather than discuss its substance, is the 
public body’s prerogative.  See OML 2019-40; OML 2017-96. 
 
CONCLUSION 
  
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Trust violated the Open Meeting Law by 
failing to conduct roll call votes during the meeting held on October 7, 2024.  We order the 
Trust’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution that 
similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.   
 
We now consider the complaint addressed by this determination to be resolved.  This 
determination does not address any other complaints that may be pending with the Trust or with 
our office.  Please feel free to contact our office at (617) 963-2540 if you have any questions 
regarding this letter.
