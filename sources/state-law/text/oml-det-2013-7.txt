Dear Attorney Ross: 
This office received a complaint from Attorney Robert Bertsche of Prince Lobel LLP, on 
behalf of the Attleboro Sun Chronicle, dated August 4, 2011, alleging that the Mansfield Board 
of Selectmen (the "Board") violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-25. 
Specifically, the complaint alleges that the Board has refused to publicly release executive 
session minutes from the Board's March 11, 2009 and March 25, 2009 meetings, even though 
the purpose for the executive sessions has since expired. The complaint was filed with the Board 
on June 10, 2011. The Board responded to the complaint by letter dated June 24, 2011. 
Following our review, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting Law 
because, even where the executive session purpose has expired, a public body may still redact or 
withhold executive session minutes on the basis of attorney-client privilege. In reaching this 
determination, we reviewed the June 10, 2011 complaint; the Board's June 24, 2011 response; 
and the August 4, 2011 complaint filed with our office. Additionally, we reviewed 
correspondence between the complainant and the Town regarding the executive session minutes. 
FACTS 
The facts are not in dispute. The Mansfield Board of Selectmen met in executive session 
on March 11, 2009 and March 25, 2009 to discuss "contract negotiations" and "union 
negotiations," respectively. At the time of the meetings, the old Municipal Open Meeting Law, 
G.L. c. 39, §§ 23A-C, was still in effect. On July 1, 2010, the current version of the Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§18-25, replaced that statute. 
During the week of March 14, 2011, Patrick Anderson, a staff writer for the Attleboro 
Sun Chronicle, verbally requested from the Board copies of the executive session minutes of the

two March 2009 meetings. Mr. Anderson followed up with a request in writing on April 26, 
2011. Attorney Robert Bertsche, of Prince Lobel LLP, separately sent the Board a letter on the 
same day requesting the minutes and providing a legal analysis to support their public release. 
The Board denied the request in a letter dated May 12, 2011. The Board stated that, "upon our 
review of the Minutes, it appears that nearly all the information in the minutes, and certainly all 
the Minutes related to litigation, would need to be redacted to prevent the Town from waiving its 
attorney-client privilege." The Board did not state whether it determined that the purpose for 
which the Board had entered executive session had expired. 
On October 13, 2012, the Board reviewed the minutes of the March 11, 2009 and March 
25, 2009 executive sessions, and determined that executive session purposes no longer warrant 
non-disclosure. The Board continues to assert that the minutes are protected from public 
disclosure by the attorney-client privilege. 
DISCUSSION 
The question presented is whether a public body may withhold from the public executive 
session minutes from meetings that occurred prior to July 1, 2010, under the old Municipal Open 
Meeting Law, G.L. c. 39, § 23B, citing the attorney-client privilege. 
The current version of the Open Meeting Law took effect on July 1, 2010. The current 
Open Meeting Law states that the "minutes of any executive session . . . may be withheld from 
disclosure to the public in their entirety under subclause (a) of clause Twenty-sixth of section 7 
of chapter 4, as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but 
no longer." G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f). Public bodies have discretion to enter executive session and to 
determine when the purpose for which the public body has entered executive session has 
concluded. See G.L. c. 30A, § 21(a); Foudy v Amherst-Pelham Regional School Committee, 
402 Mass. 179, 182 (1988). However, the public body also bears the burden of justifying 
continued nondisclosure of executive session minutes. Foudy, 402 Mass. at 184. When the 
purpose for which a valid executive session was held has been served, the Open Meeting Law 
requires that "the minutes, preparatory materials and documents and exhibits of the session shall 
be disclosed unless the attorney-client privilege or 1 or more of the exemptions under said clause 
Twenty-sixth of said section 7 of said chapter 4 apply to withhold these records, or any portion 
thereof, from disclosure." Id. This latter clause was not included in the prior version of the law, 
which stated only that the "records of each meeting shall become a public record and be 
available to the public; provided, however, that the records of any executive session may remain 
secret as long as publication may defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, but no 
longer." G.L. c. 39, § 23B (repealed). 
The complainant made his written request for the executive session minutes on April 26, 
2011.. Because we are reviewing whether the Board complied with the law in responding to a 
request for minutes made after July 1, 2010, rather than examining whether the Board followed 
the law at the time a particular meeting took place, we apply the current version of the statute. 
Under the current Open Meeting Law, the Board may cite the attorney-client privilege as a valid 
reason for withholding executive session minutes from the public, even where the executive 
session purpose may have expired. See G.L. c. 30A, § 22(f).

We make no finding as to whether or not the Board justifiably asserted the attorney-client 
privilege in this instance. The Open Meeting Law authorizes the Attorney General to investigate 
a complaint alleging a violation of the law, and to request documents in the course of that 
investigation. However, the Attorney General may not require a public body to disclose "any 
documentary material which would be privileged." G.L. c. 30A, § 24(e). 1  
CONCLUSION 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board did not violate the Open Meeting 
Law. 
We appreciate the patience and cooperation of the parties during this investigation, and 
now consider this matter closed.
