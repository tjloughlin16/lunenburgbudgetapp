RE:  Open Meeting Law Complaint 
 
Dear Ms. Makepeace-O’Neil: 
 
This office received a complaint from Thomas Siefert on October 3, 2025, alleging that 
the Shutesbury Select Board (the “Board”) violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 18-
25.  The complaint was originally filed with the Board on or about August 6, 2025, and the 
Board responded to the complaint by letter dated August 12, 2025.  The complaint alleges that a 
quorum of the Board deliberated via email outside of a properly posted meeting.  
 
 Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law in the way 
alleged.  In reaching this determination, we reviewed the original complaint, the Board’s 
response to the complaint, the complaint filed with our office requesting further review, and the 
minutes and video recording of the Board meeting held on August 12, 2025.
1   
 
The Open Meeting Law was enacted “to eliminate much of the secrecy surrounding the 
deliberations and decisions on which public policy is based.”  Ghiglione v. School Committee of 
Southbridge, 376 Mass. 70, 72 (1978).  The Open Meeting Law requires that meetings of a 
public body be properly noticed and open to members of the public, unless an executive session 
is convened.  See G.L. c. 30A, §§ 20(a)-(b), 21.  A “meeting” is defined, in relevant part, as “a 
deliberation by a public body with respect to any matter within the body’s jurisdiction.”  G.L. c. 
30A, § 18.  The law defines “deliberation” as “an oral or written communication through any

medium, including electronic mail, between or among a quorum of a public body on any public 
business within its jurisdiction; provided, however, that ‘deliberation’ shall not include the 
distribution of a meeting agenda, scheduling information or distribution of other procedural 
meeting [sic] or the distribution of reports or documents that may be discussed at a meeting, 
provided than no opinion of a member is expressed.”  Id.  For the purposes of the Open Meeting 
Law, a “quorum” is a simple majority of the members of a public body.  Id.   
 
The Board is a three-member public body; thus, two members constitute a quorum.  On 
June 5, 2025, the complainant emailed the Shutesbury Town Administrator and the Board 
expressing his distress about certain actions taken against him by a member of the Shutesbury 
Lake Wyola Advisory Committee.  Approximately three hours later, the Town Administrator 
sent an email to all three Board members asking, “Do we want to send a response?” to the 
complainant.  Later that day, Board member Eric Stocker responded to the Town Administrator 
and copied the other two members of the Board.  In his response, Mr. Stocker expressed his 
concerns about appointing the complainant to the Lake Wyola Advisory Committee.  Two 
minutes later, Mr. Stocker sent another email to the Town Administrator and two Board 
members stating, “It looks like I replied to all in error.” 
 
The Board acknowledges, and we find, that the email sent by Mr. Stocker contained 
improper deliberation because the email reached a quorum of the Board and included his opinion 
on a matter within the Board’s jurisdiction.  See OML 2022-108; OML 2018-118; 2015-3; OML 
2014-108; OML 2013-136; Boelter v. Board of Selectmen of Wayland, 479 Mass. 233, 243 
(2018).2  A one-way communication from one public body member to a quorum on matters 
within the body’s jurisdiction is a deliberation, even if no other public body member responds.  
See OML 2020-136; OML 2016-104; OML 2015-33; OML 2012-73.  Moreover, the Open 
Meeting Law does not carve out an exception to the definition of “deliberation” for discussions 
that do not result in a decision or vote.  We find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
by deliberating among a quorum via email.  While we commend the Board for acknowledging 
the violation during the meeting held on August 12, 2025, the Board must take the additional 
step of publicly releasing the June 5, 2025, emails sent by Mr. Stocker.  See OML 2013-69 
(finding that a public body cured the violation of deliberation outside of a meeting by disclosing 
in detail the substance of the improper deliberation); OML 2011-35 (finding that release of the 
deliberative email, along with other steps taken, constituted proper remedial action). 
 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law 
when a quorum of members deliberated via email outside of a properly posted meeting.  We 
order the Board’s immediate and future compliance with the law’s requirements, and we caution 
that similar future violations could be considered evidence of intent to violate the law.  In 
addition, we order the Board to disclose, within thirty (30) days of the date of this letter, the 
June 5, 2025, emails.
