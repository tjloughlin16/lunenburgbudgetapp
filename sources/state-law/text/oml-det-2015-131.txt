Dear Chair Genzabella: 
This office received a complaint from Angela Stetson, dated February 9, 2015, alleging 
that the Hinsdale Select Board (the "Board") violated the Open Meeting Law, G.L. c. 30A, §§ 
18-25. The complaint was originally filed with the Board on January 7, 2015, and the Board 
responded by letter dated January 26, 2015. The complainant alleges that the Board: (i) failed to 
respond to a request for executive session minutes; and (ii) did not conduct a roll call vote during 
an executive session. 
We appreciate the patience and cooperation of the parties while we reviewed this matter. 
Following our review, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law as alleged in the 
complaint. In reaching a determination, we reviewed the original complaint; the Board's 
response; the request for further review filed with our office; and the attached documents. 
FACTS 
We find the facts as follows. On December 2, 2014, the complainant sent an e-mail to 
the Board requesting minutes of two executive sessions. The e-mail did not specify the precise 
dates of the meetings, instead stating, "I would like to request copies of the two executive session 
meetings held between the Hinsdale Select Board and Herb Ferry. The meetings would have 
occurred around September 2013." On December 10, 2014, the complainant again e-mailed the 
Board in order to provide more information about the dates of the meetings mentioned in her 
prior e-mail. Her December 10, 2014 e-mail states, in part, "One meeting was held on 
September 18, 2013 and I believe the other was held approximately a week later." That same 
day, the Board's interim administrative assistant e-mailed the complainant, stating that her 
request for the executive session minutes was "on hold for now" and that she was trying to get it 
included for discussion at a meeting to take place the following week.

DISCUSSION 
I. The Board Failed to Respond to the Complainant's Request for Executive Session 
Minutes as Required bv Law. 
The Open Meeting Law states that a public body "shall create and maintain accurate 
minutes of all meetings, including executive sessions." G.L. c. 30A, § 22(a). A public body 
must respond to a request for executive session minutes within 10 days. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). 
If the public body has determined, prior to the request, that the requested executive session 
minutes may be released, it should respond to the requester directly, notifying him or her 
whether those minutes are available. See OML 2013-105; OML 2013-99. If the body previously 
determined that executive session minutes should remain confidential because publication would 
defeat the lawful purposes of the executive session, it should respond by stating the reason why 
the minutes continue to be withheld. Id. And if, at the time of a request, the public body has not 
conducted a review of the minutes to determine whether continued nondisclosure is warranted, 
the body must perform such a review and release the minutes, if appropriate, no later than its 
next meeting or within 30 days, whichever occurs first. G.L. c. 30A, § 22(g)(2). In such 
circumstances, the body should still respond to the requestor within 10 days, notifying him or her 
that it is conducting this review. See OML 2013-99. 
Here, the complainant requested the executive session minutes at issue on December 2, 
2014. The Board met on December 17, 2014 to discuss her request, and it voted on that date not 
to release the minutes. The complainant was not informed of this decision until December 24, 
2014. The Board's formal response to her request was mailed to Ms. Stetson on January 14, 
2015 (though the letter was dated January 5, 2015). While the Board conducted the requisite 
review of its executive session minutes within 30 days of the request, to be in compliance with 
the law's requirements, the Board's decision about whether continued non-disclosure of those 
minutes was wan-anted should have been communicated to Ms. Stetson immediately after the 
December 17, 2014 meeting, or, if that was not feasible given the time of day, on the next

II. The Board Failed to Record a Roll Call Vote Before Entering Executive Session. 
The Open Meeting Law permits a public body to enter executive session provided that it 
has first convened in open session, that a majority of the members of the body have voted to go 
into executive session, and that the vote of each member is recorded by roll call and entered into 
the minutes. G.L. c. 30A, § 21(b); OML 2014-94. A roll call vote is, "the act or an instance of 
calling off a list of names." Webster's Third New International Dictionary of the English 
Language, 683 (unabridged ed. 1961). These requirements are intended to create a clear record 
of what occurred and to reflect the significance of holding a discussion behind closed doors. See 
OML 2014-94; OML 2012-10. 
Here, the minutes of the December 17, 2014 meeting indicate that the Board failed to 
record a roll call vote to enter executive session. The minutes show "3 ayes." This does not 
satisfy the Open Meeting Law's requirement that public bodies record a roll call vote before 
convening in executive session. Even unanimous votes need to be recorded by roll call. See 
OML 2013-195. The Board's failure to do so here violated the Open Meeting Law. 
For the reasons stated above, we find that the Board violated the Open Meeting Law. We 
order the Board to amend the minutes of the December 17, 2014 meeting to reflect a roll call 
vote. We also order the Board's immediate and future compliance with the Open Meeting Law, 
and we caution that similar future violations may be considered evidence of intent to violate the 
law.
